# Preface — The Obvious, Eventually

**First draft v0.1 — editorial development**

Somewhere between 1595 and 1603, John Thorpe drew a line through a house.

The drawing was for Beaufort House in Chelsea. To a modern eye there is nothing particularly strange about the line. It passes through the centre of the plan and gives access to rooms on either side. We would call it a corridor. The Sir John Soane's Museum describes its use here as innovative; nearly four centuries later, Robin Evans would cautiously identify Beaufort House as the earliest English example he had found of a corridor used to remove traffic from rooms.[1]

That last phrase is worth lingering over.

To remove traffic from rooms.

A corridor seems too ordinary to have an intellectual history. It is now so thoroughly absorbed into the idea of a building that its absence feels like the thing requiring explanation. Yet a house does not logically require one. Rooms can simply lead into other rooms. For long periods they did. Movement could pass through the same spaces in which sleeping, eating, receiving, working and private life took place. The inconvenience was not necessarily invisible; it was simply contained within an arrangement that appeared normal.

Thorpe's line did something conceptually larger than its width on the plan. It gave movement a place of its own.

Once that relationship exists, the older arrangement becomes strangely difficult to look at without wanting to redraw it. This is one of the peculiar characteristics of a successful rearrangement: afterwards, it acquires the air of inevitability. The corridor begins to seem less like an invention than like a fact of nature.

It is not.

We inherit not only solutions, but definitions of the problem.

* * *

Three and a half centuries later, two brothers in California closed a successful restaurant.

Dick and Mac McDonald already had customers. Their drive-in was not failing for want of demand. They could have improved it in the ordinary ways: hire better carhops, adjust the kitchen, add equipment, expand the menu, reorganise shifts. Instead, in 1948, they temporarily shut the business and reconsidered the choreography of the entire operation.

One part of that reconsideration took place on a tennis court. The brothers drew a kitchen at full scale in chalk and had workers move through the imagined stations. They watched where bodies crossed, where effort was wasted, where one task interfered with another, then erased and redrew the arrangement. A rainstorm reportedly washed the exercise away; they drew it again.[2]

The eventual Speedee Service System was not one invention. It was a changed relationship between many familiar things: menu, customer, worker, grill, counter, packaging, sequence, distance. Its importance here has nothing to do with hamburgers. It lies in the distinction between improving an inherited process and questioning whether the process has been divided up correctly in the first place.

There is a profound difference between asking how to make the waiter walk faster and asking whether the waiter belongs in the system at all.

Mature practices are extremely good at producing local intelligence. A difficulty appears; a trade, profession or business learns to manage it. Tools improve. Techniques improve. Standards accumulate. Specialists become very good at solving the problems presented to them.

But the shape of the problem can survive all of that improvement untouched.

A wall is chased more neatly. A removable panel is added to reach the thing behind it. A better sealant is selected for the crack-prone joint. A more forgiving filler is specified. A stronger adhesive is found. Each answer may be competent. Each may even be excellent. Yet there is another class of question that sits one level above them:

Why is the service in the wall?

Why is the component behind a surface that must be destroyed to reach it?

Why are two assemblies known to move differently being asked to appear monolithic?

Why is the finish responsible for concealing the absence of a joint?

These are not always better questions. Sometimes the conventional arrangement survives them easily. But they are different questions, and established systems do not always generate them spontaneously.

* * *

In 1956 an ageing tanker called the *Ideal-X* left Newark carrying fifty-eight aluminium truck bodies on its deck.

Metal boxes for freight were not new. Nor were ships, trucks, cranes or railways. The consequential idea was the system that allowed the same load unit to move between them without being unpacked and handled piece by piece.

Even that idea was not enough.

Early containerisation produced a familiar problem: everybody made their own answer. Shipping lines chose different container dimensions and different corner fittings. A box that worked elegantly within one operator's system could become useless at the boundary with another. The object had improved while the system remained fragmented.

The eventual transformation depended on standardisation. Common dimensions, common handling assumptions and common corner interfaces allowed independently owned ships, cranes, ports, trains and lorries to participate in the same physical language. ISO's freight-container work, begun through a technical committee created in 1961, helped formalise that language internationally; its first freight-container standard appeared in 1968.[3]

Malcom McLean is rightly central to the story, but the myth of the lone inventor is less useful than the actual sequence. McLean came from trucking rather than the maritime establishment and was treated as an outsider by parts of the shipping industry. His system mattered. So did the engineers, ports, manufacturers, unions, shipping companies, military requirements and standards committees that converted competing private systems into interoperable infrastructure. The breakthrough survived because it eventually became larger than its originator.

The box mattered.

The corner mattered more.

This distinction is easy to underestimate. Standardisation is often imagined as the enemy of variety: a flattening exercise in which difference is sacrificed to efficiency. At the right layer it can produce the opposite. A standard interface can allow enormous variety on either side precisely because neither side has to be redesigned every time the other changes.

The container does not care which crane lifts it. The crane does not need to know what is inside.

That is a powerful kind of freedom.

It suggests that some of the most durable inventions are not objects at all. They are agreements about where objects meet.

* * *

Architecture has done this before.

Joseph Paxton did not arrive at the Crystal Palace by beginning with monumental architecture. His experience had been formed in gardens and glasshouses. As head gardener at Chatsworth he spent years working with the practical problems of growing exotic plants under glass: light, ventilation, condensation, drainage, structure, repeatable panes, ironwork and rapid assembly. His conservatories were not sketches for an exhibition hall in any conventional sense. They were another building culture.

In 1850, with the Great Exhibition approaching and the available schemes proving unsatisfactory, Paxton transferred that culture into a problem of an entirely different scale.

The result was not simply a large greenhouse. The Crystal Palace was a system of standardised, prefabricated iron, timber and glass components assembled with extraordinary speed. The V&A records that the building was completed roughly seven months after construction began. After the exhibition closed, the structure was dismantled; its prefabricated components were then used in the construction of the larger Crystal Palace at Sydenham.[4]

There are many reasons not to turn this into a parable about prefabrication. The Palace had a particular programme, life, climate problem and historical setting. A house is not an exhibition hall. A temporary iron-and-glass structure is not evidence that every building should become a kit of parts.

The more interesting lesson is narrower.

Paxton carried knowledge across a professional boundary. He did not arrive empty-handed. He arrived with another set of habits.

There is a difference.

The useful outsider is not valuable because ignorance is pure. Paxton's authority came from years of practical experiment. He understood a type of structure so well that he could recognise its principles in a problem that belonged, ostensibly, to somebody else.

And the result matters for another reason. It was not merely efficient. It became architecture.

Technical rationality is not an alibi for ugliness. Repetition is not necessarily monotony. Manufacture is not necessarily cheapness. Disassembly is not necessarily provisionality. An intelligent construction system can produce a building with presence, delight and an identity far larger than the logic by which it was assembled.

This matters because the house explored in these pages is not intended to look like an engineering demonstration.

There will be no aesthetic virtue awarded for visible services merely because they are accessible. An access panel is not an architectural language. A movement gap is not beautiful merely because it is technically honest. A room does not become better because its floor can be opened.

The technical condition still has to become architecture.

* * *

I am not an architect.

That fact requires neither apology nor romanticisation.

Architecture is full of knowledge that is invisible from outside it. Details that initially appear irrational can embody lessons purchased through fire, water, litigation, movement, labour, tolerance, weather, cost and failure. Regulations that frustrate a clean diagram may exist because somebody has already experienced the consequence of the simpler answer. A builder's apparently conservative preference may represent hundreds of repetitions of a detail that works. A plastered wall may be common because, when properly made in the right place, it is beautiful, robust, comprehensible and extraordinarily difficult to improve upon.

The outsider who fails to understand this can mistake accumulated intelligence for inertia.

That is one danger.

There is another.

Familiarity can make a choice stop looking like a choice.

Every settled discipline accumulates both wisdom and sediment. Practices acquire dependencies. Contracts acquire conventions. Trades inherit scopes. Manufacturers make products for the details people already draw; people draw the details because those are the products manufacturers make. Regulation, procurement, training and expectation settle around the arrangement. Eventually the arrangement becomes difficult to distinguish from the underlying physical problem.

Distance can occasionally be useful here.

The outsider's privilege is not expertise. It is permission to remain surprised.

To find it strange, for example, that a component expected to be replaced several times may be buried behind a surface intended to last much longer.

To find it strange that routine electrical change can require cutting into masonry.

To find it strange that predictable differential movement is so often resolved by asking a brittle finish not to notice.

To find it strange that access is sometimes treated as complete when the person can see the component but cannot withdraw it.

To find it strange that the repair of one failed part can require several unrelated trades simply to reconstruct the path taken to reach it.

Surprise proves nothing. It merely opens the question.

The responsibility then reverses. If a proposed alternative cannot survive the accumulated knowledge of the profession, it should die.

That is the method of this book.

Convention deserves respect, not immunity.

Novelty deserves scrutiny, not admiration.

Those two sentences belong together.

The fact that a detail is common is evidence that it can be built. It may also be evidence that labour understands it, suppliers support it, building control recognises it, repairers can diagnose it and generations of failure have already removed its most dangerous forms. These are serious advantages.

But common use is not, by itself, proof that the arrangement is inevitable.

Likewise, a new detail earns nothing by being new. If a removable floor is noisy, it has failed. If a demountable wall feels hollow and temporary, it has failed. If an ingenious service interface requires a vanished proprietary clip twenty years from now, it has failed. If a custom structural connection introduces ambiguity where an ordinary certified hanger would have worked, the ordinary hanger should win.

A proposal should be compared not with a caricature of conventional construction, but with its best version.

The plaster wall should be excellent.

The ordinary floor should be excellent.

The standard connector should be the right standard connector.

Then the alternative can be asked to justify itself.

Research before invention. Calculation where calculation decides. Full-scale mock-up where touch, sound and movement decide. Disassemble the supposedly disassemblable thing. Let it rattle. Let it crack. Try to replace it. Count the tools. Ask what boundary was broken on the way in and whether it can really be made whole again.

And keep the right to abandon it.

A doctrine that can only survive by protecting its favourite details from failure is not a doctrine. It is a collection of attachments.

* * *

The concerns behind this enquiry are not new.

Architects, engineers, conservationists, facilities managers and building researchers have spent decades thinking about service life, adaptability, support and infill, changing layers, maintainability, loose fit, standardisation and design for disassembly. The chapters that follow owe a great deal to that work. No claim is made here to have discovered that buildings change, that services fail, or that maintenance matters.

The dissatisfaction is more particular.

It begins with the ordinary house.

A house can be expected to stand for generations, yet it contains things that will live for years or decades. These timescales meet everywhere. Pipes meet walls. Windows meet masonry. wiring meets rooms. fans meet ducts. tiles meet waterproofing. floors meet structure. controls meet power. joinery meets plaster. Every junction is a small agreement between things that may have different lives, different movements, different manufacturers and different reasons for being there.

Too often those agreements are made permanent by default.

Wet work closes the gap.

Adhesive joins the layers.

Filler erases the line.

A chase makes room.

A sealant becomes the final negotiator between materials.

The building is complete.

Then time begins.

I do not accept that maintenance must ordinarily begin with demolition.

I do not accept that a short-lived system acquires a right to consume long-lived fabric merely because burying it is convenient during construction.

I do not accept that predictable movement becomes an unforeseeable defect simply because the junction was drawn as a line.

I do not accept that access has been designed when a hatch exists but a human body, tool or replacement component cannot perform the work.

I do not accept that technical necessity exempts anything visible from the obligations of architecture.

None of this means that services must all be exposed.

It does not mean that plaster is obsolete, that screws are bad, that every wall should be a panel, that every floor should lift, or that every component deserves to be replaceable. There is no virtue in converting a durable, quiet, inexpensive construction into a complicated machine for the pleasure of being able to dismantle it.

Nor does it mean that permanence itself is the enemy.

I want permanence.

I want walls with mass and rooms with settled proportions. I want stairs that feel as though they belong to the house rather than to a lease cycle. I want brick to weather as brick, timber to wear as timber, brass to acquire the evidence of hands. I want an architecture confident enough not to be reorganised whenever a technology changes.

The question is how to earn that permanence.

Perhaps the long-lived parts of a building can remain more stable if shorter-lived things are prevented from continually consuming them.

Perhaps movement can be given joints rather than cracks.

Perhaps maintenance can be given a geography rather than a trail of damage.

Perhaps a permanent wall can receive a small number of deliberate attachment points so that generations of changing objects do not each drill their own claim into it.

Perhaps a room surface can sometimes be understood as a manufactured architectural element rather than a wet operation irreversibly fused to everything behind it.

Perhaps the visible joint, properly placed, can be better than the false promise of seamlessness.

Perhaps the mouldings, skirtings, architraves, thresholds and cornices of traditional architecture can recover practical work as well as decorative meaning.

Perhaps a fine line of brass can indicate not applied luxury but a genuine condition: this edge moves; this panel opens; this threshold wears; this information matters.

And perhaps, after all that, the room can simply look like a room.

That last requirement is important.

The ambition is not to live inside the diagram.

* * *

There is an ideological position beneath the technical one, although I hope it is a modest ideology.

Things that exist deserve to be understood before they are rejected.

Things that are old deserve more than the assumption that they are obsolete.

Things that are new deserve less than the assumption that they are progress.

A traditional detail may contain a sophistication that only becomes visible when somebody tries to replace it. A new system may solve the problem beautifully. Either result is acceptable.

What is not acceptable is the refusal to ask because the arrangement has become familiar.

This is where I part company with inevitability.

I am interested in the moment when something ordinary becomes strange again: when the corridor returns to being a line somebody chose to draw; when the kitchen becomes bodies moving through space rather than a list of appliances; when the container becomes not a box but an agreement at its corners; when a gardener looks at the problem of a monumental building and recognises the logic of a glasshouse.

That moment does not abolish expertise.

It creates work for it.

The propositions in this book therefore move deliberately from argument to pattern to drawing to prototype. Some will survive. Some should not. Where the conventional answer proves better, the conventional answer should remain.

The objective is not a novel house.

It is a house in which novelty has been concentrated only where the inherited arrangement does not withstand examination.

The rest can be ancient.

This book is an attempt to give change its own architecture.

The ambition is not a house that changes continually. It is almost the opposite: a house able to remain itself because the things that must change have been given somewhere else to go.

## Notes

[1] John Thorpe, plan of Beaufort House, Chelsea, c.1595–1603, Sir John Soane's Museum, SM Volume 101/63–64. The Museum describes the central corridor as innovative. Robin Evans discusses Beaufort House in “Figures, Doors and Passages”, first published in *Architectural Design* 48, no.4 (1978), and later collected in *Translations from Drawing to Building and Other Essays* (Architectural Association / MIT Press, 1997).

[2] Smithsonian Magazine, “The Story of How McDonald's First Got Its Start”, on Dick and Mac McDonald's 1948 redesign and full-scale chalk rehearsal of the Speedee Service System; see also McDonald's corporate history of the system.

[3] Smithsonian Magazine, “The Now-Ubiquitous Shipping Container Was an Idea Before Its Time”; International Organization for Standardization, ISO/TC 104, *Freight containers*. ISO records the committee's creation in 1961 and the publication of its first freight-container standard in 1968.

[4] Victoria and Albert Museum, “The foundation of the V&A's Collections: the Great Exhibition of 1851”; Historic England's Crystal Palace records; Chatsworth, “Joseph and Sarah Paxton: an Introduction”. The V&A traces Paxton's exhibition design to his earlier glasshouse work and records the extensive use of standardised prefabricated parts; Historic England records the dismantling and reuse of components at Sydenham.
