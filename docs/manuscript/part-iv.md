# Part IV — The Reference House: Design Proposition

The Reference House is not a prototype offered as proof, nor a model house intended for repetition. It is a worked interpretation: one contemporary family house in which the propositions of Ovid’s House are forced to coexist with an architectural language, a programme, a structure, an envelope, services, weather, maintenance and the ordinary inconveniences of occupation.

The worked house tests how general propositions interact in a specific arrangement.

The Reference House therefore carries several authorities at once. Ovid’s House supplies the long-life architectural position. The pattern set supplies reusable responses, with the service-topology family providing the strongest current generative sequence. G-01 supplies a bounded Georgian-derived grammar. The courtyard is a project morphology. The material and tectonic choices belong to this house. Technical systems remain project selections rather than doctrine. None of these authorities is allowed to absorb the others.

The result is a four-bedroom detached courtyard house, developed at virtual-design scope. It is coordinated strongly enough to expose architectural consequences, but it is not a completed technical design. Structural capacity, detailed building physics, regulatory approval, plant sizing, product selection and physical interface performance remain downstream work.

It is also a deliberately favourable integration fixture. A detached perimeter, open courtyard and concentrated service flank make several maintenance and service-topology problems easier than they would be in compact attached housing. A compact-terrace transfer test therefore matters separately: the value of this house is that the propositions coexist here, not that its plant room, service flank or maintenance strips should be exported as a template.

# 13. Site and type

There is no commissioned site.

The Reference House is deliberately developed against a surrogate condition: a medium London or Zone-4-type plot, with the spatial and environmental pressures that such a site implies but without pretending that orientation, boundaries, utilities, neighbouring buildings, drainage or planning constraints are known. It is therefore a virtual integration project rather than a planning proposal.

The absence of a real site changes what can legitimately be fixed. The house may establish a type, programme, morphology and internal order. It may test environmental rotations and service approaches. It may not claim that a south façade is truly south, that a service route will meet a real utility connection, or that a particular wall faces an actual neighbour. Those facts belong to a future site.

## A courtyard house, deliberately

The selected type is a detached two-storey courtyard house.

The courtyard is not inherited from Georgian precedent and is not a requirement of Ovid’s House. It is a project decision: a genuine open-to-sky interior court used to organise movement, light, outlook and sheltered external space within a disciplined footprint.

The current outer reservation is approximately 12.0 by 10.8 metres. The open court is approximately 3.6 by 4.0 metres. This creates shallow occupied bands around the court: about 4.2 metres gross on the side wings and 3.4 metres gross to front and rear. Across two principal storeys the enclosed plate is approximately 230 square metres before final wall thickness, stair voids and local adjustments.

That is not a small house. The relevant claim is narrower: for a four-bedroom detached courtyard house carrying real plant, storage, maintenance and service space, the plan remains disciplined enough to behave as a domestic house rather than a miniature estate house.

The courtyard earns its cost in several ways.

First, it limits occupied depth. Important rooms can reach an outer façade, the court, or both. Second, it creates an internal orientation device: movement around the house repeatedly returns to a known centre. Third, it permits a strong sequence between street, entrance, stair hall, court and garden without requiring a monumental central hall. Fourth, it gives the house an external room whose scale is intimate rather than scenic.

The court also creates liabilities. It increases envelope area. Its walls complicate roof and drainage geometry. At two storeys it is too deep to behave as a reliable sunny garden. It requires deliberate maintenance access. Its value therefore cannot rest on romance alone.

The environmental reading is explicit: the courtyard is a shaded light court and spatial condenser, not the primary sunny amenity. The rear garden retains that role.

## Programme before symmetry

The programme is contemporary family occupation, not reconstructed Georgian domestic life.

The current ground floor places a drawing room to the west/front, a family room to the west/rear, an entrance and principal stair hall at the south centre, a compact study to the south-east, a service suite along the east flank, a garden dining hall to the north centre and the kitchen to the north-east.

The upper floor holds four bedrooms, the principal stair and landing sequence, a courtyard gallery, a family bathroom above the service hub and dry dressing/linen/storage where an earlier en-suite had been considered.

That deletion matters. The en-suite was not lost because the plan lacked space. It was removed because its position demanded a disproportionate drainage route across the structural order or a second permanent stack. The house is allowed to lose a convenience when the infrastructure required to support it is architecturally poor.

The same discipline governs the rest of the programme. Rooms are not added to fill a symmetrical shell. Services are not pushed into residual cupboards merely to protect principal rooms. Storage and maintenance approach are treated as spatial demands rather than annotations added after the plan is composed.

## The site is allowed to change the house

Because the present site is synthetic, several elements remain intentionally reversible in principle.

The service flank is shown to the east for continuity of coordination; a real site may mirror it. Solar control will depend on actual orientation. Utility entry belongs where real records place it. The garden relationship must respond to real boundaries and outlook. The external maintenance strips must be checked against actual access, planting, neighbouring ownership and temporary-work constraints.

The Reference House therefore fixes a type without pretending to fix a site.

Its persistent identity is more abstract than its current compass points:

- detached contemporary family occupation;
- a genuine open court;
- a legible front-to-court-to-garden sequence;
- shallow occupied bands;
- a concentrated high-service flank;
- a principal stair with architectural presence;
- G-01 as the selected architectural grammar;
- technical and maintenance geography made spatially explicit.

A future site is expected to alter the coordinates. It should not be allowed to dissolve those relationships casually.

# 14. Architectural order, repose and tectonic language

The house uses a Georgian-derived architectural language without attempting to reconstruct a Georgian household.

G-01 is deliberately small. It does not prescribe a universal room ratio, a centred entrance, an odd bay count, one roof shape or a fixed historical circulation sequence. Its stronger commitments concern architectural order: a bounded classical field; related and ranked openings; a principal entrance that is architecturally legible; an upper termination composed as part of the whole; relational spatial and sectional hierarchy; and restrained classical articulation concentrated where it carries architectural weight.

The Reference House conforms to those rules without asking them to explain its courtyard, service strategy or tectonic details.

## Order without false symmetry

The principal front is organised as a centred four-bay field. The entrance occupies the third bay rather than the geometric centre.

That decision is not a tolerated imperfection. It is the architectural resolution of a real conflict. A centred entrance would interfere with the principal stair or require a contrived internal vestibule whose only purpose was to satisfy exterior symmetry. The alternative—allowing each room to punch an opening independently—would dissolve the façade into plan residue.

The façade remains ordered while the entrance is off-centre.

The principal bay axes remain stable while individual attributes respond to the spaces behind them. The stair window retains the bay centreline but raises its sill because the stair owns that requirement. Ground-floor principal openings are taller than the upper family. The entrance is ranked within the same field rather than enlarged into a portico-scale event.

The other elevations use their own scoped orders. The garden front is composed around three principal fields. The service side admits greater asymmetry but groups technical expression into a controlled middle zone. The west elevation remains quiet and room-owned. The courtyard has its own internal order rather than inheriting the front grid by force.

The house is therefore composed through several related orders, not one universal symmetry diagram.

## Section as architecture

The hierarchy is also sectional.

The ground floor has a clear ceiling datum of approximately 2.95 metres. The first-floor finished floor is at approximately +3.30 metres. The upper clear ceiling reaches approximately +6.00 metres, with the cornice/eaves control around +6.25 metres.

This creates restrained ground-floor primacy without converting the house into a miniature piano-nobile villa. The principal shared rooms, entrance and garden sequence gain sectional rank over the bedroom storey, but the difference remains domestic.

A flatter equalised section was considered and rejected because the two levels became too uniformly stacked. A more emphatic principal storey was also rejected because the house became grander than its programme justified.

This is characteristic of the broader method: historical language is used to establish hierarchy, not to import spectacle.

## Arrival, compression and recentering

The off-centre entrance does not cause the house to remain off-centre.

Arrival passes through the front field into the stair hall, where the internal order re-centres on the courtyard. From there the garden becomes legible beyond the north side of the house. The architectural centre therefore moves during occupation: exterior field, entrance threshold, internal court, garden.

The sequence is deliberately stronger than a single line of bilateral symmetry.

The courtyard adds a second dialect to the house. Its warmer material possibility, including restrained green tile or other Andalusian inflection, remains local. It is not allowed to blur into a claim that G-01 itself contains an Andalusian court. The outer house and the court can belong to the same architecture without pretending to have the same lineage.

## Repose

Repose is not treated here as a historical style effect.

The house seeks repose through intelligible hierarchy, privacy, useful daylight, environmental control, controlled technical expression and the absence of unnecessary visual demands. Its rooms should not feel like demonstrations of adaptability or engineering. Maintainability is valuable only if ordinary occupation remains ordinary.

Where structure is conspicuous, support, mass and equilibrium are reviewed as architectural expression rather than a validated comfort metric. The house does not treat cantilevers as harmful or historically familiar support as therapeutic; any sense of structural calm or tension should simply be intentional rather than accidental.

This has direct consequences.

Quiet masonry walls remain directly finished where no service burden justifies another layer. Principal floors remain continuous rather than raised or panelised. Technical access is concentrated at real interfaces and service spaces. Plant and terminals are given hierarchy rather than hidden behind fake architecture. Metal is used sparsely where touch, wear, indexing or disassembly gives it a reason to exist.

## Tectonic language

The visible construction follows a simple rule: architectural expression should not contradict actual assembly.

The permanent masonry opening belongs to the long-lived building. A replaceable window or doorset sits within a designed fixing and tolerance zone. Architraves may cover that interface, but they are not the primary weather, air or thermal seal. Skirting may carry a bounded dry service route where that route is useful, but it must remain credible joinery rather than disguised trunking. A cornice may resolve a real wall/ceiling movement junction, but it is not a universal service duct.

Traditional architectural depth is therefore used as tolerance capacity where it already belongs to the composition.

The hierarchy is:

**coarse permanent construction → designed adjustment → precise assembly → architectural overlap**.

Brass and bronze remain a functional accent rather than a visual system. The useful intuition—*brass marks where the building comes apart*—survives only where something genuinely moves, wears, protects, indexes or is intended to be removed.

The architecture would be weakened if every joint announced its replaceability.

# 15. Material, structural and assembly system

The Reference House is materially conservative where conservatism improves longevity and understanding.

Its current baseline is masonry enclosure, short-span engineered timber floors, conventional continuous room finishes and selectively designed interfaces. The system is not chosen because traditional construction is inherently superior. It is chosen because the house already possesses two long-lived wall systems—the outer boundary and courtyard boundary—and those walls can carry useful structural, environmental and architectural work simultaneously.

## A layered masonry envelope

The working external wall family is:

**real facing masonry outer leaf → drained and insulated cavity → substantial loadbearing masonry inner leaf → direct mineral/plaster finish by default**.

The exact insulation, masonry strength, cavity width, ties, airtightness strategy and thermal junctions are not fixed. The current architectural planning family assumes approximately 400–425 millimetres overall wall depth, with 450 millimetres used as a whole-house geometry stress test.

That stress test matters because earlier planning had used a shallower notional wall. The house now survives a more credible high-performance masonry depth without silently stealing essential room or stair width.

The inner leaf is the first structural comparator. The outer masonry leaf remains the weathering face rather than being falsely described as a monolithic loadbearing wall where it is not.

The wall must remain complete when faster-changing trim is removed. Airtightness, moisture control and weather resistance cannot depend on an architrave, removable lining or decorative cover simply because those elements are accessible.

## Structure generated by the courtyard

The central structural fact is geometric.

The house does not require floors to span the 12.0 metre building width. The courtyard creates a ring of comparatively short spans between outer and inner permanent walls.

The working structural concept uses loadbearing masonry inner leaves at the outer and courtyard envelopes with engineered timber floor framing between them. Side strips span approximately 4.2 metres. Front and rear centre strips span approximately 3.4 metres.

Four short edge beams continue the courtyard support lines through the front and rear wings. Local stair and service openings are framed deliberately rather than cut into an otherwise finished floor system.

This is pre-engineering coordination, not structural design. No beam, joist, lintel, bearing, tie, foundation or connection has been sized. The value of the concept is prior to calculation: the architecture appears capable of being carried by an ordinary, legible load path without a whole-house steel frame, long transfer members or columns intruding into principal rooms.

If engineering later demonstrates otherwise, the architecture must respond. The present drawings are not permitted to convert plausibility into adequacy.

## The floor zone

A 350 millimetre floor/service/ceiling reservation remains the current architectural datum between ground ceiling and first-floor level.

The first engineering comparison is an engineered timber family around the 300 millimetre depth class, with deck and required acoustic/fire layers around it. That is a coordination envelope, not a specification.

Services are deliberately prevented from consuming this zone indiscriminately. The structural floor is not treated as a universal horizontal service void. Necessary crossings are designed and recorded; larger service distribution prefers explicit routes and the service flank.

The diaphragm remains part of the permanent floor structure. Any future access layer cannot become structurally necessary.

## Quiet walls and selective infrastructure

The interior rejects the idea that every wall should become demountable simply because change is possible.

Three conditions are distinguished.

**Quiet permanent wall.** Direct mineral or plaster finish on the permanent background remains the default where there is little service or change burden.

**Controlled architectural datum.** Where fixing, local services or replaceable joinery justify it, sparse known grounds are introduced at skirtings, architraves, picture rails or specific heavy-load positions.

**Replaceable architectural lining.** This remains an experimental challenger rather than the house-wide baseline. If physical testing cannot equal an excellent conventional wall for solidity, acoustics, appearance, fixing behaviour and reinstatement, the house remains complete without it.

That asymmetry is deliberate. A long-life house does not become more credible by maximising the quantity of removable construction.

## Openings as life-separated assemblies

The representative window relationship is:

**permanent masonry opening → durable mounting/tolerance zone → replaceable frame → removable internal trim**.

The architectural opening survives several frame generations. The frame can be changed without redefining the wall. Internal trim may expose fixing and tolerance zones, but the trim is not the primary environmental boundary.

The same logic applies to doorsets in genuinely permanent openings.

A slim thermally broken aluminium window remains a project comparator rather than a proprietary selection or general rule. The final product must still prove air, water, thermal, fixing, replacement and emergency-egress performance.

## What remains unresolved

The current material and structural system has not been physically or professionally validated at the level required for construction.

Important open obligations include:

- structural member and connection design;
- floor-edge engineering;
- thermal bridges and U-values;
- moisture and airtightness junctions;
- fire and acoustic build-ups;
- window jamb/head/sill performance;
- external threshold weathering;
- wall/ceiling movement-junction behaviour;
- ordinary workmanship tolerances.

Two physical questions remain particularly important to the house: the structural floor edge and the wall/ceiling movement junction. Both can still simplify or reject current implementation ideas.

The architectural system is intentionally able to survive those failures. They are also the current non-standard proof families: the ordinary wall, floor, plant, opening and service conditions should not quietly acquire bespoke mechanisms merely because the project values maintainability.

# 16. Maintenance geography

Maintenance begins with space.

A valve may be technically accessible yet practically unserviceable. A plant room may exist yet provide no replacement route. A window may be replaceable in theory while the façade offers no safe working position. A roof may contain a hatch while still offering no credible method for carrying tools, materials or edge protection.

The Reference House therefore treats maintenance as geography rather than a collection of access panels.

## The service flank

The eastern side of the current virtual house concentrates the highest service intensity.

At ground level a dedicated service suite contains the plant room, utility functions, WC, primary distribution and the base of the vertical service zone. Above it, the family bathroom and dry-side access align with the same geography.

The present plant-room reservation is approximately 2.9 by 2.2 metres gross, with a nominal 1000 millimetre external replacement-opening target toward the service side. **Those dimensions are held rather than doctrinally defended.** Real equipment selection, working clearances, area/cost/carbon comparison and the competing need for entrance-storey sanitary adaptation must determine whether the room should shrink, reorganise or retain the current reservation. The task—credible plant service and withdrawal—is stronger than the present room size.

The service side also hosts the principal utility approach, ventilation terminals where technically feasible, heat-pump geography, drainage inspection points and most external technical expression.

This concentration protects principal rooms from becoming service corridors while avoiding the opposite mistake of hiding infrastructure so thoroughly that it can no longer be worked on.

## Loss of stair use

The same finite service suite now carries a second foreseeable future obligation.

The west/rear family room is retained as a credible future entrance-storey sleeping room. Detailed design must preserve a substantially step-free route from the principal entrance to that room and to entrance-storey sanitary provision. Within the existing service-suite envelope, the WC/basin arrangement must prove that a **potential level-access shower** can be accommodated without moving the primary wet-service geography or enlarging the house.

This is an M4(2)-informed planning comparator, not an M4(2) compliance claim. A speculative domestic lift shaft is not reserved. The current generous stair width preserves a lower-burden future stair-lift option, while a real wheelchair-user brief or planning requirement is allowed to reopen the plan.

The point is the same as with technical adaptation: preserve cheap, high-value options; do not purchase systemic infrastructure merely because a future need is imaginable.

## Vertical distribution

A bounded vertical service zone links the plant/service suite with the upper bathroom and adjacent dry-side access.

Its current clear reservation is approximately 900 by 700 millimetres. It is large enough to coordinate one soil/vent stack, domestic-water risers, small-bore heating distribution where required and separated dry electrical/data routes. It is deliberately not a generic shaft into which all future systems may be pushed.

Large ventilation ducts are treated separately. Spare volume is not virtuous when no credible task justifies it.

## Horizontal routes

High-consequence wet services remain short and concentrated.

The kitchen, utility, WC and family bathroom are arranged around the same service geography. The family bathroom stacks above the service suite. Kitchen waste reaches the primary soil zone by a short branch. The rejected en-suite demonstrates the rule by exception: a private convenience was removed because its drainage burden cut across structure and permanent fabric disproportionately.

Dry services are allowed more flexibility. Where justified, substantial removable skirting can carry bounded electrical or data routes, rising at known door or architrave zones to accessible socket and switch positions. The route and the accessory are not required to occupy the same datum.

Principal rooms remain free of primary wet, soil, large-duct and plant routes.

## External maintenance

The house preserves ordinary ground-supported access to its façades and roof edges within the **surrogate site condition**.

The service side is the most important current working strip because it combines plant replacement, utility access, technical terminals, rainwater goods and courtyard drainage inspection. The west side retains ordinary façade and scaffold access. The rear garden preserves enough working line for upper-window, masonry, cornice and gutter maintenance.

The courtyard is also treated as a working space when necessary. Components of a mobile tower can be brought through the garden-to-court route and erected inside the court for maintenance to courtyard elevations.

Landscape must be coordinated with those maintenance positions. Permanent raised planters, fountains, stores or other fixed objects must not casually consume the only credible maintenance position.

A real site can invalidate this geography. If boundaries or ownership remove the assumed side route, the correct response is to mirror, relocate, simplify or reopen the architecture—not to cite the virtual strip as evidence that external maintenance has been solved.

## Roof access without symbolic infrastructure

The current house does not include a permanent roof hatch.

The likely roof tasks—waterproofing renewal, gutter and coping work, inspection of a small number of penetrations, local repairs and possible future solar work—remain infrequent. Planned scaffold or tower access provides both a route to the roof and the edge protection/material handling that a hatch alone would not.

A hatch would add another permanent roof penetration without eliminating the need for safe temporary works.

The decision is therefore task-derived rather than ideological. If a future design adds rooftop equipment requiring frequent access, the question must be reopened.

## Legibility

Maintenance geography includes information as well as space.

Primary isolations, distribution points and service routes should be discoverable without relying on an app account or a vanished contractor. A small physical service index near the plant room is one place where durable engraved metal has a clear role.

The objective is not to turn the house into an exposed technical diagram. It is to ensure that future work begins with knowledge rather than demolition. Digital records and stewardship improve that recovery; the physical architecture should not become unintelligible if they disappear.

# 17. Water and environmental systems

Water is given disproportionate attention because water failures are disproportionately destructive.

The Reference House uses form, gravity and visible consequence before it relies on sensors or hidden redundancy. Environmental systems follow the same hierarchy: architectural form is expected to do useful work before mechanical equipment compensates for it.

## Roof water moves outward

A conventional courtyard roof creates a tempting but dangerous geometry: inward gutters, valleys and outlets concentrated around the least accessible part of the building.

The selected roof does the opposite.

Four very-low-slope roof fields fall outward from the courtyard toward the external perimeter. The diagonal boundaries between those fields act as watersheds rather than valleys. Primary roof water therefore moves away from the centre of the house.

There is no principal inner courtyard gutter and no internal roof outlet.

External collection edges remain inspectable. Main downpipes are placed on side and rear return geography rather than across the principal front. Overflow is intended to become visible outside rather than reveal blockage first as internal damp.

The low-slope roof is not selected as a stylistic statement. It is a project response to failure geography. G-01 requires the upper termination to belong to the architectural composition; it does not require a visible pitched roof. The controlled cornice/parapet line therefore resolves the roof architecturally while the roof plane remains largely recessive.

## The courtyard manages only itself

Because roof water is sent outward, the courtyard drain manages principally the court’s own approximately 14.4 square metres of open-to-sky area plus local wall runoff.

The primary collector is placed toward the service side and leads through a short, inspectable route beneath service/circulation geography to an external inspection point. A separate higher-level emergency overflow provides an independent failure route before abnormal standing water reaches internal thresholds.

This is not a claim that every pipe can remain visible. It is a hierarchy of consequences: short, straight, inspectable buried infrastructure beneath low-consequence geography is accepted where it removes much more dangerous hidden collection from the centre of the roof.

## Passive environmental hierarchy

The house begins with shallow occupied bands and multiple aspects.

The courtyard ring means many important rooms can draw daylight and purge air from more than one direction. The plan therefore avoids depending on one deep rear room or one large glazed façade for environmental quality.

Glazing remains disciplined. The project explicitly rejects the generic solution of turning the garden elevation into an undifferentiated wall of glass. Opening area, shading and solar control must respond to actual orientation and dynamic modelling once a site exists.

Openable windows remain the principal summer purge mechanism. Mechanical ventilation is not treated as cooling.

## The courtyard as shaded light court

At approximately 3.6 by 4.0 metres and two storeys high, the court is relatively deep.

Simple solar screening demonstrates that the court floor receives little reliable direct sun outside high summer. Its sky aperture is also constrained. The architecture therefore does not assign principal rooms to court light alone.

The court’s useful environmental role is different:

- diffuse daylight;
- orientation;
- a sheltered external microclimate;
- secondary purge and cross-ventilation opportunity;
- an intimate external room protected from the street.

The sunny amenity remains the rear garden.

## Ventilation

Central mechanical ventilation with heat recovery is the current Reference House baseline, subject to final route, fan, acoustic and commissioning design.

Habitable rooms receive supply; wet rooms receive extract. Cooker source capture remains a separate direct-to-outside route because grease and cooking contaminants should be removed at source rather than loaded into a whole-house ventilation system.

Openable windows remain necessary for purge ventilation and summer heat removal.

If competent design cannot resolve the MVHR routes proportionately, continuous mechanical extract remains the fallback. The architecture does not depend on one proprietary ventilation product.

## Heat and electricity

An air-to-water heat pump is the current working heat-source family. Low-temperature radiators or other passive emitters are the baseline. Whole-house underfloor heating has not been selected merely because it would hide emitters.

The house also reserves approximately 50 square metres of PV-capable roof field as a forward coordination control. That reserve must remain compatible with drainage, safe access and roof penetrations. No battery is presently reserved.

These are project system choices, not principles of Ovid’s House.

## What the environmental design does not prove

The current architecture has been coordinated, not simulated to completion.

It does not yet establish:

- final U-values or thermal bridges;
- airtightness;
- overheating compliance;
- daylight compliance;
- heat loss or heat-pump capacity;
- MVHR duty or acoustics;
- PV yield;
- rainwater hydraulic sizing;
- actual site drainage destination;
- robustness against site-specific future climate / hazard scenarios.

Those questions require a real site, competent modelling and technical design. A long-life real project should test expensive-to-change geometry against a reasonable range of future conditions, state the scenario and uncertainty, and allow orientation, openings, shading, roof/drainage, landscape or plant strategy to rewind where necessary.

The architecture’s current claim is prior and narrower: it has not knowingly created a form that must be rescued by inaccessible drainage or excessive active systems under the bounded virtual assumptions used so far.

# The Interior Architecture System

This chapter interface is deliberately established before the selected interior system is fully authored.

Part IV already fixes several constraints that the eventual interior architecture must respect: quiet permanent walls remain the default where service burden is low; principal floors should feel continuous and settled; technical access must occur at credible architectural datums rather than turning every surface into equipment; and visible joinery must belong to the rooms even when it also carries fixing, service or replacement logic.

The resolved chapter will define the selected family of wall finishes and linings, floor/ceiling relationships, skirtings, architraves, grilles, access pieces, fixing grounds and other repeated interior interfaces. It must distinguish what is permanent, what is replaceable, what is merely conventional finish, and what has genuinely earned a special interface.

It must not assume that removable construction is better construction. Conventional plaster, timber, stone, tile and other ordinary finishes remain valid wherever the lifecycle case for a more elaborate system is weak.

This structural placeholder is therefore an invitation to resolve one interior architecture, not a licence to invent a universal kit of parts.

# Openings as Replaceable Assemblies

This chapter interface is reserved for the selected opening family: windows, external doors, internal doors where relevant, thresholds, reveals, architraves, fixing/tolerance zones and the junction between permanent masonry and faster-lived assemblies.

The governing relationship is already clear:

**permanent opening → designed adjustment/fixing zone → replaceable assembly → architectural overlap**.

The unresolved work is to turn that relationship into one coherent architectural and technical family for this house without making trim carry weather, air, fire or structural duties it cannot honestly own.

The resolved chapter must address the repeated architectural logic of heads, jambs, sills, thresholds, internal reveals and replacement access while keeping product selection, engineering adequacy and competent envelope/fire/security validation within their proper evidence owners.

A successful system should make the opening feel like permanent architecture even though the frame or doorset can change. Replaceability is a property of the interface, not the visual subject of the room or façade.
