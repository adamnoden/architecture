# Ovid’s House
## Part IV — Reference House

---

The preceding parts describe a proposition and a language of recurring architectural responses. Neither is a house.

A principle can remain persuasive while conflicts between principles stay hidden. A pattern can read clearly in isolation while its consequences become disproportionate when several patterns occupy the same wall, floor, room or boundary. Maintenance access can consume useful area. A generous service zone can weaken a plan. A well-ordered elevation can become hostile to drainage or replacement. A structural shortcut can erase the architectural hierarchy it was meant to support.

The **Reference House** exists to expose those conflicts.

It is one worked interpretation of Ovid’s House: a contemporary detached family house, using a Georgian-derived architectural grammar, organised around an open courtyard, and developed far enough that plan, section, structure, services, environmental strategy, maintenance geography and occupied interfaces have had to negotiate with one another.

It is not a prototype of a universal house. The courtyard is not doctrine. Georgian architecture is not doctrine. Masonry is not doctrine. The selected roof, heating system, ventilation strategy and material language are not doctrine. They belong to this project because they survived this project's particular integration problem.

Nor is the Reference House proof. It remains a coordinated virtual design. Its structural concept is not engineered; its building physics are not fully simulated; its services are not detailed by specialist designers; its principal non-standard interfaces have not all been physically tested; and no competent external review has yet been processed into the design. Those limits are part of the case, not footnotes to conceal.

The value of the house lies elsewhere. It makes the argument concrete enough to fail.

# 13. Site and type

## A house without a site

The Reference House has no commissioned site.

That fact governs everything that follows. It should not be read as a planning proposal or as an optimised response to one London plot. Boundaries, orientation, neighbours, topography, trees, sewer levels, utilities, planning policy and ground conditions are all unknown. Where the project needs geometry in order to proceed, it uses a deliberately ordinary **London / Zone-4-type detached-house context** as a stress condition rather than pretending that virtual coordinates are site facts.

The house is therefore best understood as a **type under test**.

Its current outer reservation is approximately **12.0 × 10.8 metres**, arranged over two principal storeys around an open **3.6 × 4.0 metre courtyard**. Before wall thickness, stairs and local voids are deducted, the enclosed plate is about **115 square metres per storey**. The result is not a small house in an absolute sense. It is a relatively compact four-bedroom detached courtyard house in relation to the programme and to the earlier, more expansive versions from which it was reduced.

The plan is not tied to a compass direction. Drawings place the entrance to the south and the garden to the north for coordination. The service side is shown to the east. A real site may mirror or rotate the arrangement. What matters is not the inherited compass direction but the relationships the plan is trying to preserve.

## The courtyard as project morphology

The courtyard is the strongest project-specific choice in the house.

It was not derived from House Kernel principles, and it is not a requirement of the Georgian-derived grammar. It remains because it earns several things simultaneously: an internal point of orientation, a second aspect for many spaces, daylight to circulation, a sheltered outdoor room, and a spatial interval between arrival and garden.

It also carries costs. Four internal elevations increase envelope area. The court complicates roof geometry, drainage and external maintenance. At roughly two storeys high and less than four metres wide, it is a relatively deep void. Direct sun at its floor is limited even in summer and scarce outside it. The court is therefore treated honestly as a **shaded light court and spatial condenser**, not marketed as a sunny central garden.

The rear garden remains the principal sunny outdoor amenity. Important rooms retain responsibility for useful daylight from outer façades rather than depending on the court to rescue a deep plan.

This distinction matters because the project does not defend architectural preferences by disguising their consequences. The courtyard survives after those consequences are made explicit.

## Ground floor: rooms rather than a continuous living field

The selected ground floor is organised around two different kinds of movement.

The principal architectural sequence begins at the front door, enters a proper stair hall, encounters the court, and continues visually toward the garden side of the house. The court belongs to arrival even though the ordinary route through the house does not require a person to walk outside.

An all-weather internal gallery runs along one inner flank of the court. It connects the entrance territory to the kitchen and rear rooms and gives the service side an intelligible internal approach. The gallery is useful, but it is deliberately subordinate to the main arrival sequence.

Around those routes sit distinct rooms:

- a drawing room toward the front of one side wing;
- a longer family room toward the garden;
- the entrance and principal stair in the front centre;
- a compact study toward the front of the service side;
- a plant, utility and WC territory in the middle of that flank;
- a garden dining room on the rear centreline;
- a kitchen at the rear of the service side.

The three rear domestic rooms form a connected sequence rather than one undifferentiated great room. The family room, dining room and kitchen can open generously to one another without surrendering their identities.

That choice is deliberate. A single large rear kitchen-family-dining room would have been easy to furnish and easy to market. It would also flatten the spatial hierarchy, increase structural pressure at the courtyard corners, reduce acoustic retreat and weaken the court's role as a sequence-maker. The house tests a different proposition: contemporary family life does not require every useful connection to become one room.

The asymmetry of the internal plan is also deliberate. The outer mass can remain composed while one side works harder for circulation and services. The plan does not need to display every internal subdivision on the façade.

## The stair as an architectural commitment

The principal stair sits in the entrance hall rather than being pushed into the service flank.

A conventional two-flight stair proved awkward within the shallow front wing if comfortable going and full landings were retained. The selected answer is a three-flight stair with eighteen risers, full landings and no winders. At the current 3.3 metre floor-to-floor datum its planning geometry is generous for an ordinary domestic stair: approximately 183 millimetres rise, 280 millimetres going and a clear width around one metre.

The exact numbers are less important than the decision behind them. The stair is allowed to consume enough plan to be comfortable and legible. Compactness is not pursued by making the principal vertical route mean.

The stair also establishes a useful hierarchy between different kinds of circulation. It belongs to arrival and to the architecture of the house. The gallery belongs to everyday distribution and maintenance. Neither needs to imitate the servant circulation of an eighteenth-century house for those roles to remain distinct.

## Upper floor: four bedrooms, not five because five will fit

The current upper floor has four bedrooms.

A project ceiling of five bedrooms exists, but it is a limit rather than a target. The four-bedroom arrangement gives the principal bedroom the longest field on the west/rear side, provides three credible secondary bedrooms, and allows the stair, upper gallery, family bathroom and storage to remain useful spaces rather than residual corridors.

The upper circulation forms a three-sided route around the court: stair and south landing, gallery along the service side, then a short northern landing. There is no full gallery around all four sides. Doing so would consume the width of the western bedrooms for the sake of diagrammatic completeness.

The family bathroom sits above the principal service geography and retains an outer wall for real daylight and purge ventilation. An earlier principal en-suite was deliberately removed. Its drainage burden would have required either long gravity runs across the floor framing, a second stack, lowered ceilings or a relocation of the main service system for the sake of one private convenience. The former en-suite territory becomes dressing, linen and storage instead.

This is a small but important demonstration of the project's hierarchy. Services are not required to submit to every programme request. Sometimes maintainable architecture improves when the programme is simplified.

## A type, not a template

Nothing in this chapter should be mistaken for a universal Ovid’s House plan.

A different site may favour a different morphology. A terraced house, rural house or apartment could pursue the same principles through completely different geometry. Even another detached Reference House could reverse the service side, lose the courtyard or select a different architectural grammar if the brief changed.

The worked house matters because its choices are specific enough to create consequences.

# 14. Architectural order, repose and tectonic language

## A Georgian-derived language without historical reconstruction

The Reference House selects **G-01**, a bounded Georgian-derived domestic grammar.

The grammar is intentionally smaller than a style guide. It does not prescribe a golden ratio, a centred door, an odd number of bays, a six-over-six sash, a particular roof form or a historical service plan. It requires a more general but still recognisable bundle: an ordered classical architectural field, related and ranked openings, a legible principal entrance, an integrated upper termination, relational spatial and sectional hierarchy, and a preference for concentrating explicit classical articulation where architectural rank actually occurs.

This distinction protects both sides of the project.

Without a grammar, the house could become technically coherent but architecturally generic. With an over-prescriptive historical grammar, the technical and domestic realities of a contemporary house would be forced to imitate circumstances that no longer exist.

The Reference House was tested against G-01 only after the grammar had been frozen. It passed without the grammar being rewritten to accommodate the house. That order matters. The worked example applies the language; it does not define it.

## The front: order without a centred door

The principal front uses a centred four-bay field across the twelve-metre width.

The entrance occupies the third bay rather than the geometric centre. This emerged from the plan: the principal stair occupies the western part of the entrance hall, and forcing a central doorway would either collide with it or create an artificial vestibule solely to preserve abstract symmetry.

The solution is not to abandon order. The bay field remains regular and legible. The door is ranked clearly as the entrance. The composition accepts that **a coherent classical field and an off-centre entrance can coexist**.

This is representative of the house's wider use of symmetry. Alignment is given an owner and a scope. The front has one order; the court another; the garden elevation another; the service elevation another. They relate without becoming one global grid.

The front windows follow the same principle. Ground-floor principal-room openings are taller and more substantial than those above. The stair window remains on its bay axis while its sill rises in response to the stair. Centreline, sill and head need not all be governed by the same authority. Architectural order is maintained by coordination rather than by forcing every attribute into uniformity.

## The court as an inner order

Inside the house, the court establishes a stronger centre than the front door.

Arrival begins off-centre, then re-centres on the court and the garden sequence beyond. The north and south faces of the court use the central axis directly. The gallery side follows its own repeated rhythm. The room side is allowed to respond more closely to the spaces behind it.

This is not accidental irregularity. It is a hierarchy of orders.

The court can also carry a bounded local dialect. Earlier studies explored a warmer character with arches and restrained green tile or zellige. If retained, that influence belongs only here. It does not convert the whole house into an Andalusian-Georgian hybrid and does not modify G-01 itself. The junction between dialects must still be architecturally composed.

## Section as architecture

The house is two storeys, but the storeys are not treated as equal stacked trays.

The ground floor has a clear ceiling around 2.95 metres. The first-floor datum is at 3.30 metres. The upper clear ceiling is around 6.00 metres, with the principal cornice or eaves-control line at roughly 6.25 metres.

The difference is restrained. The house does not need a monumental piano nobile or a double-height saloon to express hierarchy. Instead the principal ground floor gains a little more vertical generosity, taller openings and a stronger entrance scale. Private rooms above remain calmer and slightly more compressed.

The selected section therefore says:

> **principal shared ground floor > private upper floor**

without turning domestic hierarchy into spectacle.

The stair reinforces this order. It is architecturally present, but not enlarged into a theatrical void. Repose depends partly on resisting the idea that every important relation must become a visual event.

## Repose and legible equilibrium

The Reference House uses **repose** as a design requirement independent of G-01.

The Georgian-derived language is useful here because it can support legible hierarchy, bounded openings, substantial wall fields and visible relationships of support. It is not claimed to possess a unique neurological virtue. Another architectural language could pursue repose differently.

In this house, repose means that principal rooms should feel settled enough to recede from attention when daily life requires it. Openings should appear borne by plausible construction. Privacy should be real rather than notional. Technical interfaces should not advertise themselves continuously. Daylight, ventilation, shade and temperature should have intelligible local control.

The courtyard and stair can provide moments of compression and release. The service side can be visibly more workmanlike. The house need not be monotonous. The larger order should simply remain stronger than the exceptions.

This requirement becomes particularly important where serviceability enters the occupied room. The project could easily have made maintainability its visual theme: brass seams around access panels, demountable wall grids, floor hatches and exposed infrastructure. It rejects that outcome.

The intended room should still be desirable if the occupant knows nothing about the maintenance doctrine behind it.

## Tectonic language: architecture where the building changes

The Reference House gives ordinary architectural elements real technical duties where those duties already belong naturally.

Skirtings can protect the wall base, cover tolerance and carry a bounded dry route. Architraves can conceal the fixing and adjustment zone between long-lived masonry and a replaceable door or window frame. A cornice can cross a genuine wall-ceiling movement junction. A threshold can resolve wear, weather, drainage, movement and change of datum.

The direction is not to disguise engineering with decoration. Nor is it to expose every joint in the name of honesty. It is to make the place where assemblies meet into a deliberate architectural condition.

The typical hierarchy is:

**coarse permanent construction → designed adjustment → precise replaceable assembly → architectural overlap or cover**.

The cover is not allowed to become the thing that makes the building technically work. An architrave is not the primary air seal. A cornice is not permission to ignore fire or acoustic continuity. A decorative threshold is not introduced where the floor genuinely continues without a constructional change.

## Brass and bronze: a local punctuation

Brass and bronze remain part of the Reference House's local tectonic vocabulary, but only sparingly.

A useful intuition is that visible metal should usually indicate something that is touched, wears, protects, indexes, moves or can be removed. Door hardware, a durable service index, a sacrificial threshold edge or a genuine movement protector can justify it. A continuous brass grid announcing that the building is demountable does not.

“Brass marks where the building comes apart” survives here as a mnemonic, not a decorative instruction.

## The roof as the clearest negotiation between language and performance

An early version of the house preferred a visible pitched roof because it appeared more naturally aligned with the selected architectural language. Later drainage work challenged that assumption.

A pitched ring around the courtyard creates inner eaves, valleys or gutters at the least forgiving part of the building. A near-flat roof with internal outlets removes the visible roof but places critical rainwater components inside the envelope. Neither was satisfactory.

The selected roof uses four very-low-slope fields falling **outward from the courtyard toward the external perimeter**. The court receives no primary roof drainage. The roof remains largely recessive behind a controlled cornice/parapet line.

This does not require a loophole in G-01. The frozen grammar requires the upper termination and roof to belong coherently to the architectural order; it does not require a particular pitch or visibility. The low roof can therefore be a real project response to water and maintenance while the cornice/parapet becomes an honest architectural and weathering termination rather than a cosmetic screen.

The episode is representative of the project at its best. Architecture and technical performance are allowed to change one another without either being declared the universal authority.

# 15. Material, structural and assembly system

## Permanent fabric should earn its permanence

The Reference House currently uses a conventional, legible masonry envelope family:

**real masonry outer leaf → insulated and drained cavity → substantial masonry inner leaf → quiet direct internal finish by default**.

The outer leaf provides weathering and architectural mass. The inner leaf is intended to carry structural and thermal-mass duties where engineering and building physics support it. Insulation, airtightness and moisture control occupy their proper boundary layers rather than being inferred from the appearance of a thick wall.

This family is a project selection, not an Ovid’s House requirement. It is useful here because it offers mass, acoustic solidity, robust ordinary construction and a credible long service life while providing a clear permanent background against which shorter-lived openings, services and fit-out can be organised.

Red facing brick remains a leading external material, but even that is not yet a universal or irreversible claim. What matters more is that the material story is true. A surface should not imply a structural or weathering reality that the assembly does not possess.

## The courtyard creates the structural idea

The house contains two long-lived masonry boundaries: the outer perimeter and the courtyard ring.

That geometry produces a simple structural opportunity. Instead of spanning the full twelve-metre width or introducing an independent frame, the first floor can span the thickness of the ring between those two permanent boundaries.

The side strips span approximately **4.2 metres** from outer wall to courtyard wall. The front and rear central strips span approximately **3.4 metres**. Engineered timber is the current first comparison for the floor because those spans are ordinary enough to avoid whole-house steelwork and because a timber floor can remain a distinct structural layer above the masonry.

Where the side courtyard walls stop at the front and rear wings, four short repeated edge beams continue those support lines. They are local floor-edge conditions rather than long transfer girders. The stair and service openings are framed deliberately rather than cut through an already completed floor.

The architectural proposition is modest but important: the plan should admit an ordinary load path.

The current concept does not rely on room-shaping columns in principal spaces, deep transfer beams across the drawing room, or a frame hidden behind masonry solely to permit a façade. The permanent architectural walls do useful permanent structural work.

None of this is structural design. Member sizes, masonry strength, foundations, lateral stability, connection design, vibration, fire resistance and robustness remain for a structural engineer. The selected 350-millimetre floor zone is a coordination reservation that appears plausible for the current short spans; it is not a proved build-up.

## Protected structural geography

The courtyard corners and the short edge-beam reaction points concentrate real structural obligations. They are therefore protected from casual service use.

That sounds obvious, yet ordinary construction often allows services to discover structure only when a hole is required. Here the sequence is reversed. The architecture establishes support geometry; the service strategy then receives explicit crossing points and no-drill zones.

The same principle applies to the stair. It is a designed opening in the floor diaphragm. The main floor does not depend on decorative stair strings or later carpentry to become stable.

The permanent structural deck remains the diaphragm. No removable finish floor, access panel or future service layer is allowed to become structurally necessary.

## The floor is not an excuse for a plenum

A deep floor can tempt designers to fill it with everything.

The Reference House resists that move. The structural floor remains structure first. Long-life ducts or bounded parallel routes may cross it where deliberately coordinated, but it is not treated as an anonymous service void in which future trades are expected to find space.

That choice is one reason a house-wide removable floor is not selected. There are currently no active components beneath principal-room floors that justify covering every room with access joints, added depth, potential squeaks and acoustic complexity. If a later bounded route genuinely requires floor access, it can be addressed locally.

Serviceability is not maximised. It is proportioned to an actual task.

## Quiet walls first

The same discipline governs internal linings.

Most low-service permanent walls use a conventional direct mineral or plaster finish. This is not an embarrassed fallback from a more advanced demountable wall. It is the preferred solution where it gives solidity, acoustic quality, repairability and simplicity without trapping shorter-lived systems behind it.

A second condition adds **controlled architectural datums** only where repeated fixing, local services or replaceable joinery earn them: skirting grounds, architrave grounds, picture-rail grounds, local heavy-load plates and bounded adjustment geometry.

A fully replaceable dense-mineral lining remains an experimental challenger rather than the house-wide baseline. If physical testing cannot make it equal or superior to an excellent plaster wall in solidity, sound, appearance, fixing, reinstatement and workmanship robustness, the house loses nothing by rejecting it.

This is a recurring project rule:

> **A maintainable alternative must be good architecture before its maintainability counts as a benefit.**

## Openings as life-separated assemblies

Windows and doors provide one of the clearest places to separate lives.

The masonry opening belongs to the long-lived building. The frame or doorset is a replaceable assembly fitted within a deliberate fixing and tolerance zone. Internal trim can be removable where it provides useful access to that interface. Brittle plaster should not bridge directly from permanent masonry onto a frame expected to move or eventually be replaced.

The decorative trim is not allowed to become the primary air, water or thermal boundary. The technical seal remains technical; the architectural cover remains architectural.

The current window product is not frozen. A slim thermally broken aluminium family remains a credible comparator because it can carry the vertically proportioned G-01 opening language without placing exposed timber in the primary weathering position. A different product can replace it if performance, appearance, repair and replacement prove better.

Full-frame replacement is treated as a real operation. Internal removal of sashes, glazing and hardware is useful where the product permits it, but the project does not force every complete frame through the room merely to claim inside-only maintenance. Ordinary external scaffold or tower access remains available for the rarer full replacement.

## Movement without brittle continuity

Where two assemblies move differently, finishes should not be asked to pretend that they are one.

The representative wall-ceiling junction illustrates the problem. The wall and ceiling terminate independently. A cornice may overlap a bounded movement gap and attach to one controlling assembly while allowing the other to move behind it. The air, fire and acoustic layers still have to be resolved independently of the moulding.

That detail is not yet selected technically. A physical test programme must compare an excellent conventional joint against a concealed-slip cornice and any restrained visible-metal alternative. The book can therefore state the architectural problem and the intended interface logic; it cannot claim that a particular non-standard mechanism has been proved.

The difference is essential. Drawings can identify a sound problem before a prototype proves a sound solution.

# 16. Maintenance geography

## Maintenance occupies space

A building can contain an access panel and still be unmaintainable.

A person needs an approach, working room, light, a place for tools, a way to isolate the system and a path by which the failed component can leave. The replacement item needs the reverse route. External work needs ground from which a tower, scaffold or lifting method can operate.

The Reference House therefore treats maintenance as a **geography** rather than a collection of hatches.

The geography is intentionally uneven. High-service functions are concentrated where intervention is expected. Principal rooms are kept comparatively quiet. The point is not to make the whole house equally accessible; it is to give maintenance somewhere credible to happen.

## One service side

The main service territory occupies one flank of the house.

On the ground floor, the internal gallery provides an all-weather approach to a real plant room, utility/WC territory and the service edge of the kitchen. Above, the upper gallery provides dry-side access to the family bathroom and the principal riser zone.

The arrangement creates a useful gradient:

**principal occupied room → circulation / service approach → maintainable service boundary → plant or wet system**.

That gradient reduces the number of occasions on which a maintenance task has to begin by emptying a principal room.

The service side is shown east in the virtual house, but east is not the principle. A real site can mirror it. What must survive is the concentration and approach logic.

## The plant room is a room

The plant room is approximately **2.9 × 2.2 metres gross**. It is treated as an equipment room rather than an enlarged cupboard.

It holds, or reserves working geography for, the main water isolation and distribution, heating and hot-water equipment, ventilation plant, electrical/controls territory, the principal riser and a physical service index. Exact product packing remains unresolved, but the architectural requirement is already clear: a technician should be able to enter, see the systems, isolate them, work in front of them and remove major equipment without dismantling domestic cabinetry.

A nominal one-metre-class external replacement opening connects the room directly to the side maintenance strip. Large equipment therefore has a route that does not depend on carrying it through the entrance hall, around the courtyard and through finished rooms.

The room also localises consequence. Water-bearing plant sits over a robust, inspectable floor condition in high-service geography. A significant leak should become visible and manageable there rather than first appearing as staining in a principal room.

## One bounded vertical service zone

A vertical service zone of roughly **900 × 700 millimetres clear** connects the plant/wet territory to the family bathroom above.

It is intentionally bounded. It can carry the principal soil/vent stack, domestic water risers, selected hydronic runs and a segregated dry vertical route. It is not treated as a magical shaft into which any later technology will fit.

The shaft is accessible from dry/service territory at both levels. Its base can expose leakage and provide rodding or inspection. Branch joints are biased toward accessible ends rather than distributed behind finishes.

Large ventilation ducts were not simply assumed to fit later. When central MVHR became the environmental baseline, its ducts had to prove a separate route through the architecture. Spare volume is valuable only when the future use is credible enough to justify it.

## Dry services at the room edge

Ordinary power and data do not need the same geography as soil pipes or ventilation trunks.

Where useful, substantial removable skirting can carry a bounded dry route. At selected door surrounds, the route can rise behind removable architrave to switches or sockets at appropriate accessible heights. This turns existing architectural edges into understandable local distribution without turning every skirting board into trunking.

The route is documented. Heavy fixing zones and service/no-fix zones are distinguished. A future owner should be able to hang an ordinary picture without consulting a digital model, while more consequential work should not depend on drilling blindly.

## Water: isolation before containment

Water services use a hierarchy of defence rather than one heroic layer.

The main isolation is immediately accessible. Appliance valves sit near the appliance. Concealed pressurised runs are kept joint-minimised. Bathroom components that require future attention are biased toward dry-side access. Stack bases and drainage access are deliberate. Leak sensors may be useful, but they are not treated as architecture.

A washing machine or dishwasher is allowed to fail. The design task is to make that failure local, visible and isolatable enough that a common appliance fault does not become a building-fabric event.

The same logic explains why the principal en-suite disappeared. Avoiding a long high-consequence drainage route was more valuable than preserving a room label.

## External maintenance begins at the ground

The house has no permanent roof hatch.

This is not because the roof is considered inaccessible. It is because a hatch solves only one small part of the task: arrival at roof level. It does not provide edge protection, a working platform or material handling. The likely roof tasks are relatively rare and already require safe external working equipment.

Planned scaffold or tower positions therefore remain the principal roof and façade maintenance method. Enough ordinary ground must stay available around the building for that strategy to remain realistic.

The side service strip has the highest maintenance burden. It must support plant replacement, utility work, ventilation terminals, rainwater goods and access to the court drainage route. The opposite side remains useful for façade and gutter work. The rear garden edge preserves enough working line for upper windows, masonry, cornice and rainwater maintenance.

Landscape is consequently part of maintenance design. Permanent planters, stores, terraces or trees should not casually consume the only viable support position for future façade work.

## Courtyard access as a logistics problem

The inner court creates a special maintenance problem because external equipment cannot simply approach from the street.

The preferred logistics route runs from the rear garden through the garden-side room into the courtyard. Door geometry is coordinated to allow dismantled tower components, window parts, repair materials and waste to pass without removing permanent architecture.

The court itself is large enough to act as a plausible small-tower working area. This constrains later landscape design. A fountain or fixed planter may be beautiful, but it is not entitled to occupy the only useful working position unless another access method is demonstrated.

The early image of a courtyard fountain therefore has no authority merely because it was visually appealing.

## Maintenance does not require exposure

The service side can be legible without turning into an equipment dump.

On its exterior elevation, occupied windows and doors retain architectural priority. The plant replacement opening is treated as real secondary architecture. Downpipes are pushed toward return edges. Ventilation and extract terminals are grouped in service geography. Small utility penetrations remain visually subordinate.

No decorative screen is introduced merely to pretend the equipment does not exist. If an acoustic or weather screen becomes technically necessary, it must become a genuine maintainable architectural object with the airflow and access duties that follow.

The governing distinction is simple:

> **Accessible does not mean exposed. Concealed does not mean inaccessible.**

# 17. Water and environmental systems

## Form does the first work

The environmental strategy begins before equipment.

The courtyard produces shallow occupied bands: approximately 4.2 metres on the side wings and 3.4 metres on the front and rear. Many rooms can therefore take light and air from an outer façade and from the court or a second outer face. Circulation can receive daylight without becoming an internal core.

That geometry is useful, but it is not automatically benign. A real London site can expose the house to strong east or west summer sun. The deep courtyard can reduce sky view at lower level. Masonry mass helps only if it remains thermally coupled to the occupied space. Windows provide purge only if they can open securely and usefully under real noise, pollution and safety conditions.

The project therefore uses a passive-first hierarchy rather than declaring the form self-sufficient.

First: keep glazing disciplined.  
Then: control solar gain with orientation-specific external shading or appropriate glazing where evidence requires it.  
Then: preserve useful purge and cross ventilation through openable windows.  
Then: provide controlled whole-dwelling ventilation for indoor air quality and winter heat recovery.  
Then: add efficient heat.  
Mechanical cooling is not reserved as the default answer to architectural overheating.

## The courtyard as light court and purge reservoir

The court receives diffuse sky and gives the interior an orientation point. It is particularly valuable to the stair, upper gallery and landings, which would otherwise risk becoming artificially lit internal circulation.

It also creates opportunities for purge paths from outer façade to court and from court to outer façade. During favourable conditions, several rooms can ventilate across two aspects rather than depending on one opening.

The court is not treated as a source of winter solar heat. Its depth makes that proposition implausible. Nor should principal rooms depend on court-facing glass as their only daylight source.

This is a useful example of environmental honesty changing the architectural story without invalidating the architecture. The courtyard remains, but its value is described more precisely.

## No generic glazed rear wall

The rear of the house connects strongly to the garden, but that does not become permission for wall-to-wall glass.

Large undifferentiated glazing would flatten the room order, remove useful masonry piers, increase structural transfers and worsen summer solar risk on some orientations. Garden connection is therefore achieved through ranked openings whose size can respond to the actual site.

A south-facing garden may justify effective horizontal shading. East- or west-facing conditions may need vertical or operable external control. The correct answer cannot be selected until a real orientation, neighbour condition and thermal model exist.

The architectural rule is simply that solar control should be designed with the elevation rather than attached afterwards to rescue it.

## Ventilation: controlled air and openable windows

Central mechanical ventilation with heat recovery is the current whole-house baseline, subject to specialist design.

It was not selected as an alternative to opening windows. The two systems do different work.

MVHR supplies and extracts air in a controlled way, particularly useful in winter and in a high-performance envelope. Openable windows remain the principal architectural mechanism for purge and summer heat rejection. Bedrooms should retain credible secure night-purge conditions rather than relying on a token latch opening.

Wet rooms and the kitchen also keep source-capture duties explicit. Cooker extract is routed directly to an external service-side wall rather than pulled across the house or through a shared shaft. Bathroom, WC and utility extraction stay short where possible.

The ventilation network has been coordinated topologically, not designed. Duct sizes, pressure losses, silencers, commissioning, acoustics, condensate and terminal separation remain specialist tasks.

## Rainwater: fail outward

The roof is one of the clearest expressions of failure architecture in the house.

Four very-low-slope roof fields rise modestly toward the court and fall outward to the external perimeter. Their diagonal boundaries are watersheds rather than valleys. There is no primary inner-courtyard roof gutter and no internal roof outlet.

Rainwater is collected at inspectable outer edges and discharged through downpipes concentrated toward side and rear returns. A blocked or overflowing outer gutter should reveal itself outwardly rather than first as damp inside the thermal envelope.

The court therefore has to drain only its own open-to-sky area and local wall runoff, not the whole roof.

Its primary surface-water route uses an accessible collector toward the service side, followed by a short, straight route beneath service/circulation territory to an external inspection point. A separate higher-level emergency overflow provides an independent visible failure path. Courtyard thresholds are not asked to become accidental overflow weirs.

Final hydraulic sizes and site discharge remain unknown because there is no site. The building-side principle can nevertheless be fixed: keep the critical rainwater geography visible, external and consequence-aware where practical.

## Heating: replaceable plant, ordinary emitters

An air-to-water heat pump is the current working heat-source family.

The external unit sits provisionally in the service-side working zone beside the plant room, subject to real-site acoustic, boundary and airflow review. Hot-water and hydraulic equipment remain in the plant room, where replacement and water failure already have credible geography.

The baseline room emitter is a generously sized low-temperature radiator or other passive emitter rather than whole-house underfloor heating. The reasoning is not nostalgic. Visible emitters keep valves and joints accessible and avoid distributing active wet services throughout principal-room floors merely because a heat pump prefers lower water temperatures.

If room-by-room heat-loss design shows that passive emitters become architecturally disproportionate, a bounded alternative such as joint-free ground-floor underfloor loops in a non-structural layer can be tested. The project does not need to select buried infrastructure before a calculation creates the need.

Exact heat loss, flow temperatures, emitter sizes, hot-water demand, heat-pump duty and acoustics remain unproved.

## Renewable electricity without sacrificing maintainability

The low-slope roof reserves approximately **50 square metres of PV-capable field** as a forward design control.

The array has no fixed orientation because the site is unknown. The reserve exists to ensure that the roof geometry, drainage and maintenance strategy are not hostile to a likely contemporary renewable-electricity requirement.

Panels must remain subordinate to the water and access logic. Gutters, overflows, roof penetrations and working routes must stay reachable. Inverters, isolators and other active electrical components belong in accessible service geography rather than beneath modules where avoidable.

A battery is not selected. It would introduce another replacement, ventilation, fire and cost obligation that has not yet earned permanent space.

## What environmental coordination has not proved

The Reference House does not yet possess a completed daylight model, dynamic overheating analysis, final energy calculation, as-built airtightness result, room-by-room heat loss, detailed ventilation design or site-specific drainage design.

Its environmental maturity lies one level earlier: the architecture has been arranged so those analyses can test explicit choices rather than discover that the plan has left no credible answer.

A real project may still force change. That is the point of the next evidence stage.

# 18. Future maintenance scenarios

## Draw the intervention, not only the finished state

A maintainable building should be judged by what happens when something changes.

The Reference House therefore treats future interventions as design scenarios. They are not predictions and they are not demonstrations that the proposed details have been physically proved. Their purpose is to expose the extent of collateral work implied by the current architecture.

The useful unit is the **intervention boundary**: what must be isolated, opened, removed, protected, transported and reinstated to complete one foreseeable task?

A good scenario keeps that boundary proportionate to the failed or changed component.

## Scenario 1 — replace major plant

A heat-pump hydraulic component, hot-water cylinder or ventilation unit reaches the end of its useful life.

The intended sequence is straightforward:

1. identify the equipment through the physical service record;
2. isolate water and electrical supplies within the plant room;
3. work in front of the equipment without entering a principal room;
4. disconnect accessible connections;
5. remove the failed unit through the plant room's external replacement opening;
6. bring the new unit back by the same side route;
7. reconnect, commission and update the physical/digital record.

The architectural intervention is concentrated in a room already intended for work. No kitchen cabinetry, bathroom finishes, principal floor or masonry opening should need to be demolished merely to replace plant.

The scenario will fail if product-level packing leaves insufficient working space or if the replacement opening proves smaller than actual equipment. Those are explicit specialist-review questions, not assumptions hidden by the diagram.

## Scenario 2 — replace a window frame

A window remains shorter-lived than the masonry around it.

The intended assembly is:

**permanent masonry opening → deliberate fixing/tolerance zone → replaceable frame → removable internal trim**.

Routine sash, glazing or hardware work should occur from the room side where the selected product permits it. Full-frame replacement may use external scaffold or tower access.

The intervention should expose fixings and seals without cutting back large areas of plaster. The masonry reveal, lintel and sill remain. The new frame returns into the same long-lived opening, with adjustment accommodated at the designed interface rather than by damaging the wall until the product fits.

The final jamb, head and sill build-up still requires a full-scale mock-up. This scenario describes the intended life separation, not a completed weathering detail.

## Scenario 3 — washing-machine or dishwasher leak

An appliance hose fails.

The house does not attempt to make such failure impossible. Instead:

- the appliance sits in high-service geography;
- its local isolation is accessible;
- water should become visible locally;
- the floor and adjacent interfaces should tolerate enough wetting for the failure to be discovered and isolated;
- service joints are not buried behind unrelated permanent finishes;
- repair should not require opening the structural floor.

A sensor may shorten detection time, but the scenario must remain intelligible if the sensor, battery, app or network fails.

The same principle applies at the plant room and riser base. Electronics can assist failure architecture; they do not replace it.

## Scenario 4 — bathroom valve or concealed component fails

A shower control, cistern component or valve requires replacement.

The selected bathroom places maintainable components toward the dry gallery side where possible. The waterproofed room face should remain substantially intact. Access occurs from a real joinery or panel field on the dry side rather than through a small tiled hatch cut into the wet boundary.

The intervention boundary therefore aims to stop at the service wall.

Not every pipe can remain exposed, and final runs through the wet side may still be concealed. Those runs should be short and joint-minimised. The task is not universal access; it is to put the parts most likely to need work where work can occur without destroying the waterproof room.

## Scenario 5 — add a new dry room service

A later household needs another data cable, control cable or electrical circuit in a principal room.

The first question is whether the existing room-edge route can carry it. If so, local skirting is removed, the new cable is pulled through the bounded dry route, and a declared vertical rise carries it to the required accessory or control point. The service record is updated and the architectural joinery is reinstated.

The route may not fit every future system. A large duct, soil pipe or unknown industrial technology is not entitled to use it simply because future change was anticipated. When the new requirement exceeds the prepared route, the building is allowed to require a more substantial intervention.

Future-proofing is strongest when it remains bounded.

## Scenario 6 — roof gutter or waterproofing repair

A perimeter gutter blocks or a local roof/waterproofing condition fails.

The primary access method is external scaffold or tower. The worker reaches an outer collection edge that is visible and replaceable rather than a concealed box gutter buried at the centre of the house. Overflow behaviour is intended to reveal blockage outwardly.

Because the roof falls away from the court, routine roof drainage work does not require intervention in an inner courtyard gutter or internal downpipe.

The absence of a permanent roof hatch is tested by this scenario. If ordinary external access becomes disproportionate once real products, PV and site geometry are known, the decision can be reopened. Until then a hatch would add a permanent roof penetration without solving the full safety task.

## Scenario 7 — work to a courtyard window or inner façade

A courtyard frame, seal or masonry condition needs replacement or repair.

Dismantled tower components move from the rear garden through the garden-side logistics opening into the court. The court provides a working position. The window or masonry is repaired from the tower and materials leave by the same route.

This scenario is why courtyard landscape cannot be designed independently of maintenance. A permanent central object must leave a credible alternative working position or justify another method.

The courtyard remains domestic space precisely because maintenance has been designed to be occasional rather than permanently visible.

## Scenario 8 — the heat source changes

The selected air-to-water heat pump is a present technical family, not an architectural identity.

A future heat source may have different dimensions, controls or external equipment. The house does not attempt to reserve every imaginable technology. It does preserve useful things that make change less destructive:

- a genuine equipment room;
- an external replacement route;
- accessible isolation and distribution;
- bounded service crossings;
- low-temperature hydronic distribution that may outlive one generation of plant;
- separation between active equipment and principal permanent fabric.

If the future system fits within those affordances, change remains local. If it does not, the building must be altered honestly. Ovid’s House is not a promise that all future technologies will plug in.

## Scenario 9 — an architectural layer moves while another remains

Not every future intervention is a service event.

A door may be renewed while its masonry opening remains. A skirting may be removed for work and replaced. A cornice may need to accommodate seasonal or long-term differential movement between wall and ceiling. A room may change use without its structural order changing at all.

These smaller cases are where selective permanence becomes ordinary rather than spectacular. The house persists not because nothing changes, but because change has been prevented from propagating unnecessarily.

## What the scenarios reveal

The scenarios expose several recurring conditions.

First, **maintenance access is not the same as disassembly**. A component can be reachable for service and still impossible to remove. Replacement routes must be tested separately.

Second, **local isolation matters as much as physical access**. A reachable pipe is not maintainable if half the house must be shut down to work on it.

Third, **external space is part of the building's maintenance system**. Side passages, garden working lines and courtyard logistics can matter as much as internal risers.

Fourth, **the building should retain conventional fallbacks**. If the experimental removable lining fails, direct plaster remains. If selective floor access is unnecessary, continuous floors remain. If a novel movement joint proves inferior, an excellent conventional junction can win.

Finally, **future change is not allowed to erase present architecture**. The house does not become a visible kit of parts in order to make every component replaceable. Most of the time it should simply feel like a solid, ordered house.

That is the intended meaning of permanence without stasis.

## Reference House evidence boundary

The Reference House is currently **M3 — Coordinated at virtual-design scope**.

Its plan, section, principal architectural order, structural concept, service topology, environmental hierarchy, maintenance geography, roof/drainage strategy and occupied-interface logic have been reconciled far enough to expose major internal conflicts. G-01 v0.1 has been applied after freeze and the house conforms at this architectural scope.

The next meaningful evidence does not come from adding another coordination chapter.

It comes from competent structural, fire/building-control, MEP/acoustic and building-physics review; from engineering the structural floor-edge condition; from physically testing the selected wall-ceiling movement problem; and eventually from a real site with real planning, utility, neighbour, drainage and environmental constraints.

Until those tests occur, the Reference House remains what it is intended to be:

> **a sufficiently concrete house to make Ovid’s House argue with itself.**
