# P03 Structural Floor Edge — Engineer Review Brief v0.1

**Status:** pre-engineering options-study brief  
**Prototype:** `P03`  
**Reference House status:** provisional coordination geometry; not construction information  
**Purpose:** determine whether HSA's floor-edge doctrine is best served by an ordinary certified restraint hanger, direct bearing with separately designed restraint, or a genuinely useful custom seated interface.

This brief is designed to invite attack, not endorsement.

The project has no preference for a custom connection surviving. The default outcome should be the simplest ordinary solution that satisfies the building's real structural and lifecycle requirements.

---

# 1. Decision required

Please compare three connection families for an engineered-timber upper floor supported by permanent masonry:

### A — certified restraint-type masonry hanger

The ordinary UK baseline. Use a current restraint hanger suitable for I-joists as the representative family; an SFHI/SFWHI-type product or equivalent is sufficient for the options study.

### B — direct/seated bearing + separate restraint

Gravity reaction is delivered through bearing on masonry, a suitable wall plate or another conventional bearing detail. Wall restraint, joist stability and diaphragm actions are deliberately resolved separately.

### C — engineered seat / ledge / shoe

A purpose-designed steel or similarly durable interface fixed to the permanent masonry. The joist bears on the interface and is captured/restrained separately.

This is the challenger, not the preferred answer.

The study should answer:

> **Does B or C deliver a material whole-life advantage over A after all structural and construction functions are counted?**

If not, recommend A and close the custom-seat line of inquiry.

---

# 2. Architectural doctrine being tested

The relevant HSA strategy is:

> **Decompose the floor-to-wall interface into gravity support, member stability, diaphragm action, wall restraint, movement allowance, fire/acoustic closure and finish; do not make one fixing perform every conceptual role merely because conventional detailing bundles them.**

This is **not** a requirement that the joist should float, slide, be demountable or avoid standard hardware.

The engineering question is whether decomposition produces a better connection or simply a clearer understanding of an ordinary one.

---

# 3. Reference House study geometry

The current whole-house coordination fixture is a two-storey masonry courtyard house with a provisional outer rectangle of **12.6 × 11.4 m** and a **3.6 × 3.6 m** open courtyard.

The resulting gross wing dimensions are approximately:

- north/south wing depth: **3.9 m**;
- east/west wing width: **4.5 m**.

For this options study, use **~3.9 m and ~4.5 m clear structural span cases as sensitivities**, not as final member spans.

The final structural grid, stair opening, trimming members, point loads, floor build-up and member centres remain unresolved. State any additional assumptions rather than treating the coordination fixture as a structural design.

---

# 4. Working floor assumptions

Current design-development baseline:

- engineered timber **I-joists** as the first comparison family;
- permanent masonry external and courtyard walls as primary support lines;
- structural deck provides primary diaphragm action;
- removable/changeable floor layers, if used, do **not** provide primary diaphragm action;
- routine services are kept out of the structural joist zone where practical;
- unavoidable structural/service crossings are designed explicitly;
- vulnerable joist ends should not be buried casually into masonry;
- fire, acoustic and finish closures at the floor edge are separate design obligations.

Metal-web joists remain a valid whole-floor comparator if they materially improve depth, vibration, construction or cost. Do not choose them merely to make the primary structure a general service zone.

---

# 5. Regulatory / conventional baseline

The options study should use current UK/England requirements and the engineer's normal design basis.

The project has identified the following current context for orientation only:

- Approved Document A continues to recognise bearing and restraint-type joist-hanger routes as distinct means of providing floor/wall restraint in defined domestic conditions;
- current NHBC upper-floor guidance includes specific I-joist hanger conditions, including bearing and top-flange restraint provisions;
- current proprietary restraint hangers for I-joists provide published vertical/lateral capacity and installation limits.

These references are **not** a substitute for the engineer selecting the applicable Eurocodes, UK National Annexes, product certification, masonry properties and project-specific load cases.

# 7. Required engineering questions

## 7.1 Gravity support

For each option:

- what carries the joist reaction?
- what minimum bearing/contact geometry is required?
- what masonry/block strength and local bearing assumptions control?
- is any local spreader, stiffener, web reinforcement or bearing block required?
- what construction tolerance can be accepted without packers or site improvisation?

## 7.2 I-joist local stability

Identify:

- top-flange restraint requirement;
- web stiffener/bearing reinforcement requirement;
- end blocking requirement;
- lateral-torsional/roll restraint during construction and in service;
- any manufacturer-specific conditions that make a superficially simple seat non-compliant with the joist system.

The custom option must not obtain apparent simplicity by quietly discarding proprietary I-joist end requirements.

## 7.3 Wall restraint

Show separately:

- how the masonry wall is restrained by the floor;
- direction of restraint;
- spacing/frequency of restraint points;
- whether the proposed detail is consistent with the applicable Approved Document / PD 6697 / BS EN 845 route or requires project-specific justification;
- what happens where the floor runs parallel rather than perpendicular to a wall.

Do not equate 'joist supported' with 'wall restrained'.

## 7.4 Diaphragm action

State:

- what provides the horizontal diaphragm;
- how deck forces are collected at the wall edge;
- how those actions transfer into the masonry/other stabilising system;
- whether a more detachable joist-end detail weakens this path;
- what blocking/rim member/collector is required.

The removable finish/platform layer must not be used to conceal a missing primary diaphragm detail.

## 7.5 Movement

Identify the actual movements worth accommodating:

- timber moisture movement;
- long-term joist deflection;
- construction tolerance;
- masonry movement;
- floor vibration;
- finish/ceiling differential movement.

Then state **where each movement should be accommodated**.

Please challenge explicitly whether any deliberate sliding freedom belongs at the structural bearing at all. If finish-level slip/movement joints solve the real problem more cleanly, say so.

## 7.6 Fire and acoustic edge

The study need not provide final fire/acoustic certification, but it must expose the geometry those disciplines inherit:

- cavity/void continuity at the floor edge;
- likely fire stopping/protection zone;
- steel exposure/protection consequences;
- acoustic flanking route through ledges/rails/hangers;
- interaction with independent/removable wall lining;
- whether inspection/removal of architectural layers compromises required closure.

## 7.7 Moisture / durability / corrosion

Identify:

- timber-to-masonry moisture risks;
- durability class/coating assumptions for metalwork;
- inaccessible corrosion traps;
- whether a custom ledge creates a water/dust shelf or uninspectable crevice;
- expected life of the interface relative to the wall and joists.

## 7.8 Construction sequence and tolerance

For each option show, at minimum:

1. masonry state when connection is installed;
2. whether joists require propping;
3. when floor deck can be installed;
4. when wall restraint becomes active;
5. what geometry controls floor level;
6. how realistic masonry/joist tolerances are absorbed;
7. what error triggers remediation rather than hidden packing.

A concept that is elegant only after perfect set-out should score badly.

## 7.9 Inspection and repair

Separate three claims:

### Inspectable
Can the support/end condition be surveyed without destructive masonry work?

### Locally repairable
Can connector, blocking, restraint or edge closure be repaired/replaced without demolishing the primary wall?

### Primary-joist replaceable
Can an entire joist actually be withdrawn once deck, partitions, ceiling, services, blocking and stair/opening geometry exist?

Do not award C a lifecycle advantage for theoretical joist demountability unless the whole assembly provides a credible withdrawal path.

# 8. Option-specific challenge

## Option A — certified restraint hanger

Treat this as the baseline to beat, not a straw man.

Please identify:

- representative current product family;
- suitable I-joist depth/width range;
- bearing/end-gap conditions;
- published vertical/lateral capacities relevant to the comparison;
- required masonry condition;
- connector/strap/nailing requirements;
- realistic inspection and future-repair limitations.

Do not optimise the unconventional options while leaving A crudely specified.

## Option B — direct bearing + separate restraint

Please determine whether this can be achieved without creating worse problems through:

- timber embedded into masonry;
- large wall-plate fixing demands;
- moisture vulnerability;
- inaccessible joist ends;
- awkward acoustic/fire closure;
- excessive bearing geometry.

If a conventional bearing detail is viable, identify what it genuinely improves over A.

## Option C — engineered seat / ledge / shoe

Assume this option has **no right to exist** until it demonstrates one.

Please identify:

- vertical reactions and anchor-group actions;
- eccentricity/prying effects;
- masonry substrate requirements;
- number/spacing of permanent anchors;
- steel size/depth implications;
- fire/corrosion protection burden;
- acoustic bridge consequences;
- fabrication and installation tolerances;
- inspection access;
- replacement/remanufacture route;
- whether the same benefits can be obtained by A plus better architectural/finish interfaces.

Kill C if its only advantage is conceptual neatness.

---

# 9. Comparison criteria

Safety/compliance functions are gates, not weighted preferences.

For options that clear those gates, compare:

| Criterion | Evidence requested |
|---|---|
| structural legibility | load-path sketch + calculation basis |
| conventionality | recognised product/detail route and installer familiarity |
| masonry intervention | permanent fixings, pockets, bearing geometry |
| tolerance robustness | stated installation tolerance and remediation route |
| inspectability | what can actually be seen/accessed later |
| local repairability | replaceable connector/edge components |
| whole-joist replacement reality | actual withdrawal constraints |
| fire/acoustic integration | inherited closure/flanking geometry |
| moisture/corrosion | durability assumptions and inaccessible risks |
| construction sequence | temporary stability / propping / deck sequence |
| cost/fabrication burden | relative order-of-magnitude, not QS precision |
| future reproducibility | standard vs bespoke components/fabrication data |

The non-standard option should win **materially**, not by a narrow aesthetic preference.

# 10. Required outputs

Please return a compact engineer's options note containing:

1. **design basis / assumptions**;
2. **representative floor span/member assumptions** for ~3.9 m and ~4.5 m sensitivity cases;
3. **A/B/C load-path sketches**;
4. completed **function matrix**;
5. capacity/feasibility calculations sufficient to distinguish viable from non-viable options;
6. key product/system constraints for A;
7. key structural geometry and anchor demands for C;
8. fire/acoustic/building-physics items requiring other disciplines;
9. ranked recommendation;
10. explicit **kill reasons** for any rejected option;
11. what exact geometry/data would be needed for final design;
12. whether a 1:1 P03 mock-up would answer a meaningful question after engineering.

The preferred outcome may be:

> **Use an ordinary certified hanger, and spend the project's innovation budget on the finish/service interfaces around it.**

That is a successful result.

---

# 11. P03 physical prototype gate

Do **not** build a custom structural-edge mock-up merely because this programme contains `P03`.

Proceed to a 1:1 structural edge only when the engineer has produced calculable geometry for at least the baseline and one challenger and the mock-up can answer a question that drawings/calculations cannot, such as:

- tolerance/assembly sequence;
- inspectability;
- interaction with removable lining/ceiling;
- realistic local repair procedure;
- buildability of fire/acoustic closure geometry.

If Option A wins cleanly and no physical uncertainty remains at the structural connector itself, P03 may become an **interface mock-up around a standard hanger**, not a custom-connector experiment.

---

# 12. Evidence boundary

This options study is not final structural design and should not be presented as such.

Final construction still requires project-specific design based on actual:

- structural grid and spans;
- actions/load combinations;
- joist product and manufacturer requirements;
- masonry/block properties;
- deck/diaphragm system;
- openings/trimmers/point loads;
- robustness/stability strategy;
- fire/acoustic construction;
- foundations and overall structural scheme.

The value of this study is to kill weak connection concepts early and select a defensible family for detailed coordination.

# 14. Decision rule

> **Ordinary wins by default. The custom seat survives only if competent engineering shows a significant benefit that cannot be obtained more simply by a standard structural connection plus better-designed architectural interfaces.**
