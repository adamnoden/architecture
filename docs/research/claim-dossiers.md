# House Design Doctrine — Claim Dossiers v0.2

**Status:** research/editorial working paper  
**Purpose:** test the principal claims that distinguish the proposed publication before prose rewrite.  
**Source doctrine:** `house-design-doctrine-v7(1).md`

## Evidence labels used here

- **Established professional principle** — clearly represented in standards, regulation, institutional guidance or mature professional practice.
- **Established architectural lineage** — recognisable architectural theory or built precedent, but not necessarily a prescriptive standard.
- **Supported design strategy** — credible evidence/precedent exists, but applicability depends strongly on building type and context.
- **Project hypothesis** — plausible and worth testing, but insufficient evidence to present as generally preferable.
- **Reference implementation** — one architectural response to the doctrine, not part of the doctrine itself.

# Dossier 1 — Selective permanence, lifespan hierarchy and layer separation

## V7 proposition

The house should be understood as a long-lived architectural platform. Elements change at materially different rates; shorter-lived components should not be materially entangled with longer-lived fabric. Adaptability should be concentrated where change is valuable rather than making the whole building generically flexible.

## Finding

**Evidence status: established architectural lineage + established professional principle.**

This is not a novel premise in isolation. It sits squarely within several mature bodies of thought:

1. **Open Building / Habraken.** Separation of durable support from changeable infill is foundational to Open Building. Contemporary Open Building literature explicitly describes stability and change as simultaneous realities that architecture must manage.
2. **Duffy / Brand building layers.** Building elements are understood as layers with differing timescales of change. Later adaptability research describes separation of these layers as allowing one layer to be maintained or replaced without compromising adjacent layers.
3. **Service-life planning.** ISO 15686 establishes service-life planning as a systematic whole-life process including maintenance, refurbishment and replacement of components and building services.
4. **Design for disassembly/adaptability.** ISO 20887 provides principles and strategies for integrating disassembly and adaptability into design.
5. **Long life, loose fit, low energy.** Alex Gordon's RIBA-era formulation gives the project a particularly relevant British lineage.

The useful professional phrase **lifetime compatibility** appears in adaptability literature for the principle that rapid-cycling components should be replaceable without damaging slower-cycling ones.

## What the book can safely claim

The publication can confidently state that:

- buildings contain components with materially different service lives and rates of change;
- coupling fast-changing layers to slow-changing layers makes maintenance and adaptation more destructive;
- access, mechanical connections and layer separation are recognised enablers of adaptability and disassembly;
- service-life planning is an established professional discipline rather than an eccentric concern.

## What it should not claim

It should **not** imply that the support/infill distinction, building layers, replaceability or design-for-disassembly originate here.

It should also resist putting speculative lifespan numbers on domestic elements unless supported and qualified. The useful proposition is relative ordering and compatibility, not a fake precision that a masonry wall lasts X years and a service Y years.

## Where the doctrine becomes more distinctive

The stronger contribution is **selective permanence**:

> Change should be given deliberate places to occur so that architecture can afford to remain permanent elsewhere.

This differs in emphasis from maximal flexibility. The proposal values enduring rooms, axes, stairs, openings and architectural character, while demanding high adaptability from services, interfaces, fit-out and selected secondary layers.

That deserves to become a central argument of the publication rather than a paragraph buried in the adaptability chapter.

## Editorial decision

Promote to **Part I — Proposition** and make it one of the intellectual foundations of the book.

Suggested chapter sequence:

1. Buildings change at different rates.
2. Coupling consumes slower layers.
3. Separation permits renewal.
4. Selective permanence: why not everything should be flexible.
5. The architectural platform.

## Drawing requirement

A publication-quality **pace/lifespan section** through the reference house: not a generic Brand diagram, but the actual architectural section coloured or hatched by rate of change, with interfaces explicitly marked.

# Dossier 2 — Maintenance geography

## V7 proposition

Maintenance should have an architectural plan of its own. Utility entry, plant, risers, wet rooms, horizontal service spines, undercroft, roof access and external maintenance routes should form coherent and predictable paths with genuine working and replacement space.

## Finding

**Evidence status: established professional principle; proposed synthesis/terminology.**

The underlying proposition is extremely well supported, although the exact phrase needs careful handling.

### Existing professional practice

The profession already uses documents such as:

- **Access & Maintenance Strategy**;
- **plant replacement strategy**;
- maintenance-access drawings and routes;
- facilities-management maintainability reviews.

UK CDM guidance explicitly places responsibility on designers to consider people who will maintain, repair and clean a building, not just those who construct it. Institutional building-services guides require defined personnel and plant routes and major-plant replacement routes.

CIBSE Guide M makes maintenance engineering and management an explicit design concern. Singapore's Building and Construction Authority has gone further with a formal **Design for Maintainability (DfM)** framework. BCA defines DfM as integrating maintenance needs into design so future maintenance can be efficient, safe and economical. Its 2026 study of four developments found that many DfM measures produced substantial reductions in maintenance labour and relatively short paybacks, although those figures are context-specific and must not be generalised to houses.

### Architectural lineage

Kahn's distinction between **served and servant space** provides an architectural ancestor: technical and supporting spaces can possess their own spatial order rather than being leftovers in the occupied plan. The Salk Institute and laboratory work expand this into sectional service space.

### Terminology caution

The phrase is not entirely unprecedented. Ignaz Strebel's 2011 paper is titled *The living building: towards a geography of maintenance work*. It studies the actual routines and spatial practices of maintenance workers rather than proposing a design doctrine.

Our proposed use is narrower and more architectural:

> **Maintenance geography:** the deliberate spatial organisation of maintenance-intensive systems, working spaces, access routes and replacement paths into a coherent topology established during design.

The book may still use this term, but should acknowledge the adjacent literature and avoid claiming to have coined the general idea of a “geography of maintenance”.

## What the book can safely claim

- Maintainability is materially affected by design decisions made before handover.
- Reaching a component is not enough; safe working clearance, isolation, tools, component withdrawal and replacement routes matter.
- Major plant replacement and maintenance access are recognised design-stage subjects.
- Concentrating services and maintenance-intensive functions can reduce disruption and make service logic more legible.

## Where the doctrine becomes more distinctive

Most guidance treats maintainability **component by component**: can I reach the fan, clean the façade, remove the pump?

V7's stronger move is to treat maintenance as **whole-building spatial order**:

- where a trade enters;
- how they move through the building;
- where technical work concentrates;
- which occupied spaces are protected from maintenance traffic;
- how plant, risers, wet rooms, roof and undercroft relate in plan and section.

That is sufficiently distinctive to deserve a named concept even if the words themselves are not unprecedented.

## Editorial decision

Promote to a major chapter in **Part II — Architecture of the Platform**.

Do **not** bury it inside “Service architecture”. It is broader than MEP routing and connects architectural planning, safety, access, logistics and stewardship.

## Drawing requirement

A pair of architectural drawings:

1. **Maintenance geography plan** — utility entry → plant → riser → wet/service rooms → roof/undercroft, including personnel and plant-removal routes.
2. **Maintenance geography section** — vertical relation of undercroft, occupied floors, service zones, riser, attic and roof.

These should look like architectural circulation drawings, not MEP schematics.

- CIBSE Guide M (2023). https://cibse.org/knowledge-research/knowledge-portal/guide-m-maintenance-engineering-and-management-2023/
- Building and Construction Authority, Design for Maintainability. https://www1.bca.gov.sg/growth-and-transformation/facilities-management/design-for-maintainability/

# Dossier 3 — Accessible undercroft / service interstitial space

## V7 proposition

The first house should investigate a raised ground floor with an accessible undercroft and a deeper service trench along principal wet/service zones, so that ground-floor services remain inspectable and replaceable rather than being buried under a slab.

## Finding

**Evidence status: supported design strategy in high-service buildings; domestic application remains a project hypothesis/reference implementation.**

This idea has a strong precedent, but mostly in building types whose service density and rate of change are much greater than a house.

### Established precedent

Hospitals and laboratories have a long history of **interstitial service floors**: walkable spaces between occupied floors dedicated to mechanical/electrical distribution and maintenance. Research and government studies have found plausible life-cycle benefits through easier maintenance, alteration and future rearrangement despite higher first cost.

The Salk Institute is an architectural precedent for using substantial sectional depth to give building services an independent spatial order.

### Why this does not automatically validate a domestic undercroft

Hospitals and laboratories have:

- very high service density;
- frequent reconfiguration;
- expensive disruption when rooms close;
- professional facilities-management staff;
- much larger construction budgets.

A five-bedroom house has none of those to the same degree. Therefore “interstitial floors work in hospitals” is evidence that the concept is technically serious, **not evidence that a walkable domestic undercroft is cost-effective**.

### Moisture warning

The undercroft is one of the most technically dangerous ideas in v7 if its environmental status is vague.

UK guidance on suspended ground floors treats the subfloor void as a moisture-sensitive space requiring deliberate ventilation. Historic England notes that cross-ventilation protects timber but also causes heat loss. Norwegian and North American building-science literature similarly show that crawlspace moisture behaviour depends strongly on climate and on whether the space is ventilated externally or incorporated within the conditioned enclosure.

The publication should therefore insist on a binary design decision:

- **external/ventilated undercroft**, with the occupied floor forming the thermal/air boundary; or
- **conditioned/semi-conditioned service basement**, with thermal, air and ground-moisture control at the perimeter/ground.

An accidental hybrid should be prohibited.

### Fire and acoustic warning

Any large continuous service void is a potential concealed route for smoke, flame, sound, pests and odour. UK Approved Document B explicitly treats concealed cavities as routes for hidden fire spread and requires cavity subdivision/closure in relevant locations.

V7 already anticipates this with service compartmentation. That should be retained as a non-negotiable consequence of any undercroft or raised floor.

## What the book can safely claim

- Full interstitial service space is a proven strategy in service-intensive buildings.
- Dedicated service space can improve access, maintenance and later alteration.
- The concept carries first-cost, sectional-height, fire, acoustic, moisture and spatial penalties.
- A domestic version must earn its floor area and construction complexity through an options appraisal.

## What v7 currently overstates

The undercroft appears too close to a preferred default. At publication level it should become:

> **Pattern / Reference implementation: Service undercroft** — a high-access horizontal service zone appropriate where the project can justify its depth, cost and environmental complexity.

The doctrine itself requires **accessible, replaceable service distribution**. It does not require an undercroft.

## Design alternatives that must be compared

Before the reference house adopts an undercroft, compare at least:

1. conventional suspended floor with carefully placed inspection zones;
2. shallow raised service floor;
3. corridor-only service floor/spine;
4. deep local service trench;
5. conditioned crawlspace/mini-basement;
6. full accessible undercroft.

The comparison should include construction cost, lost height/volume, thermal envelope complexity, ground moisture, fire compartmentation, acoustic flanking, pest risk, service density, maintenance frequency and removal logistics.

## Drawing requirement

A comparative sectional plate showing the six strategies above at the same scale. This would be more convincing than presenting the undercroft in isolation.

# Dossier 4 — Removable floor systems

## V7 proposition

Finished floor panels/cassettes could form an intentionally removable access layer, potentially using timber or stone finishes, resilient seating and durable edge frames while still feeling like a permanent domestic floor.

## Finding

**Evidence status: established technology at commercial scale; domestic architectural expression is a project hypothesis.**

### What is established

Raised access flooring is mature construction technology. BS EN 12825 specifically covers modular raised access floors whose purpose includes **full access to services in the plenum**. UK industry specifications additionally deal with load, deflection, durability, fire, acoustics and installation.

So the proposition “a floor can be modular, removable, structurally adequate and provide service access” is not speculative.

### What is not established by that precedent

A commercial 600 × 600 mm pedestal floor is not the desired domestic experience.

The doctrine proposes something much harder:

- visually substantial timber/stone flooring;
- no office-floor character;
- no bounce, clicking, rattle or lipping;
- good impact and airborne acoustic performance;
- compatibility with resilient floor/ceiling separation;
- safe repeated lifting;
- clean edges after decades of use;
- fire and smoke compartmentation beneath it;
- selective rather than indiscriminate access.

Those requirements turn an established service technology into an architectural-detail research problem.

### Acoustic warning

Published testing of access floors confirms that acoustic performance depends on panels, finishes, pedestal damping and the ceiling/floor system. V7 is right to treat resilient seating and flanking paths as first-order design issues rather than assuming removable panels can simply be dropped into an acoustic floor.

### Fire warning

Raised floors create concealed cavities. Current UK guidance and industry fire-stopping guidance emphasise continuity of compartment lines beneath raised floors. Any domestic cassette system must be designed with the fire strategy rather than detailed later.

### Editorial decision

Keep the idea, but demote **whole-room removable floors** from preferred implementation until prototyping demonstrates that they outperform simpler access strategies.

The pattern catalogue should distinguish:

1. **service-access panel** — local, infrequent access;
2. **service band / border** — continuous accessible strip around a room or corridor;
3. **raised access zone** — larger service-rich area;
4. **full removable floor field** — only where justified.

My current expectation is that the eventual house will use **more of (1)–(3) and less of (4)** than v7 presently implies.

## Prototype brief

A full-scale room mock-up should test:

- concentrated and distributed load;
- static deflection and long-term creep;
- vibration/bounce;
- walking noise and impact sound;
- panel edge wear and lipping;
- rattle after repeated removal cycles;
- lifting tools and safe handling;
- dust ingress;
- accidental liquid spill behaviour;
- floor finish repair/refinishing;
- service-compartment/fire-barrier continuity;
- replacement of one damaged cassette without disturbing neighbours.

## Drawing requirement

One exploded axonometric showing **finish / cassette / resilient bearing / support / service void / compartment barrier / ceiling** and a second plate showing the four access intensities above.

# Dossier 5 — Passive water containment and visible failure

## V7 proposition

Pressurised water routes and water-connected appliances should assume eventual leakage. Where practical, failures should be passively contained and directed to a safe visible location rather than relying solely on sensors or discovering a hidden leak after permanent fabric is damaged.

## Finding

**Evidence status: strongly established principle; v7's proposed house-wide implementation is more ambitious than normal UK domestic practice.**

This dossier produced one of the strongest external validations of the doctrine.

### Norwegian regulation is extremely close to the v7 philosophy

Norway's TEK17 requires indoor water installations to be designed for future maintenance and easy replacement. It also states that leaks should be **easy to detect and should not damage other installations or building elements**.

Official guidance lists accepted water-damage-safe methods including:

- exposed pipes in waterproof/drained rooms;
- pipes in cabinets or casings where leaks become visible and are stopped;
- pipe-in-pipe systems for concealed runs, terminating at a waterproof manifold cabinet with drainage.

Its wet-room guidance likewise requires leakage from concealed installations to be made visible and, for concealed cisterns, drained into the room so the leak becomes apparent.

That is strikingly close to v7's combination of replaceability, secondary containment and visible failure.

### Pipe-in-pipe provides a mature precedent

Scandinavian domestic systems use a replaceable pressurised inner pipe inside a protective conduit. A leak can be led back toward a manifold cabinet or safe visible discharge instead of entering the building fabric. This demonstrates that the doctrine is not demanding an impossible or purely bespoke concept.

### UK precedent for visible discharge

Approved Document G requires safety discharge from an unvented hot-water system to pass through a tundish and explicitly says any discharge should be visible at the tundish. This is a narrower safety case, but it confirms the design principle that an abnormal water event can be deliberately made visually legible rather than concealed.

### UK loss context

Escape of water remains a major insurance risk, and insurers publish dedicated loss-prevention guidance. This justifies treating water-failure architecture as a serious design subject rather than a gadget problem.

## Where v7 currently needs refinement

The doctrine should not leap from the sound principle “water failures should be visible and harmless” to “all horizontal water routes require fabricated metal troughs”. That is one possible pattern, not the doctrine.

A better hierarchy is:

1. **Avoid vulnerability** — minimise concealed joints and unnecessary pressurised runs.
2. **Make runs accessible/replaceable** — manifolded point-to-point distribution where appropriate.
3. **Contain credible concealed leakage** — pipe-in-pipe, waterproof service cabinet, tray, trough or other secondary path according to risk.
4. **Drain to a safe place** — preferably by gravity where this is genuinely robust.
5. **Make abnormal discharge visible** — visible outlet, tell-tale or wet-room discharge point.
6. **Isolate quickly** — simple local/manual isolation; automatic shutoff may supplement rather than replace passive design.

This hierarchy is much more defensible than prescribing one containment construction everywhere.

## Terminology opportunity

The publication should consider adopting the Scandinavian idea **water-damage-safe installation** as a useful generic category, then explain the house's specific patterns beneath it.

“Visible failure” remains useful as the wider architectural principle because it applies beyond plumbing to roof overflows, drainage, condensate and potentially other systems.

## Editorial decision

Promote **visible and harmless failure** to governing doctrine.

Demote specific metal troughs and external tell-tales into the pattern catalogue, alongside pipe-in-pipe and wet-service-room containment as alternative implementations.

## Drawing requirement

A water-failure sequence drawing:

**normal operation → inner failure → secondary path → visible indication → isolation → access → component replacement**, with no wetting of permanent fabric.

A second plate should compare several containment patterns rather than imply one universal system.

# Cross-dossier conclusions

## 1. The doctrine is much less eccentric than its form currently makes it look

Several apparently unusual propositions in v7 are recognisable developments of existing professional principles:

- service-life planning;
- layer separation;
- design for adaptability/disassembly;
- design for maintainability;
- access and plant replacement strategies;
- interstitial service space;
- raised access flooring;
- water-damage-safe plumbing.

The publication should make these lineages explicit. Doing so will make the more original synthesis easier for architects to see.

## 2. The most defensible originality claim is synthesis, not invention

A cautious formulation for later development:

> The work brings together service-life planning, maintainability, failure-tolerant detailing and architectural permanence at the scale of the private house, treating them not as separate technical concerns but as one spatial and tectonic system.

That is more credible than claiming to invent support/infill, accessible floors or leak containment.

## 3. Two proposed concepts deserve special development

### Selective permanence

A direct counterweight to generic flexibility. Permanent architecture is not the problem; **badly located change is**.

### Maintenance geography

A whole-building spatial concept that expands ordinary access-and-maintenance strategy from a collection of local clearances into a planned topology of routes, working spaces, service concentrations and replacement paths.

Neither term should be marketed as an unsupported historical first. They should be defined precisely and then allowed to earn their usefulness.

## 4. Three v7 implementations should be demoted pending proof

- full accessible domestic undercroft;
- widespread removable floor cassettes;
- continuous metal secondary-containment troughs for all water routes.

All three remain valuable **patterns/hypotheses**. None should currently carry the rhetorical weight of doctrine.

## 5. One v7 implementation becomes substantially stronger after research

The manifold / accessible / replaceable / leak-visible water strategy has a strong Scandinavian regulatory and technical precedent. The publication should study Norwegian water-damage-safe installations properly rather than designing the whole system from first principles.

# Immediate editorial changes implied by these dossiers

1. Introduce **lifetime compatibility** as established vocabulary adjacent to the book's own “selective permanence”.
2. Give **maintenance geography** a formal definition and a literature note acknowledging Strebel's “geography of maintenance work”.
3. Present **Access & Maintenance Strategy** and **plant replacement strategy** as professional implementation documents derived from the doctrine.
4. Reclassify the **undercroft** as a reference-house options decision.
5. Reclassify **removable floor cassettes** as a graded family of access-floor patterns, from local panels to full fields.
6. Replace “secondary containment everywhere” with a **water-damage-safe hierarchy**, of which troughs are one possible pattern.
7. Add a Norwegian domestic plumbing precedent study to the eventual book.
8. Add an interstitial-service-space precedent study (Salk / hospital/laboratory lineage) but explicitly discuss the danger of importing institutional complexity into a house.
