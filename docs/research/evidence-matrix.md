# House Design Doctrine — Evidence Matrix v0.3

**Status:** editorial/research control document  
**Purpose:** distinguish what the proposed publication can state as established knowledge, what it advances as an architectural position, and what remains a project hypothesis. This is deliberately stricter than v7.

## Evidence classes

- **A — Established professional basis:** standards, regulation or mature professional guidance directly supports the proposition.
- **B — Established architectural lineage:** recognised theory and/or serious built precedent supports the proposition, but it remains an architectural strategy rather than a universal rule.
- **C — Authorial synthesis / design position:** defensible architectural proposition assembled from established ideas; should be argued, not presented as scientific fact.
- **D — Project hypothesis:** plausible implementation needing modelling, engineering, cost review or prototyping before recommendation.

## P1 — Build for time

### Working public principle
**Design the house as a building that will be maintained, repaired, altered and renewed repeatedly, not as an object frozen at practical completion.**

### Evidence status
**A + B. Very strong.**

### Established basis
- ISO 15686 establishes service-life planning as a systematic building-life-cycle discipline.
- RICS whole-life carbon methodology explicitly includes maintenance, repair, replacement and refurbishment in the use stage.
- CIBSE Guide M treats full-life maintenance and satisfactory long-term operation as design concerns.
- RIBA Stage 7 explicitly lasts for the life of the building and covers use, operation, maintenance and post-occupancy evaluation.

### Architectural lineage
Alex Gordon's “long life, loose fit, low energy”; Habraken/Open Building; Duffy/Brand building layers.

### Distinctive contribution
Not “buildings have lifecycles”; that is established. The book's contribution is to make **anticipated renewal a generator of domestic architectural form and detail**.

### Main counterargument
Designing too heavily for unknown future change can waste material, money and space today.

### Required response
Distinguish **cheap future capacity** from speculative over-provision. Require an options/value test for spare capacity and future loads.

### Editorial consequence for v7
Retain strongly. Replace vague century-scale rhetoric with service-life planning vocabulary where technical precision matters.

### Core sources
- ISO 15686-1: https://www.iso.org/standard/45798.html
- RICS WLCA: https://www.rics.org/profession-standards/rics-standards-and-guidance/sector-standards/construction-standards/whole-life-carbon-assessment
- CIBSE Guide M1: https://cibse.org/knowledge-research/knowledge-portal/guide-m1-guidance-for-designers-2023/
- RIBA Plan of Work 2020: https://www.architecture.com/knowledge-and-resources/resources-landing-page/riba-plan-of-work

## P2 — Preserve the permanent fabric

### Revised working public principle
**Do not use long-lived fabric as the routine distribution zone for shorter-lived systems. Where services must cross or attach to permanent construction, make the interface deliberate, limited and documented.**

### Evidence status
**A + B, but v7 wording is too absolute.**

### Established basis
Historic England's building-services guidance for historic fabric explicitly advocates mitigation, minimisation and reversibility; reuse of routes; shared routes; accessible service routes; spare capacity; and limiting permanent scarring. Design-for-disassembly guidance similarly supports layer independence and access to reversible connections.

### What does not survive the audit
V7's sentence **“No service is permitted inside the permanent fabric of the building.”** is rhetorically strong but professionally brittle as a general doctrine. Some crossings and embedded systems can be rational, necessary or integral to performance. The stronger rule concerns **routine distribution and avoidable entanglement**, not metaphysical purity.

### Distinctive contribution
Apply conservation-style respect for fabric **at first construction**, before the building has become historic.

### Main counterargument
Separating every service can enlarge sections, increase embodied material and produce needless complexity.

### Required response
Use a hierarchy: avoid entanglement → minimise crossings → design unavoidable crossings → document and provide renewal path.

### Editorial consequence for v7
Rewrite the absolute prohibition throughout. Preserve “no retrospective chasing/drilling as a default” as a strong project requirement for the reference house.

### Core sources
- Historic England, Installing New Services: https://historicengland.org.uk/advice/technical-advice/building-services-engineering/installing-new-services/
- ISO 20887: https://www.iso.org/standard/69370.html
- Arup Circular Buildings Toolkit: https://ce-toolkit.dhub.arup.com/report
- UKGBC Design for Deconstruction: https://ukgbc.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Design-for-Deconstruction-1.pdf

## P3 — Design the interface

### Working public principle
**Treat junctions between systems, materials and lifespan layers as designed building elements: they must accommodate movement, tolerance, sealing, fixing, inspection and renewal as required.**

### Evidence status
**A + C. Strong technical basis; architectural synthesis is ours.**

### Established basis
- Normal construction guidance already requires differential movement to be allowed for at masonry/cladding joints.
- ISO 20887 and circular-design guidance support independence, accessible reversible connections and layer separation.
- Systemised-envelope standards explicitly include interfaces and maintenance requirements.

### Distinctive contribution
The book elevates the **interface itself** from technical junction to first-class architectural object. A cornice, threshold, reveal, panel border or trim can simultaneously resolve movement, replacement and visual order.

### Main counterargument
Over-articulating every interface can produce visual fuss and unnecessary bespoke metalwork.

### Required response
Separate the principle from its expression. Many interfaces can be visually quiet or conventional. Visible articulation is justified only where it performs real work.

### Editorial consequence for v7
Retain the designed-interface concept. Demote “brass line” from recurrent quasi-rule to one reference-house material language.

### Core sources
- NHBC masonry movement-joint guidance: https://nhbc-standards.co.uk/
- ISO 20887: https://www.iso.org/standard/69370.html
- Arup Circular Buildings Toolkit: https://ce-toolkit.dhub.arup.com/report
- CWCT systemised building envelope standard: https://www.cwct.co.uk/collections/standards/products/cwct-standard

## P4 — Make foreseeable failure visible, containable and repairable

### Revised working public principle
**Design credible failures to reveal themselves early, remain locally containable where practical, and be reachable for repair before they consume long-lived fabric.**

### Evidence status
**A + C. Very strong for water; broader formulation is an architectural synthesis.**

### Established basis
Norwegian building regulations/guidance explicitly require indoor water installations to be arranged so leakage is easy to detect and does not damage building elements; pipe-in-pipe systems and drained cabinets are established methods. UK unvented-hot-water guidance uses visible discharge at a tundish as a safety principle. Building-envelope practice widely uses drained cavities, overflows and secondary drainage.

### What should change
“Harmless failure” is too absolute. Failures can have consequences. The professional formulation should be **visible, contained/limited, isolatable and repairable**.

### Distinctive contribution
Generalise a principle seen strongly in water systems into a building-wide **failure architecture**: ask not only how a system works, but how it fails, how the failure announces itself, and what it damages before intervention.

### Main counterargument
Secondary containment everywhere can itself create cost, trapped moisture, inaccessible dirt traps and false confidence.

### Required response
Risk-proportionate hierarchy: eliminate vulnerable arrangements first; use passive containment only where credible leakage warrants it; provide drainage/drying/inspection.

### Editorial consequence for v7
Retain passive/visible failure as core doctrine. Demote continuous metal troughs and external tell-tales to patterns selected by risk.

### Core sources
- Norwegian TEK17 §15-5 / official guidance: https://www.dibk.no/regelverk/byggteknisk-forskrift-tek17/15/ii/15-5
- Approved Document G: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/sanitation-hot-water-safety-and-water-efficiency-approved-document-g
- SINTEF/building-research pipe-in-pipe guidance and Scandinavian system precedents.

## P5 — Give maintenance a geography

### Working public principle
**Organise maintenance-intensive systems, working spaces, access routes and replacement paths as a coherent spatial topology established during design.**

### Evidence status
**A + B + C. Underlying duty is established; whole-building spatial synthesis is distinctive.**

### Established basis
CDM places maintainers/cleaners/repairers within the designer's safety thinking. CIBSE Guide M/M1 makes maintainability a design concern. Institutional briefs already require Access & Maintenance Strategies and major-plant replacement routes.

### Architectural lineage
Kahn's served/servant organisation and the Salk Institute's interstitial service floors demonstrate that supporting systems can possess explicit spatial order.

### Terminology caution
Ignaz Strebel's 2011 *The living building: towards a geography of maintenance work* already uses neighbouring language. Our definition is different—prospective architectural topology rather than ethnography—but the publication should cite it.

### Distinctive contribution
Move from “can I reach this component?” to “what is the building-wide route by which people, tools, replacement equipment and waste move through maintenance events?”

### Main counterargument
A domestic house cannot justify hospital-grade service circulation or sacrifice too much valuable area to trades that arrive intermittently.

### Required response
Maintenance geography does **not** imply separate corridors. Ordinary halls, cupboards, stairs and joinery can do double duty. The doctrine asks for coherence, not maximum service area.

### Editorial consequence for v7
Promote from service subsection to major architectural chapter. Keep undercroft/riser/etc. as possible instruments, not definitions of the concept.

### Core sources
- HSE CDM designer duties: https://www.hse.gov.uk/construction/cdm/2015/designers.htm
- CIBSE Guide M/M1: https://cibse.org/knowledge-research/knowledge-portal/guide-m-maintenance-engineering-and-management-2023/
- Salk Institute architecture: https://www.salk.edu/about/about-salk-architecture/
- Strebel 2011, *The living building: towards a geography of maintenance work*.

## P6 — Prefer ordinary parts in extraordinary arrangements

### Working public principle
**Prefer widely understood components, standard interfaces and ordinary fabrication methods; concentrate invention at the architectural arrangement and the interfaces that genuinely require it.**

### Evidence status
**B + C. Strong circular/maintenance rationale; fundamentally an authorial design rule.**

### Established basis
ISO 20887 and circular-design guidance favour simplicity and standardisation. Arup's toolkit explicitly recommends standardisation, reversible connections and minimising connection types. Mature maintenance practice values availability of parts and familiar service procedures.

### Distinctive contribution
The useful phrase is not “use standard products” but **make the logic unusual, not the consumables**. The house may be highly considered without becoming dependent on one-off valves, proprietary software or irreplaceable mechanisms.

### Main counterargument
Commodity components can be lower quality, visually inappropriate, or themselves become obsolete. Bespoke components are sometimes longer-lived precisely because they are simple and remanufacturable.

### Required response
Use a hierarchy: standard commodity component → standard component in custom enclosure → repeatable custom component from standard stock/processes → unique mechanism only with justification. Preserve v7's **remanufacturability** test.

### Editorial consequence for v7
Retain strongly, but explicitly distinguish **proprietary** from **bespoke**. A bespoke bronze grille that any metalworker can remake is less locked-in than a proprietary smart valve whose cloud platform disappears.

### Core sources
- ISO 20887: https://www.iso.org/standard/69370.html
- Arup Circular Buildings Toolkit: https://ce-toolkit.dhub.arup.com/report
- UKGBC Design for Deconstruction: https://ukgbc.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Design-for-Deconstruction-1.pdf

## P7 — Let permanence be architectural

### Working public principle
**Concentrate adaptability in layers that benefit from change; allow rooms, axes, stairs, structure and other defining architectural relationships to be deliberately enduring.**

### Evidence status
**B + C. This is principally the book's architectural position.**

### Established lineage
Open Building proves that stable support and changeable infill can coexist. Molenvliet is especially useful because adaptable dwellings were achieved without abandoning coherent architectural/urban character. “Loose fit” traditions provide the opposing pressure toward generic future capacity.

### Distinctive contribution
The book refuses the lazy inference that longevity requires generic space. **Selective permanence** is the stronger idea: the building can change precisely because not everything is asked to change.

### Main counterargument
Today's “enduring” spatial order may become tomorrow's functional obstacle; highly specific architecture can become obsolete.

### Required response
Argue for robust specificity: well-proportioned rooms, useful dimensions, generous circulation and good light permit multiple uses without physical transformability. Distinguish confidence from rigidity.

### Editorial consequence for v7
Promote “functional longevity and appropriate permanence” into Part I. The Georgian reference must remain in the reference-house section, not be used as evidence for the principle.

### Core sources
- Council on Open Building, Molenvliet: https://www.councilonopenbuilding.org/projects/molenvliet
- Council on Open Building, NEXT21 and case studies: https://www.councilonopenbuilding.org/projects
- Habraken/Open Building literature.

## P8 — Let passive architecture do the first work

### Working public principle
**Use form, envelope, orientation, shading, mass, controllable openings, drainage and gravity to reduce demands on active systems before adding mechanical or electronic correction.**

### Evidence status
**A + C. Strong for environmental design; needs careful scope.**

### Established basis
CIBSE TM59 (2026) now explicitly emphasises optimising passive design at the outset and testing homes under 2050s weather. RICS whole-life-carbon guidance recognises fabric-first and naturally ventilated approaches as reducing both operational and repeat equipment carbon where appropriate. Moisture and drainage practice likewise favours geometry, falls and drainage rather than relying only on sealants.

### Distinctive contribution
The doctrine extends “fabric first” beyond energy into a broader **physics-before-controls hierarchy**: geometry/gravity/drainage/mechanical simplicity before sensors/software where practical.

### Main counterargument
Passive-first can become ideological. Airtight low-energy homes, heat recovery, heat pumps and controls may outperform supposedly simple passive approaches; climate and air quality can require mechanical systems.

### Required response
Use the phrase **first work**, not “only work”. Performance and health outrank aesthetic or ideological simplicity.

### Editorial consequence for v7
Retain but remove any implication that active systems are second-rate. The reference house should be modelled rather than chosen by doctrine.

### Core sources
- CIBSE TM59 2026: https://cibse.org/knowledge-research/knowledge-portal/tm59-overheating-risk-in-dwellings-a-design-stage-methodology-2026/
- RICS WLCA: https://www.rics.org/profession-standards/rics-standards-and-guidance/sector-standards/construction-standards/whole-life-carbon-assessment

## P9 — Make the building legible across generations

### Revised working public principle
**The building should preserve enough information—in records and, for critical systems, in the fabric itself—that future owners and trades can understand, isolate, maintain and alter it safely.**

### Evidence status
**A + C. Digital lifecycle information is strongly established; physical self-documentation is a distinctive extension.**

### Established basis
The Building Safety Regulator's “golden thread” regime (for higher-risk buildings) embodies the principle that building information should remain current, accessible and usable through the building lifecycle. RIBA Stage 6/7 expects Building Manual/Asset Information and updating through use. Uniclass provides a common classification language for assets and maintenance information. Circular-design guidance recommends disassembly manuals and keeping records current.

### Important boundary
A private house is not subject to the higher-risk-building golden-thread regime merely because the concept is useful. The book should cite it as professional precedent for lifecycle information, not imply a legal duty.

### Distinctive contribution
V7 adds **physical redundancy**: critical valves, routes, boundaries and isolation should remain understandable even if the cloud account/BIM/archive disappears. That is a defensible resilience position, not established regulation.

### Main counterargument
Too much labelling becomes visual clutter and can itself go stale. Physical records can diverge from altered reality.

### Required response
Limit permanent physical identification to high-value information and pair it with disciplined change control. Use open digital formats plus a concise on-building index.

### Editorial consequence for v7
Retain building passport and physical index. Replace ornamental assumptions (“engraved brass everywhere”) with a requirement for durable, maintainable identifiers; brass becomes reference-house expression.

### Core sources
- BSR golden thread: https://www.gov.uk/guidance/keeping-information-about-a-higher-risk-building-the-golden-thread
- RIBA Plan of Work 2020 / Plan for Use
- Uniclass: https://uniclass.thenbs.com/
- Arup Circular Buildings Toolkit, disassembly manual guidance: https://ce-toolkit.dhub.arup.com/report

## P10 — Resolve technology as architecture

### Working public principle
**Technical necessities that occupants encounter should be resolved through architectural composition, material and craft rather than treated as either concealed accidents or gratuitous technical display.**

### Evidence status
**B + C. Primarily a design position.**

### Established lineage
Historic England guidance explicitly notes that services can be integrated via mouldings, balustrades, picture rails and other architectural elements, and that modern services in historic buildings should be thoughtfully integrated rather than crudely disguised. Kahn demonstrates the stronger proposition that service requirements can generate architectural order. Lloyd's and Pompidou demonstrate the opposite expressive extreme: systems can become overt architectural language.

### Distinctive contribution
The book seeks a third condition: **technical legibility without technical aesthetic**. Access panels, service skirting, grilles and movement joints can be real architecture while occupied rooms still feel solid, domestic and period-informed.

### Main counterargument
Decorating technical apparatus can increase cost, complicate access and disguise poor engineering beneath craftsmanship.

### Required response
Architecture follows performance. The enclosure/trim may be bespoke; the valve, fan, terminal or connector behind it should remain ordinary where possible. Every visible technical motif must correspond to a real function.

### Editorial consequence for v7
Retain as an explicit architectural ambition. Georgian language and brass/bronze are one demonstration, not the universal rule.

### Core sources
- Historic England, Installing New Services: https://historicengland.org.uk/advice/technical-advice/building-services-engineering/installing-new-services/
- Salk Institute: https://www.salk.edu/about/about-salk-architecture/
- Lloyd's Building: https://www.lloyds.com/about-lloyds/the-lloyds-building
- Centre Pompidou architecture: https://www.centrepompidou.fr/en/centre-pompidou-is-transforming-itself/an-iconic-architecture

# Cross-principle conclusions

## 1. Four principles are predominantly established knowledge
P1 (time/service life), P2 (separation/minimising damage), P4 (failure management, particularly water), and P8 (passive-first environmental hierarchy) have strong professional bases. The book's task is synthesis and application, not discovery.

## 2. Three are strongest as architectural syntheses
P3 (interface), P5 (maintenance geography), and P9 (physical legibility) take recognised technical concerns and elevate them into whole-building architectural ideas. These are likely to be the publication's most interesting contributions.

## 3. Three are explicit design positions
P6 (ordinary parts), P7 (selective permanence), and P10 (technology as architecture) should be written as reasoned architectural positions, not disguised as evidence-led universal truths.

## 4. The publication should visibly mark epistemic status
Every pattern should carry a small status marker such as:
- **Established** — standard/mature practice;
- **Supported** — strong precedent but context-dependent;
- **Proposed** — architectural synthesis requiring project validation;
- **Experimental** — prototype/reference-house hypothesis.

This single device will make the book substantially more credible.

## 5. Three wording changes should happen before prose rewrite
1. “No service is permitted inside permanent fabric” → no **routine distribution** within permanent fabric; unavoidable crossings are deliberately designed.
2. “Make failure harmless and visible” → make foreseeable failure **visible, containable and repairable**.
3. “Critical protection does not depend on electronics” → passive/manual protection is the **base layer**, with electronics allowed as additional protection where valuable.
