# Preface Precedents — Selection and Research Notes

**Status:** research control for `Preface — The Obvious, Eventually`  
**Purpose:** distinguish examples that carry the argument from examples that merely illustrate delayed obviousness.

## Selection rule

An example belongs in the preface only if it advances a distinct part of the book's intellectual method.

The preface is not a catalogue of inventions. It is an argument about **inherited arrangements becoming invisible**, and about breakthroughs that occur when relationships, interfaces, circulation or assembly are reconsidered.

The selected examples therefore need to form a sequence.

## Selected precedent 01 — John Thorpe / Beaufort House

### Date
c. 1595–1603.

### Evidence
The Sir John Soane's Museum holds Thorpe's plan for Beaufort House and describes its central corridor as innovative.

Robin Evans later used Beaufort House in *Figures, Doors and Passages* as an early recorded English example of the corridor used to remove traffic from rooms.

### What it proves rhetorically
**A function can be given its own space.**

Before circulation is separated, movement consumes rooms.

Once circulation has an independent geography, the older arrangement becomes difficult to see as inevitable.

### Connection to the book
The house doctrine repeatedly makes the same architectural move:

- maintenance should not consume rooms;
- services should not consume permanent fabric;
- change should be given deliberate places to occur.

Thorpe is therefore not an analogy pasted onto the project. He is a direct architectural precedent for **separating a recurring function from the spaces it formerly consumed**.

### Caution
Do not write that Thorpe "invented the corridor" as an uncontested fact.

Preferred formulation:
- innovative central corridor;
- an early recorded English use;
- Robin Evans cautiously identified Beaufort House as the earliest English example he had found.

### Sources
- Sir John Soane's Museum, *A Golden Age of Architecture: Rare Elizabethan and Early Jacobean Drawings by Thorpe and the Smythsons*: https://www.soane.org/exhibitions/golden-age-architecture
- Robin Evans, *Figures, Doors and Passages*, *Architectural Design* 48, no. 4 (1978), later in *Translations from Drawing to Building and Other Essays* (Architectural Association / MIT Press, 1997).
- MIT Press edition: https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262550277/translations-from-drawing-to-building-and-other-essays/

## Selected precedent 02 — Dick and Mac McDonald / Speedee Service System

### Date
1948.

### Evidence
The McDonald brothers deliberately closed a successful conventional drive-in and redesigned its operating system.

The Smithsonian account describes their use of a tennis court, chalk and full-scale rehearsal to choreograph the new kitchen arrangement before rebuilding the restaurant.

### What it proves rhetorically
**Optimising an inherited process is different from redrawing the process.**

The relevant question was not how to make the carhop move faster. The brothers changed:
- menu;
- labour;
- customer movement;
- workstation position;
- production sequence;
- service interface.

### Connection to the book
The doctrine repeatedly tries to escape local optimisation.

Examples:
- not "how do we chase walls more neatly?" but "why is routine distribution in permanent walls?";
- not "which filler cracks less?" but "why is brittle finish bridging predictable movement?";
- not "how can plastering be cleaner?" but "which layers should be wet-made in situ at all?".

The tennis-court exercise is especially relevant to the project's prototype method: draw the whole sequence, rehearse it physically, then change the arrangement.

### Caution
Keep this episode short.

Too much McDonald's turns the preface into a management-book anecdote.

Its function is **choreography and system topology**, not celebration of fast food.

### Sources
- Smithsonian Magazine, *The Story of How McDonald's First Got Its Start*: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/story-how-mcdonalds-first-got-its-start-180960931/
- McDonald's corporate history, Speedee Service System: https://corporate.mcdonalds.com/corpmcd/our-company/who-we-are/our-history.html

## Selected precedent 03 — Malcom McLean / containerisation

### Date
Initial Sea-Land system 1956; international standardisation developed through the following decade; ISO/TC 104 created 1961; first ISO freight-container standard published 1968.

### Evidence
Containers existed before McLean.

The transformative system joined:
- truck;
- crane;
- ship;
- rail;
- port;
- standard box;
- common corner interface.

Early proliferation produced incompatible container and corner-fitting systems.

The Smithsonian account describes the importance of standardising corner fittings and lengths, including McLean's decision to allow relevant Sea-Land patents to be used royalty-free.

ISO subsequently standardised dimensions, handling, tests and marking.

### What it proves rhetorically
**The interface can be more consequential than the object.**

A metal box is unremarkable.

A box that can be handled by independent ships, ports, trains and trucks without repacking becomes an infrastructural system.

### Connection to the book
This is the clearest external precedent for:

- architectural backplanes;
- standard mounting geometries;
- finish-agnostic floor interfaces;
- ordinary commodity components inside durable architectural systems;
- remanufacturability after the original supplier disappears.

The desired innovation is often not a bespoke panel or device.

It is a stable interface that lets many future objects participate.

### Additional value
McLean was treated as an outsider by parts of the established shipping industry.

This is useful tonally, but should not become an outsider-genius myth.

Containerisation succeeded through engineering, labour agreements, standards work and system-wide coordination—not individual defiance alone.

### Sources
- Smithsonian Magazine, *The Now-Ubiquitous Shipping Container Was an Idea Before Its Time*: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/shipping-container-idea-before-time-180963730/
- ISO/TC 104 — Freight containers: https://www.iso.org/committee/51156.html
- ISO history, first freight-container standard in 1968: https://www.iso.org/about
- ISO, *Boxing clever — How standardization built a global economy*: https://www.iso.org/news/2017/09/Ref2215.html

## Selected precedent 04 — Joseph Paxton / Crystal Palace

### Date
Designed 1850; completed 1851.

### Evidence
Paxton's experience was rooted in horticulture and greenhouse construction.

The V&A traces the Great Exhibition proposal directly to his earlier glasshouse work at Chatsworth.

The Crystal Palace used highly standardised prefabricated parts and production-line assembly; the V&A records completion roughly seven months after construction began.

After the exhibition, the structure was dismantled and its prefabricated components were used in the larger Sydenham Crystal Palace.

### What it proves rhetorically
**Manufacture, assembly, disassembly and architectural delight are not mutually exclusive.**

Paxton also strengthens the outsider theme:
- horticulturist / gardener;
- experience formed in another building culture;
- imported a system of thinking from glasshouses into monumental public architecture.

### Connection to the book
This precedent does two jobs at once.

It supports:
- manufacture according to lifespan;
- repeatable parts;
- dry assembly;
- disassembly;
- standard interface families.

More importantly, it refuses the false choice between **technical rationality and architecture**.

The book does not seek a technical-looking house.

It seeks constructional intelligence capable of producing richer architecture.

### Caution
Do not romanticise the Palace as proof that prefabrication is always superior.

Its environmental conditions, life, programme and scale differ radically from a house.

Use it as evidence that standardisation and architectural ambition can coexist.

### Sources
- V&A, *The foundation of the V&A's Collections: the Great Exhibition of 1851*: https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/the-great-exhibition-of-1851/
- V&A Blog, *Flowers fit for a Crystal Palace*: https://www.vam.ac.uk/blog/museum-life/flowers-fit-for-a-crystal-palace
- Historic England, Crystal Palace history: https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1385457/
- Chatsworth, *Joseph and Sarah Paxton: an Introduction*: https://www.chatsworth.org/news-media/news-blogs-press-releases/joseph-and-sarah-paxton-an-introduction/

# Examples deliberately excluded from the main preface

## Wheeled suitcase

### Why it was attractive
An exceptionally memorable example of a technically obvious idea arriving late.

### Why it is excluded
It proves delayed obviousness but not enough of the book's architectural method.

It pushes the preface toward "clever inventions everyone missed" rather than **systems, interfaces and spatial arrangements**.

Keep available for talks or shorter explanatory writing.

---

## Whitworth screw thread

### Why it was attractive
An excellent example of standardised interfaces creating interchangeability.

### Why it is excluded
Containerisation makes the same point at greater spatial and infrastructural scale and connects more naturally to architectural platforms.

Whitworth may be useful later in Part V or the pattern catalogue when discussing ordinary fasteners and remanufacturability.

---

## Piggly Wiggly / self-service retail

### Why it was attractive
A strong example of changing circulation, labour and customer roles rather than merely improving a conventional store.

### Why it is excluded
McDonald's already occupies the choreography/system-redesign role and has the unusually useful full-scale tennis-court prototype story.

A second retail/operations example would be redundant.

# Intellectual precedents that should not become anecdotes

The preface should acknowledge that its architectural concerns do not begin here.

The manuscript proper already develops:
- Habraken — support / infill;
- Duffy / Brand — changing layers;
- Alex Gordon — long life, loose fit, low energy;
- service-life planning;
- design for disassembly;
- conservation practice;
- maintenance design.

Do not repeat that literature review in the preface.

The preface establishes **temperament and method**.

Part I establishes professional lineage and evidence.

---

# Tonal lesson from Stewart Brand and Christopher Alexander

Both demonstrate that outsiders or cross-disciplinary writers can address architecture seriously when they:
- observe buildings closely;
- make ordinary things newly visible;
- take time and use seriously;
- offer testable propositions rather than aesthetic rebellion alone.

The present preface should have conviction without pretending to professional authority.

The relevant stance is:

> The outsider's privilege is not expertise. It is permission to remain surprised.

That privilege is only useful if followed by research, engineering, prototypes and willingness to abandon an attractive idea when it fails.

---

# Final precedent sequence

The essay should use the examples in this order:

1. **Thorpe — space:** give circulation its own geography.
2. **McDonald brothers — choreography:** redraw the process rather than optimise inherited motion.
3. **McLean / containerisation — interface:** standardise the relationship and heterogeneous systems can cooperate.
4. **Paxton — assembly and beauty:** manufactured, repeatable, disassemblable construction can still become architecture.

The examples progressively approach the house.

After Paxton, the historical analogies should stop.

The final third of the preface belongs entirely to the project's own position.
