# Repose and Low Vigilance

A house is an environment of unusually long and repeated exposure. It is occupied during sleep, illness, fatigue, family life, solitude and recovery as well as during moments of deliberate activity.

Ovid’s House therefore uses **repose** for a simple architectural objective:

> **A good home should minimise unnecessary vigilance.**

“Vigilance” is used architecturally rather than clinically. Repose does not mean sensory deprivation, minimalism, symmetry, historical style or the elimination of surprise. It means that the ordinary background condition of the house should not continually demand monitoring, interpretation or corrective action from its occupants.

A rich room can be restful. A sparse room can be stressful. The relevant distinction is between **richness that resolves into order** and stimulus that remains unresolved.

The evidential basis is cumulative rather than reducible to one paper or one measurable state. [Repose — Evidence and Limits](repose-evidence-audit.md) records the claim boundary in detail.

## Environmental demands

The strongest foundation is physical performance.

A house that overheats, transmits chronic unwanted noise, has poor indoor air quality, creates disabling glare or cannot be ventilated comfortably is not made restful by calm proportions or familiar materials.

The physical baseline includes:

- stable and appropriate temperature;
- effective overheating mitigation;
- good indoor air quality;
- controlled moisture;
- low unwanted noise, especially where sleep and retreat occur;
- useful daylight without disabling glare;
- secure and usable openings;
- adequate space;
- safe and understandable movement.

WHO housing and environmental-noise guidance, English overheating regulation and current healthy-homes guidance provide firmer ground here than most claims associated with “neuroarchitecture”.

The useful framing is that environments create demands while occupants possess finite coping and attentional resources. Architecture therefore matters not only through measurable hazards but through the amount and character of adaptation it repeatedly asks from the people living in it.

## Control is part of comfort

Research on indoor environments repeatedly associates perceived control with satisfaction, although the relationship varies with context and with whether the available control actually works.

The implication is architectural before it is technological. Useful local agency may include:

- an operable window;
- effective curtains, shutters or blinds;
- a door that closes;
- understandable heating controls;
- local lighting choice;
- the ability to shade direct sun;
- the ability to separate noisy and quiet activities;
- a comprehensible fallback when automation fails.

This does not mean every environmental variable should be manually controlled. It means important immediate conditions should not become inexplicable or impossible to alter merely because a central system nominally manages them.

The stronger design question is:

> **Can the occupant understand and alter the condition that is affecting them?**

## Privacy, retreat and graded exposure

Housing research treats privacy as physical, social and psychological rather than as a stylistic preference.

The architectural consequence is not universal enclosure. It is **choice over exposure**.

A dwelling should provide credible means to:

- close a room;
- achieve acoustic as well as visual privacy;
- move through intelligible thresholds from public to shared to private territory;
- withdraw without leaving the household entirely;
- keep unrelated activities from being forced into permanent mutual exposure;
- enjoy outlook without unavoidable display to neighbours or passers-by.

An open shared room can therefore be excellent when genuine alternatives exist. A dwelling in which nobody can retreat is a different proposition.

## Coherence, fascination and hominess

Experimental work on architectural interiors has identified dimensions including **coherence**, **fascination** and **hominess** in how people evaluate interior scenes.

That is useful because it breaks a false equation between repose and simplicity.

A room can contain ornament, books, patterned surfaces, joinery, artwork, texture, patina and complex natural materials while remaining easy to comprehend as a whole. Conversely, a sparse room can remain perceptually demanding if its hierarchy is unclear, its scale ambiguous or its technical elements compete continuously for attention.

Fascination is not the opposite of repose. The relevant question is whether visual interest is held within an intelligible larger order.

## Daylight and external connection

Daylight has architectural and environmental value, while evidence for specifically restorative effects indoors is promising but mixed.

The practical conclusion is therefore modest:

- provide useful daylight;
- avoid disabling glare;
- provide outlook where possible;
- allow occupants to perceive day, weather and seasonal change;
- coordinate glazing with privacy, noise, solar gain and shading rather than treating maximum glass area as an independent good.

**More glazing is not the principle.**

## Apparent structural equilibrium

Vision science provides strong evidence that people rapidly infer physically meaningful properties from scenes, including support, stability, mass and likely interaction with gravity.

That supports a bounded architectural review question:

> **What account of support, mass and equilibrium does this room appear to present?**

Engineering legibility and perceptual structural legibility are different. The actual load path must be correct and calculable. The visible architecture does not need to reveal every beam, reinforcement bar or fixing.

But where structure is conspicuous, substantial masses should not appear accidentally unsupported or precarious. A large cantilever, an apparently floating volume, a very slight-looking support beneath a heavy mass or an opening whose head appears inexplicable can make tension part of the permanent visual background.

That may be desirable in a pavilion, museum or deliberate moment of drama. In ordinary domestic rooms the presumption is different:

> **Principal spaces should normally present a plausible visual account of their own equilibrium.**

This is an architectural hypothesis, not a physiological law. Current evidence does **not** establish that cantilevers cause stress, arches reduce stress, traditional load-bearing expression is universally preferred or literal structural exposure improves health.

## Prospect, refuge and protected outlook

Prospect-refuge theory is often used as if it were a universal formula for comfort. The evidence is more mixed, particularly around refuge and when findings from natural settings are extended directly into interiors.

The useful architectural question survives without the overclaim:

> **Can an occupant look outward while retaining control over their own exposure?**

Deep reveals, bays, window seats, alcoves and seating with substantial enclosure behind may be excellent devices. Their value does not require an evolutionary law.

## Curvature

Research suggests that curvature can influence aesthetic preference, but it does not justify claims that rectilinear corners are inherently threatening or that curved rooms constitute a health intervention.

Curvature therefore remains an aesthetic and compositional resource rather than a prescription for repose.

## Different rooms, different intensities

A dwelling need not impose one emotional register on every room.

Homes support different kinds of life: sociability, work, sleep, intimacy, storage, ceremony, play and withdrawal. The useful objective is a **stable whole capable of different local intensities**.

A house may therefore contain:

- convivial rooms;
- quiet rooms;
- private rooms;
- work rooms;
- transitional spaces;
- places of ceremony;
- places of retreat.

Repose is the background condition that allows those differences to exist without turning the entire dwelling into a continuous stimulus field.

## Neuroarchitecture — useful evidence, weak shortcut

Work at the boundary of neuroscience, cognition and architecture is increasingly sophisticated, but the field remains methodologically heterogeneous. Studies use different stimuli, exposure durations, instruments and outcome measures; many rely on images or short immersive experiences rather than long occupation.

Three disciplines follow.

### Prefer convergent evidence

A proposition supported by building science, environmental psychology, post-occupancy evidence and appropriate physiological work is stronger than one resting on an isolated brain-imaging result.

### Do not confuse measurable response with meaningful outcome

A statistically different neural or physiological signal is not automatically better, healthier or architecturally important.

### Do not jump scales

Evidence about abstract shapes, images or short VR exposures should not silently become a claim about living in a room for twenty years.

Terms such as “the brain needs”, “humans evolved to prefer”, “neurologically correct” or “activates the amygdala therefore” do not strengthen an architectural proposition unless the evidence actually supports the causal step being claimed.

## Architectural consequences

Repose becomes useful only when translated into ordinary design questions.

### Structural settlement

Prefer principal domestic spaces whose apparent support is deliberate and settled rather than accidentally precarious.

Ask:

- can a lay observer form a plausible account of how the visible mass appears to stand?
- is structural drama intentional and local, or the persistent background condition?
- is a visually improbable support relationship doing genuine architectural work or merely performing novelty?

### Spatial coherence

The house should be comprehensible without becoming simplistic.

Ask:

- can occupants form a stable mental model of the plan?
- are principal routes, thresholds and room relationships intelligible?
- is hierarchy readable?
- does visual richness resolve into larger order?

### Graded exposure

Ask:

- can principal private rooms genuinely become private?
- can someone withdraw without leaving the household?
- can outlook coexist with controlled exposure?
- are acoustic and visual privacy considered together?

### Local agency

Ask:

- can light, shade, ventilation and temperature be adjusted intelligibly?
- can a noisy or visually intrusive condition be shut out?
- is there a useful fallback where automation materially affects comfort?
- does the occupant understand what a control will do?

### Sensory quiet where quiet matters

Bedrooms and retreat spaces deserve particular protection.

Ask:

- are external and internal noise controlled?
- are plant and plumbing noises kept away from sleep spaces where practical?
- can darkness be achieved?
- are glare and unwanted night lighting controllable?
- can ventilation preserve comfort without forcing an avoidable choice between fresh air and intolerable noise?

### Daylight without exposure or overheating

Ask:

- does the room receive useful daylight?
- can direct sun and glare be moderated?
- is the view worth having?
- does glazing compromise privacy, acoustics or summer comfort?
- can the occupant control the boundary?

### Richness within order

Do not confuse repose with minimalism.

Ask:

- is there hierarchy across scales?
- can ornament, material variation and occupation accumulate without destroying coherence?
- are technical elements visually subordinate unless deliberately celebrated?
- does detail reward attention rather than demand it?

### Deliberate intensity

Strong contrast, compression, release, surprise and unusual geometry can enrich a house. The important distinction is between a deliberate local event and a permanent background of unresolved tension.

## Relationship to the wider architecture

Repose is not a decorative agenda added after technical design. It reinforces several existing positions.

**Selective permanence** can give occupants stable rooms, axes, stairs, openings and boundaries against which faster-changing occupation occurs.

**Passive-first architecture** provides direct relationships with daylight, shade, air and thermal mass before invisible active systems take over.

**Tectonic honesty** keeps visible construction and material relationships intelligible without demanding structural exhibition.

**Maintenance geography** moves technical work away from principal rooms so maintainability does not become constant interaction with building systems.

**Replaceable assemblies** succeed only if seams, access and serviceability remain architecturally quiet in normal occupation.

**Workmanship robustness** matters because rattles, wavering joints, misaligned datums and unresolved interfaces can become persistent visual, acoustic and tactile disturbances even where the underlying technical system is notionally adequate.

## Repose review

A design review of a principal room or approach sequence can ask nine questions:

1. **Gravity** — does the architecture appear settled?
2. **Comprehension** — can the space and its relationships be understood readily?
3. **Exposure** — can occupants regulate privacy and retreat?
4. **Agency** — can important immediate conditions be controlled directly?
5. **Sensory load** — are noise, glare, heat, visual competition and technical activity proportionate to the room?
6. **Richness** — is there interest without unresolved competition?
7. **Recovery** — where can a tired, ill or overstimulated occupant withdraw?
8. **Technology** — does the house serve without continually announcing its systems?
9. **Intensity** — where are surprise or tension intentional, and where should they subside?

This review is qualitative unless a claim can be tested quantitatively. Measured environmental performance remains separate and mandatory.

## Open evidence question

The relationship between apparent structural support and long-duration domestic comfort remains the least established part of the repose argument and deserves direct testing rather than repeated assertion.

A useful study would compare otherwise matched domestic interiors with controlled variation in apparent support—for example conventional bearing, visually ambiguous transfer, pronounced cantilever, concealed but intuitively plausible support and arch/lintel conditions while controlling style and ornament.

Relevant outcomes could include perceived stability, tension/ease, hominess, preference and willingness to remain in the room. Architects and lay participants should be considered separately. Physiological or eye-tracking measures are useful only where they answer a defined question rather than serving as decorative scientific authority.

Until such evidence exists, apparent structural repose remains a reasoned architectural hypothesis.
