# Repose and Low Vigilance — Evidence Note

**Status:** audited research synthesis for doctrine integration.  
**Purpose:** establish the evidential basis, limits and architectural consequences of treating domestic repose as a design requirement. See also the [claim-by-claim evidence audit](repose-evidence-audit.md).  
**Scope:** ordinary dwellings. This note does not claim that architecture can determine mental state, diagnose health, or produce universal psychological responses.

---

## 1. Central proposition

A house is not merely an object that protects occupants from weather and accommodates activities. It is an environment of unusually long and repeated exposure, occupied during sleep, illness, fatigue, family life, solitude and recovery.

The architectural proposition developed here is:

> **A good home should minimise unnecessary vigilance.**

The word **repose** is used for the resulting architectural condition.

Repose does not mean sensory deprivation, blandness, symmetry, historical style or the elimination of surprise. It means that the ordinary background condition of the house should not continually demand monitoring, interpretation or corrective action from its occupants.

This proposition is a synthesis rather than a single experimentally proven law. Its constituent parts have different levels of evidential support and must not be presented as equally established.

---

## 2. What “low vigilance” means here

“Vigilance” is used architecturally rather than clinically.

A dwelling can impose unnecessary attentional demand when an occupant must repeatedly:

- make sense of confusing spatial organisation;
- monitor unwanted exposure to other people;
- compensate for uncontrollable heat, glare, noise or air movement;
- wonder whether an apparently precarious element is stable;
- attend to visually competing systems or technical apparatus;
- rely on hidden automation without an intelligible local override;
- remain alert to disturbance that cannot be shut out;
- negotiate spaces that provide no clear transition between public, shared and private life.

The objective is not zero stimulation. A rich room can be restful. A sparse room can be stressful.

The relevant distinction is between **richness that resolves into order** and **stimulus that remains unresolved**.

---

## 3. Evidence posture

The doctrine should distinguish four levels of confidence.

### Level A — direct and comparatively robust

Use confidently where relevant, while retaining normal qualifications about population and context.

- thermal comfort and protection from overheating/cold;
- acoustic protection and sleep disturbance from unwanted noise;
- indoor air quality and ventilation;
- daylight, glare and visual comfort;
- adequate space and avoidance of harmful crowding;
- basic usability, safety and accessibility.

These areas are supported by substantial building-science, public-health and regulatory evidence.

### Level B — supported but context-sensitive

Use as design principles rather than universal formulae.

- perceived control over indoor conditions;
- privacy regulation and the ability to withdraw;
- coherence / comprehensibility of environments;
- environmental richness and fascination;
- “hominess” or personal ease;
- restorative value of daylight and benign external connection.

### Level C — plausible transfer requiring explicit qualification

Use as an architectural hypothesis and research programme, not as settled causal fact.

- visually legible support and apparent structural equilibrium as possible contributors to domestic repose;
- the proposition that gratuitous apparent instability may contribute to perceptual tension;
- the preference for principal domestic spaces whose structural expression is settled rather than intentionally precarious.

The evidence that visual perception extracts support, stability and other physical properties is strong. The proposed contribution of apparent structural equilibrium to long-duration domestic comfort has not been established directly and remains an architectural inference.

### Level D — exploratory, contested or commonly overstated

Do not use as doctrine without stronger evidence.

- universal prospect-refuge prescriptions;
- claims that curvature is inherently safe or calming;
- claims that particular historical proportions are neurologically preferred;
- golden-ratio or “sacred geometry” health claims;
- deterministic claims that natural materials directly reduce stress;
- claims that a specific architectural form “activates” or “deactivates” a brain region in a way that establishes a design rule;
- generic “biophilia” claims treated as proof rather than hypothesis.

## 4. Environmental stress as a framing device

Evans and McCoy’s 1998 paper *When Buildings Don’t Work: The Role of Architecture in Human Health* remains useful because it does not pretend to have discovered a catalogue of therapeutic shapes.

Instead, it uses psychological stress as a heuristic and identifies environmental dimensions including stimulation, coherence, affordance, control and restoration. Crucially, the authors explicitly acknowledged that direct evidence linking specific design features to health was then sparse.

That caution should remain ours.

The important conceptual move is that an environment can create **demands**, while occupants possess finite coping and attentional resources. Architecture therefore matters not only through measurable physical hazards but through the amount and character of adaptation it continually asks of its occupants.

This provides a useful conceptual basis for the low-vigilance proposition without requiring a direct neurological claim.

**Source:** Evans, G. W. & McCoy, J. M. (1998), *Journal of Environmental Psychology* 18(1), 85–94. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1006/jevp.1998.0089

## 5. Control is part of comfort

Building research repeatedly finds that satisfaction is influenced not only by measured environmental conditions but by occupants’ **perceived control** over them.

The implication is architectural before it is technological.

Useful forms of local agency include:

- an operable window;
- effective curtains, shutters or blinds;
- a door that closes;
- understandable heating control;
- local lighting choice;
- the ability to shade direct sun;
- the ability to separate noisy and quiet activities;
- manual fallback when automation fails.

The doctrine should therefore prefer controls that are intelligible and effective at the point of use over systems that deliver nominally optimal conditions while leaving occupants unable to understand or alter them.

This does not mean every environmental variable must be manually controlled. It means the design should take seriously the psychological and practical value of being able to alter one’s immediate condition.

**Sources:**

- Hellwig, R. T. (2015), “Perceived control in indoor environments: a conceptual approach”, *Building Research & Information* 43(3). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/09613218.2015.1004150
- Lechner et al. (2021), “Does thermal control improve visual satisfaction?”, *Indoor Air* 31, 2329–2349. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/ina.12851
- Homes England (2026), *Healthy Homes — a foundation for healthier and resilient communities*: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/healthy-homes/healthy-homes-a-foundation-for-healthier-and-resilient-communities

## 6. Coherence, fascination and hominess

A substantial experimental study of architectural interiors asked 798 participants to rate 200 interior images on sixteen aesthetic-response measures. Three components explained 90% of the variance in ratings:

- **coherence** — ease of organising and comprehending the scene;
- **fascination** — informational richness and generated interest;
- **hominess** — the extent to which the scene feels personal / domestic.

Later work found that these dimensions remained useful across participant groups, although their contribution to preference varied.

This is important because it gives us a better formulation than “calm architecture should be simple”.

The target is not minimal stimulus.

A room may contain ornament, books, patterned surfaces, joinery, artwork, texture, patina and complex natural materials while remaining easy to comprehend as a whole.

Conversely, a sparse environment can remain perceptually demanding if its hierarchy is unclear, its scale is ambiguous or its technical elements compete for attention.

Fascination is not in conflict with repose. The relevant question is whether visual interest is organised by an intelligible larger order.

**Sources:**

- Coburn et al. (2020), “Psychological and neural responses to architectural interiors”, *Cortex* 126, 217–241. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2020.01.009
- Coburn et al. (2021), “Individual differences in preference for architectural interiors”, *Journal of Environmental Psychology* 77, 101668. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2021.101668

## 7. Privacy, retreat and graded boundaries

A systematic review of 71 housing studies identified privacy as a physical, social and psychological housing concern rather than a merely stylistic preference.

For doctrine purposes, the useful proposition is not that every occupant wants the same amount of enclosure. It is that a house should provide **means of regulating exposure**.

Architectural consequences include:

- rooms that can close;
- acoustic as well as visual privacy;
- understandable transitions between entrance, shared life and private life;
- places where an occupant can withdraw without leaving the household entirely;
- layouts that do not force unrelated activities into permanent mutual exposure;
- glazing that provides outlook without making the occupant continuously visible.

The principle is **choice over exposure**, not universal enclosure.

An open-plan space can therefore be excellent where it is accompanied by genuine alternatives. An entire dwelling in which nobody can withdraw is a different proposition.

**Source:** Macedo, Ornstein & Elali (2022), “Privacy and housing: research perspectives based on a systematic literature review”, *Journal of Housing and the Built Environment* 37, 653–683. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10901-022-09939-z

## 8. Physical comfort remains the strongest foundation

The doctrine should not allow the language of neuroarchitecture to distract from better-established environmental causes of discomfort.

A house that is visually serene but overheats, transmits road noise, has poor air quality or creates glare is not a low-vigilance environment.

The physical baseline includes:

- stable and appropriate temperature;
- effective overheating mitigation;
- good indoor air quality;
- controlled moisture;
- low unwanted noise, especially where sleep and retreat occur;
- useful daylight without disabling glare;
- secure, usable openings;
- adequate space;
- safe and understandable movement.

WHO housing guidance, WHO environmental-noise guidance, English overheating regulation and current Homes England guidance all provide firmer ground here than most “neuroarchitecture” claims.

**Sources:**

- WHO (2018), *Housing and health guidelines*: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241550376
- WHO (2018), *Environmental noise guidelines for the European Region*: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789289053563

## 9. Daylight and external connection

A 2024 systematic review of 33 studies found promising evidence for restorative effects of daylight indoors, particularly affective and clinical outcomes, while also reporting substantial heterogeneity and null findings.

The design conclusion should therefore be modest and practical:

- provide useful daylight;
- avoid glare;
- provide outlook where possible;
- allow occupants to perceive day, weather and seasonal change;
- coordinate glazing with privacy, noise, heat gain and shading rather than maximising glass area as an independent good.

**More glazing** is not the principle.

**Source:** Jamrozik et al. (2024), “Restorative effects of daylight in indoor environments — A systematic literature review”, *Journal of Environmental Psychology* 97, 102323. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2024.102323

## 10. Intuitive physics and apparent structural equilibrium

Recent vision science provides strong evidence that perception automatically extracts physically meaningful properties from scenes.

A 2026 *Annual Review of Vision Science* synthesis describes intuitive physics as rapid, spontaneous and stimulus-driven. Human vision estimates properties including stability, support, mass, stiffness and the likely effects of gravity even though many of those properties are not directly observable.

This is directly relevant to architecture, but the translation must be disciplined.

### What the evidence supports

People do not need engineering training to perceive that something looks:

- supported or unsupported;
- balanced or unstable;
- heavy or light;
- compressed, bent or precarious;
- likely to fall or remain at rest.

### What the evidence does not yet establish

It does not establish that:

- a visible masonry arch causes measurable long-term stress reduction;
- a domestic cantilever is physiologically harmful;
- traditional load-bearing expression is universally preferred;
- literal structural exposure is required for comfort.

### Architectural hypothesis

The doctrine therefore adopts the following **qualified** proposition:

> **The ordinary domestic environment should normally present a plausible visual account of its own equilibrium.**

This is **perceptual structural legibility**, distinct from engineering legibility.

The real load path must be correct and deterministic. The visible architecture need not reveal every concealed beam, reinforcement bar or fixing. But substantial masses should not gratuitously appear to defy support, and principal rooms should not depend on apparent precariousness for their architectural effect.

This is especially relevant to:

- large cantilevers;
- apparently floating masses;
- very thin-looking supports beneath visually heavy construction;
- openings whose heads appear unsupported;
- stairs or balconies designed primarily to appear weightless;
- roof forms whose support is visually inexplicable.

The issue is not structural safety. It is whether the architecture chooses **equilibrium or tension** as its persistent perceptual background.

For a museum, pavilion or monument, tension may be the point.

For a house, the presumption should favour equilibrium.

**Source:** Bi, W. & Yildirim, I. (2026), “Visual Perception of Intuitive Physics and Its Computational Foundations”, *Annual Review of Vision Science* 12, 491–513. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-vision-110423-024138

## 11. Prospect-refuge: limits of the evidence

Prospect-refuge theory is attractive because it appears to explain why protected outlooks, alcoves, window seats and edge positions often feel good.

The evidence is more mixed than architectural discourse often admits.

A meta-analysis of 34 quantitative studies found support for prospect in some settings, but refuge produced substantial neutral and contrary results, and evidence derived from natural settings was frequently overextended into interiors.

Therefore:

- do not present prospect-refuge as an evolutionary law;
- do not derive fixed geometry from it;
- retain the practical question: **can an occupant see outward while controlling their own exposure?**

Deep reveals, bays, window seats, alcoves and chairs with a substantial wall behind them may remain excellent architectural devices. Their value need not be justified by an overconfident evolutionary story.

**Source:** Dosen & Ostwald (2016), “Evidence for prospect-refuge theory: a meta-analysis of the findings of environmental preference research”, *City, Territory and Architecture* 3, 4. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1186/s40410-016-0033-1

## 12. Curvature: evidence and limits

An influential 2013 fMRI study found curvilinear interiors more likely to be judged beautiful than rectilinear interiors, but contour did not alter approach/avoidance decisions.

A later systematic review and meta-analysis found a broader preference for curvature across many visual stimuli, but this still does not justify the architectural claim that sharp corners are inherently threatening or physiologically harmful.

Therefore:

- curvature may be an aesthetic resource;
- rectilinearity is not a stressor by default;
- do not use amygdala or threat-language as a shortcut from preference data to domestic health.

**Sources:**

- Vartanian et al. (2013), “Impact of contour on aesthetic judgments and approach-avoidance decisions in architecture”, *PNAS* 110(Suppl. 2), 10446–10453. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1301227110
- Gómez-Puerto et al. (2023), “How universal is preference for visual curvature? A systematic review and meta-analysis”, *Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences*. Open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10091794/

## 13. The home is a special case

Research specifically on homes remains thinner than the confidence of architectural commentary often suggests.

Graham, Gosling and Travis (2015) explicitly called residential space under-researched. Their exploratory work identified desired home ambiances including restoration, kinship, stimulation, intimacy, productivity and storage, with different rooms supporting different combinations.

This is another reason not to design the whole house toward one undifferentiated emotional state.

A house should contain **different intensities within a stable whole**:

- convivial rooms;
- quiet rooms;
- private rooms;
- work rooms;
- transitional spaces;
- places of ceremony;
- places of retreat.

Repose is the baseline condition that allows these differences to exist without turning the entire dwelling into a continuous stimulus field.

**Source:** Graham, Gosling & Travis (2015), “The Psychology of Home Environments: A Call for Research on Residential Space”, *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 10(3). DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615576761

## 14. Neuroarchitecture: methodological caution

“Neuroarchitecture” is a useful name for work at the boundary of neuroscience, cognition and architecture. It is not yet a licence to translate every biometric difference into a design command.

Recent systematic reviews find rapid growth but substantial methodological heterogeneity, heavy dependence on EEG and self-report, limited standardisation and many studies that remain descriptive rather than explanatory.

The doctrine should therefore adopt three rules:

1. **Prefer convergent evidence.** A plausible design proposition supported by environmental psychology, building science, post-occupancy evidence and neurophysiology is stronger than an isolated brain-imaging result.
2. **Do not confuse measurable response with meaningful outcome.** A statistically different neural or physiological signal is not automatically better, healthier or architecturally relevant.
3. **Do not jump scales.** Evidence about abstract shapes, images or short VR exposures should not silently become a claim about living in a room for twenty years.

**Sources:**

- Naghibi Rad et al. (2023), “Exploring Methodological Approaches of Experimental Studies in the Field of Neuroarchitecture: A Systematic Review”, *HERD* 16(2), 284–309. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/19375867221133135
- Sedes et al. (2026), “Toward evidence-based neuroarchitecture: a systematic mapping and framework for immersive spatial cognition”, *Frontiers in Psychology*: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1919278/full
- “A comprehensive review of neuroarchitecture measurement tools and methods” (2026), *Building and Environment* 289, 114103. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2025.114103

# 15. Doctrine translation

The research supports the constituent concerns behind a governing principle of **repose**. Repose itself is a doctrine term: a synthesis of environmental performance, control, privacy, comprehensibility and architectural judgement rather than a single validated psychological measure.

The principle should not prescribe a style. It should create a set of architectural burdens of proof.

## 15.1 Structural settlement

Prefer a house whose principal masses appear supported and at rest.

Ask:

- can a lay observer form a plausible account of how this appears to stand?
- does a major element appear to float, teeter or depend on improbably slight support?
- is structural drama an intentional local event or the permanent background condition?

## 15.2 Spatial coherence

The house should be comprehensible without becoming simplistic.

Ask:

- can occupants form a stable mental model of the plan?
- are principal routes, thresholds and room relationships intelligible?
- is hierarchy readable?
- does visual richness resolve into larger order?

## 15.3 Graded exposure

Provide choices about privacy and social contact.

Ask:

- can principal private rooms genuinely become private?
- can someone withdraw without leaving the household?
- can occupants enjoy outlook without unavoidable exposure?
- are acoustic and visual privacy considered together?

## 15.4 Local agency

Occupants should be able to alter ordinary environmental conditions directly.

Ask:

- can light, shade, ventilation and temperature be adjusted intelligibly?
- can a noisy or visually intrusive condition be shut out?
- is there a manual fallback where automation materially affects comfort?
- does the occupant understand what a control will do?

## 15.5 Sensory quiet where quiet matters

Bedrooms and retreat spaces deserve particular protection.

Ask:

- is external and internal noise controlled?
- are plant and plumbing noises kept away from sleep spaces?
- can darkness be achieved?
- are glare and unwanted night lighting controllable?
- does the ventilation strategy preserve comfort without forcing a choice between fresh air and intolerable noise where practical?

## 15.6 Daylight without exposure or overheating

Ask:

- does the room receive useful daylight?
- can direct sun and glare be moderated?
- is the view worth having?
- does glazing compromise privacy, acoustics or summer comfort?
- can an occupant control the boundary?

## 15.7 Richness within order

Do not confuse repose with minimalism.

Ask:

- is there hierarchy across scales?
- can ornament, material variation and occupation accumulate without destroying coherence?
- are technical elements visually subordinate unless deliberately celebrated?
- does detail reward attention rather than demand it?

## 15.8 Deliberate intensity

Strong contrast, surprise, compression, release or unusual geometry can enrich a house when used deliberately.

The ordinary domestic background should not depend on continual novelty, exposure, disorientation or apparent structural improbability. Where a space is intentionally more intense, the design should be clear about where that effect begins and ends.

# 16. Relationship to existing doctrine

Repose is not a separate decorative agenda. It strengthens existing principles.

### Selective permanence

Enduring rooms, axes, stairs, openings and substantial boundaries can provide a stable spatial order against which faster-changing occupation occurs.

### Passive-first architecture

Operable windows, shading, mass and useful daylight are not merely energy strategies. They can increase intelligible local agency and reduce dependence on invisible correction.

### Tectonic honesty

Construction that appears coherent with material and support can reduce perceptual ambiguity. Concealment remains legitimate; falsification remains undesirable.

### Maintenance geography

Technical work should not dominate principal rooms. Maintainability should not make ordinary occupation feel like continuous interaction with building systems.

### Replaceable interiors

Access seams, panels and service routes must not generate gratuitous visual complexity. Maintainability succeeds when ordinary occupation remains architecturally settled.

### Workmanship robustness

Execution matters. Misaligned datums, wavering joints, rattling floors, loose trims and visibly unresolved interfaces can create persistent visual, acoustic or tactile disturbance even when the underlying technical system is sound.

---

# 17. Design review test

Every major design review should include a **repose pass** separate from technical compliance.

For each principal room and approach sequence ask:

1. **Gravity** — does the architecture appear settled?
2. **Comprehension** — can the space and its relationships be understood readily?
3. **Exposure** — can occupants regulate privacy and retreat?
4. **Agency** — can important immediate conditions be controlled directly?
5. **Sensory load** — are noise, glare, heat, visual competition and technical activity proportionate to the room?
6. **Richness** — is there enough interest without unresolved competition?
7. **Recovery** — where can a tired, ill or overstimulated occupant withdraw?
8. **Technology** — does the house serve without continually announcing its systems?
9. **Intensity** — where are surprise or tension intentional, and where should they subside?

This review is qualitative unless a claim can be tested quantitatively.

Measured environmental performance remains separate and mandatory.

---

# 18. Research agenda

The structural-legibility hypothesis deserves direct testing rather than repeated assertion.

A useful study could compare otherwise matched domestic interiors with controlled variation in apparent structural support:

- conventional bearing;
- visually ambiguous transfer;
- pronounced cantilever;
- concealed but intuitively plausible support;
- arch/lintel variants where style and ornament are controlled.

Outcome measures should prioritise:

- perceived stability;
- tension / ease;
- hominess;
- preference;
- willingness to remain in the room;
- eye-tracking or physiological measures only where they answer a defined question.

The experiment should distinguish **engineering knowledge** from **naive perception** and should test architects separately from lay participants.

Until such work exists, structural repose remains a reasoned design hypothesis—not a claimed physiological fact.

---

# 19. Editorial guardrails

The public manuscript should use formulations such as:

- “the doctrine proposes”;
- “the evidence supports”;
- “research suggests”;
- “a plausible architectural inference is”;
- “the reference house therefore prefers”.

Avoid:

- “the brain needs”;
- “humans evolved to prefer”;
- “this form reduces cortisol”;
- “this geometry is natural”;
- “traditional architecture is neurologically correct”;
- “cantilevers cause stress”.

The doctrine gains authority by being explicit about what is known, what is inferred and what remains to be tested.
