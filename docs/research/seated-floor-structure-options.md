# Seated Floor Structure — Options Appraisal

**Status:** current structural-interface option appraisal / pre-engineering  
**Classification:** **Seated Floor Structure is an implementation challenger, not a canonical HSA pattern.** The reusable architectural knowledge is captured by [Decompose Structural Interface Functions](../patterns/strategies/decompose-structural-interface-functions.md).  
**Purpose:** determine whether the project's instinct — support by bearing, restrain only where restraint is required — should produce a genuinely non-standard floor-to-wall connection or simply a more disciplined use of established timber-floor details.

## Executive position

The **principle survives strongly**.

The evidence does **not** currently justify inventing a special sliding or loosely seated joist connection.

The strongest present interpretation is:

> **Decompose the floor-to-wall interface into gravity support, joist stability, diaphragm action, wall restraint, movement allowance, fire/acoustic closure and finish — then use the simplest certified connection family that satisfies each function.**

For the Reference House, an engineered restraint-type masonry hanger or another standard engineered hanger remains the baseline to beat.

A custom bearing ledge / seat should advance only if it produces a demonstrable advantage in inspection, replacement, tolerance, acoustic isolation or architectural coordination.

The project no longer needs **Seated Floor Structure** as a pattern identity. The general proposition belongs at strategy level; direct bearing, restraint hangers and custom seats are competing implementation families beneath it.

# 1. What regulation already tells us

Approved Document A treats **vertical support and lateral restraint as related but separable requirements**.

For certain houses of not more than two storeys, tension straps in the longitudinal direction of joists may be omitted where joists:

- are at not more than 1.2 m centres; and
- have at least 90 mm bearing on the supported wall, or 75 mm bearing on a timber wall plate at each end.

Approved Document A also recognises restraint-type joist hangers as an alternative means of providing lateral support, and otherwise illustrates tension straps fixed across multiple joists at intervals not exceeding 2 m.

This is important conceptually.

The floor does not need to be understood as “every joist rigidly screwed to the wall”. It needs:

1. a safe vertical load path;
2. adequate floor-member stability;
3. the required transfer of horizontal actions;
4. the required restraint of the masonry;
5. the required diaphragm behaviour.

Those functions may be delivered by different pieces of hardware.

**Source:** UK Government, *Approved Document A — Structure*, Diagram 15 / paragraph 2C35.  
https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/structure-approved-document-a

# 2. Engineered timber is already dimensionally favourable

TRADA notes that timber I-joists have better dimensional stability than solid wood and therefore shrink or swell less with changing ambient conditions.

This matters because the project should not invent a large movement mechanism for a movement that can already be reduced materially by the choice of structural product.

The Reference House direction remains:

- engineered I-joist or other engineered timber floor;
- avoid vulnerable timber ends built deep into masonry;
- coordinate service penetrations before construction;
- use movement allowance where evidence shows it is needed rather than making looseness a virtue.

# 3. Standard restraint hangers already embody part of the strategy

Commercial restraint-type masonry hangers already separate several functions more intelligently than a naive “fixed joist” description suggests.

For example, Simpson Strong-Tie Safety Fast masonry restraint hangers:

- support timber or I-joists from masonry;
- provide defined vertical support;
- use separate restraint straps for lateral restraint;
- permit a small installation gap/tolerance at the joist end;
- are certified connection products rather than bespoke structural hardware.

This is not an endorsement of one manufacturer. It demonstrates that the market already contains **ordinary components in arrangements very close to the HSA strategy**.

The project should prefer exploiting a standard certified connection before designing a custom steel shoe whose future calculation, fabrication and approval all become project-specific liabilities.

# 4. Option set

## Option A — Restraint-type masonry hanger

### Description

Engineered timber joists terminate at the masonry inner leaf and are supported by certified masonry hangers. The hanger/strap family provides the required support and selected lateral restraint.

### Strengths

- conventional UK practice;
- certified hardware and published load data;
- timber not embedded into masonry;
- clear load path;
- compatible with engineered I-joists;
- comparatively little custom design;
- ordinary future structural engineer can recognise the system;
- replacement hardware likely to remain reproducible even if an exact product disappears.

### Weaknesses

- hanger is usually built into or closely integrated with masonry;
- full future joist replacement remains difficult;
- restraint and support remain somewhat bundled in one product family;
- steelwork at the floor edge must be detailed against corrosion/fire/acoustic issues;
- may provide less movement decoupling than a deliberately seated arrangement.

### Current judgement

**Baseline / preferred comparison case.**

Any non-standard proposal must demonstrate a clear whole-life advantage over this.

---

## Option B — Direct bearing on masonry or wall plate + separate restraint

### Description

Joists achieve gravity support through direct bearing, with separate straps, blocking and/or restraint details providing horizontal stability.

### Strengths

- structurally legible;
- closest literal expression of “support by bearing”;
- Approved Document A recognises bearing as part of accepted domestic restraint arrangements in defined conditions;
- gravity support can remain independent of restraint hardware.

### Weaknesses

- vulnerable timber/masonry moisture relationship if detailed badly;
- 90 mm-class bearing can consume geometry;
- joist ends can become difficult to inspect;
- acoustic flanking and fire closure still need full design;
- direct bearing does not automatically make later replacement easy;
- wall-plate variant introduces another long-lived timber component and its own permanent fixings.

### Current judgement

**Worth keeping as an engineered option, but not obviously superior to Option A.**

Do not embed untreated/vulnerable timber deep in masonry merely to satisfy the aesthetic purity of bearing.

---

## Option C — Continuous steel bearing ledge / replaceable shoe

### Description

A deliberately engineered steel ledge, angle or shoe is fixed to the permanent masonry. Joists bear onto the metal interface and are separately captured/restraint-connected.

### Strengths

- clearest separation of permanent wall and floor member;
- bearing can be visually/technically legible;
- potentially easier joist-end inspection;
- can create a controlled tolerance datum;
- could make individual support hardware locally replaceable;
- potentially useful where the same floor edge also coordinates service or acoustic geometry.

### Weaknesses

- numerous high-load anchors into the permanent masonry;
- potentially large bespoke steel interface;
- corrosion/fire protection burden;
- acoustic bridging risk;
- dimensional tolerances become demanding;
- thermal implications at external wall interfaces must be checked;
- higher cost and design liability;
- may solve a theoretical replacement problem that remains physically impossible because deck, partitions and ceilings still trap the joist.

### Current judgement

**Experimental. No current evidence that the benefit justifies the custom interface.**

Only progress if the wall/floor-edge prototype shows a real additional serviceability benefit.

---

## Option D — Timber ledger / wall plate

### Description

A timber ledger or wall plate fixed to masonry provides the bearing datum; joists attach or bear from it.

### Strengths

- simple carpentry;
- adjustable and understandable;
- replaceable in principle;
- easy to fix timber-to-timber.

### Weaknesses

- many permanent masonry anchors;
- timber becomes a semi-permanent structural interface;
- moisture/corrosion interface around fixings;
- fire and acoustic edge detail;
- ledger replacement could be extremely disruptive;
- not obviously better than a certified hanger.

### Current judgement

**Low priority for the Reference House.**

# 5. The movement question

The project should be precise about what movement it actually wants.

Potential relative movements include:

- timber moisture movement;
- long-term joist deflection;
- floor vibration;
- masonry shrinkage/creep;
- construction tolerance;
- differential movement between floor deck, ceiling and masonry finish.

Not all of these belong at the joist bearing.

For engineered I-joists, longitudinal moisture movement is relatively limited. Floor deflection belongs primarily in the span. Ceiling/wall cracking may be better resolved at the **finish interface** than by making the structural bearing itself slide.

Therefore:

> **Do not put movement into the structural connection merely because the architectural doctrine values movement accommodation. Put each movement where it can occur safely and usefully.**

A small joist-end tolerance, ordinary connector deformation, a slip detail in the ceiling/cornice and independent wall lining may together solve the real problem more elegantly than a literal sliding bearing.

---

# 6. Replaceability reality check

The most important red-team point is that **replaceable bearing hardware does not make a joist replaceable by itself**.

An individual floor joist may still be trapped by:

- structural decking;
- partitions;
- ceilings;
- services;
- blocking;
- restraint straps;
- adjacent joists;
- stairs/openings;
- floor-platform support geometry.

The Reference House should therefore distinguish three different ambitions:

### Inspectable

The end condition can be understood and surveyed where deterioration is plausible.

### Repairable

Local hardware, blocking or edge components can be repaired without masonry demolition.

### Fully replaceable

An entire primary joist can actually be withdrawn and replaced.

The third is much more demanding and may not be proportionate.

A seated implementation should **not claim full joist replaceability unless the whole floor assembly proves it**.

---

# 7. Current Reference House direction

Proceed to structural-engineer review with the following hierarchy:

### Baseline

**Certified restraint-type hanger + engineered I-joist + explicit floor-edge movement/finish detail.**

### Comparator

**Direct/seated bearing + separate wall-restraint strategy.**

### Experimental challenger

**Continuous or discrete engineered bearing shoe / ledge.**

The engineer should compare them against:

- gravity capacity;
- wall restraint;
- diaphragm transfer;
- robustness;
- joist stability;
- construction sequence;
- acoustic flanking;
- fire;
- corrosion;
- inspection;
- realistic repair/replacement;
- number and permanence of masonry fixings;
- cost.

Unless Option C wins materially on those criteria, **do not invent it**.

---

# 8. Classification outcome

The stable HSA knowledge is:

> **Make gravity support explicit, distinguish restraint from support, and provide only the movement freedom that has a demonstrated structural or constructional purpose.**

That proposition is now carried by the strategy [Decompose Structural Interface Functions](../patterns/strategies/decompose-structural-interface-functions.md).

The seated connection remains one implementation challenger. The Reference House may ultimately use completely conventional certified hardware if it gives the better structural, fire, acoustic, construction and whole-life result.

# 9. Required next work

1. Structural-engineer options study using actual house spans and masonry geometry.
2. Retain engineered I-joists as the working baseline while comparing metal-web joists once spans, vibration, depth and cost are known.
3. Define floor diaphragm action independently of any removable finish/access layers.
4. Develop calculable versions of the alternatives already coordinated in [Vertical Bay Coordination](../reference-house/vertical-bay-coordination.md) and [Vertical Bay Options](../reference-house/vertical-bay-options.md).
5. Build a 1:1 structural edge challenger only after the engineer has reduced the comparison to credible geometries.
