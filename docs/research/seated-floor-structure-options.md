# Floor-to-Masonry Structural Interface

The architectural proposition is stronger than any particular connection detail:

Make gravity support explicit; distinguish restraint from support; provide only the movement freedom that has a demonstrated structural or constructional purpose.
That proposition is captured by [Decompose Structural Interface Functions](../patterns/strategies/decompose-structural-interface-functions.md). A seated or bearing connection is only one possible implementation beneath it.

For the Reference House, current evidence does **not** justify inventing a special sliding or loosely seated joist connection. A conventional certified engineered connection remains the baseline to beat.

## Separate the jobs

The floor-to-wall junction may need to provide several different functions:

- gravity support;
- floor-member stability;
- diaphragm transfer;
- wall restraint;
- robustness;
- construction tolerance;
- movement accommodation;
- fire closure;
- acoustic separation;
- durability;
- inspection and repair.

One component may legitimately perform several of these jobs, but the relationship should be understood explicitly rather than described simply as “the joist is fixed to the wall”.

Approved Document A is useful conceptually because domestic masonry guidance already treats vertical bearing and lateral restraint as related but separable requirements. In defined conditions it recognises direct bearing, straps and restraint-type hangers as different parts of a structural solution.

That does not prove a Reference House detail. It does support the architectural distinction between **support** and **restraint**.

## Engineered timber and movement

Engineered I-joists remain a plausible repeating floor family because they are dimensionally more stable than solid timber and belong to mature UK construction systems with established connection hardware.

That matters to the movement strategy. The first response to foreseeable movement should often be to reduce unnecessary movement through material and system choice rather than invent a large slip mechanism afterward.

Potential relative movements at the floor edge include:

- timber moisture movement;
- long-term floor deflection;
- vibration;
- masonry shrinkage or creep;
- construction tolerance;
- relative movement between deck, ceiling and wall finish.

Not all of those belong at the joist bearing.

A small joist-end tolerance, ordinary connector deformation, correctly designed support and restraint, and a separate wall/ceiling movement detail may resolve the real condition more effectively than a literal sliding bearing.

Put each movement where it can occur safely and usefully.

## Option A — certified restraint-type masonry hanger

### Arrangement

Engineered timber joists terminate at the masonry inner leaf and are supported by a certified hanger family. Associated straps or other designed components provide the required restraint.

### Strengths

- conventional UK practice;
- certified hardware and published design data;
- clear gravity load path;
- compatible with engineered timber;
- timber ends need not be embedded deeply in masonry;
- comparatively little project-specific invention;
- recognisable to future structural engineers and trades;
- exact proprietary products can change without destroying the basic connection family.

### Weaknesses

- the hanger remains closely integrated with the masonry edge;
- whole-joist replacement is still difficult;
- support and restraint may remain partly bundled within one hardware family;
- fire, acoustic, corrosion and edge-closure duties still require explicit design;
- it may provide less deliberate movement decoupling than some bearing arrangements.

### Position

**Current baseline.**

Any non-standard connection should demonstrate a clear whole-life or coordination advantage over excellent conventional hanger construction.

## Option B — direct or seated bearing with separate restraint

### Arrangement

The joist obtains gravity support through direct bearing on masonry or another engineered bearing datum, while separate straps, blocking or restraint details provide the required horizontal actions.

### Strengths

- structurally legible;
- literal separation between bearing and restraint is possible;
- gravity support can remain independent of some restraint hardware;
- can avoid conceiving the wall/floor junction as one monolithic fixing.

### Weaknesses

- timber/masonry moisture relationships become important;
- useful bearing length consumes geometry;
- joist ends may become difficult to inspect;
- fire and acoustic edge closure remain unresolved by bearing alone;
- direct bearing does not automatically create repairability or whole-joist replaceability;
- a wall-plate variant introduces its own semi-permanent timber interface and fixings.

### Position

**Credible engineered comparator, not an automatic improvement.**

Direct bearing should be selected only when the complete structural and constructional result is better, not because “bearing” is philosophically purer than a hanger.

## Option C — engineered steel ledge or shoe

### Arrangement

A continuous or discrete steel bearing interface is attached to permanent masonry. Joists bear onto the interface and receive separate capture or restraint where required.

### Potential advantages

- clear separation between permanent wall and replaceable floor member;
- a precise repeatable bearing datum;
- potentially improved joist-end inspection;
- potentially replaceable local support hardware;
- may coordinate tolerance, acoustic or other floor-edge duties in one engineered zone.

### Burdens

- numerous consequential anchors into permanent masonry;
- project-specific steel design;
- corrosion and fire-protection obligations;
- acoustic bridging risk;
- demanding dimensional coordination;
- possible thermal consequences at external walls;
- higher fabrication, approval and future-repair burden;
- danger of solving a theoretical replacement problem that deck, ceilings, partitions and services make impossible anyway.

### Position

**Experimental challenger only.**

It should not advance unless engineering demonstrates a material benefit that survives fire, acoustics, durability, construction sequence, inspection, cost and whole-life repairability.

## Option D — timber ledger or wall plate

### Arrangement

A timber ledger or wall plate fixed to the masonry provides the bearing datum, with joists attached or seated from it.

### Strengths

- familiar carpentry;
- easy timber-to-timber fixing;
- understandable geometry;
- some adjustment is possible.

### Weaknesses

- many permanent masonry anchors;
- a semi-permanent timber interface is created at the wall edge;
- moisture, fire and acoustic obligations remain;
- ledger replacement could be highly disruptive;
- no obvious whole-life advantage over a certified hanger family.

### Position

**Low-priority comparator.**

## Replaceability — three different claims

A replaceable support detail does not by itself make a primary joist replaceable.

A joist may still be trapped by:

- structural decking;
- partitions;
- ceilings;
- services;
- blocking and restraint;
- neighbouring members;
- stairs or openings;
- floor build-up and finish geometry.

The design should therefore distinguish:

### Inspectable

The end condition can be understood and surveyed where deterioration is plausible.

### Repairable

Local hardware, blocking or edge components can be repaired without masonry demolition.

### Fully replaceable

An entire primary joist can actually be withdrawn and replaced.

The third is much more demanding and may not be proportionate. No floor-edge proposal should imply whole-joist replaceability unless the complete assembly and withdrawal sequence prove it.

## Reference House comparison

The current hierarchy is:

1. **baseline — certified restraint-type hanger + engineered I-joist + explicit floor-edge and finish interfaces;**
2. **comparator — direct or seated bearing + separate restraint;**
3. **challenger — engineered bearing shoe or ledge only if it earns its additional machinery.**

The comparison should include:

- gravity capacity;
- wall restraint and diaphragm transfer;
- robustness and joist stability;
- construction sequence and tolerance;
- acoustic flanking;
- fire closure;
- corrosion and moisture durability;
- inspection;
- realistic repair and replacement;
- number and permanence of masonry fixings;
- cost and future recognisability.

The current architectural position remains fail-safe against novelty: if the challengers do not win materially, use the excellent conventional connection.

The physical proof question is developed in [Physical Prototypes — Structural floor edge](../prototypes/#structural-floor-edge), while [Vertical Interface Coordination](../reference-house/vertical-bay-coordination.md) records the wider wall/floor/ceiling boundary.
