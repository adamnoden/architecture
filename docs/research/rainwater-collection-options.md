# Rainwater collection: access and failure paths

Collection is the meeting of a roof or paved surface with its first controlled water route. Its position determines what happens when debris stops that route. The same gutter profile can spill harmlessly beyond an outer eave or discharge into a confined court beside a door. A comparison therefore needs the surrounding geometry as well as the component.

This study compares ordinary gravity collection families for the pre-site Reference House. The [rainfall and hydraulic basis](rainfall-and-hydraulic-basis.md) supplies the demand, operating-level and blockage questions. No gutter size, outlet count, fall, product, roof form or threshold level is selected here.

## Compare the physical collection families

The classifications below deliberately separate a **collecting geometry** from a **transfer fitting**. A valley may end in a hopper; a box gutter may use a roof outlet or scupper. Those combinations retain every interface and common failure path. Component labels alone cannot establish independence.

| Family | Normal path and useful attribute | Blockage / overflow question | Critical interfaces and renewal extent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exposed eaves gutter | Receives distributed edge runoff outside the enclosing roof/wall line; profile and outlets can remain visually legible | Does water spill away from fascia, wall and openings, or run behind the gutter? Roof-edge overshoot and a backed-up outlet must both be considered | Roof drip/underlay termination, brackets, joints and outlet. Local lengths may be replaceable if joints/supports are accessible; removal must not dismantle the permanent eaves boundary |
| Open valley and terminal receiver | Carries converging runoff from adjoining faces to a deliberate collecting edge or receiver | Does concentrated flow overshoot the receiver or back up under adjacent covering? Where does debris lodge at the change from valley to gutter/outlet? | Valley weathering belongs with the roof; receiver attachment, laps/transitions and local movement cross the collection seam. Replacement may disturb both roof margins, unlike a detached gutter length |
| Box or parapet gutter | Can collect behind an architectural edge and accept concentrated runoff with a deliberately designed section | It may store water against inner margins or wallheads when its outlet fails. Show a genuinely usable escape before water reaches vulnerable upstands, joints or fabric | Continuous lining, ends, corners, supports, outlets and movement joints. Repair can require lifting adjacent weathering/coping; a replaceable lining needs a real release and temporary protection sequence |
| Internal gutter through the building | May connect an otherwise inaccessible inner catchment to an exterior discharge | What declares leakage before roof space, insulation or permanent structure remains wet? Can blockage surcharge joints along the internal run? | Roof/structure crossings and lining continuity create high-consequence interfaces. Inspection, supported removal and replacement cannot depend on opening permanent fabric |
| Hopper receiver | Creates an inspectable transition from a valley/scupper/short open discharge to a downpipe; can expose backing-up water | Can incoming water be caught without overshoot? If the throat blocks, where does the hopper spill and what gets wet below? | Inlet trajectory, wall stand-off/support, throat and downpipe joint; local replacement is plausible only with ordinary release access and independent upstream support |
| Scupper / overflow opening | Carries water through an edge or wall, often making an emergency discharge visible | Is it the primary route or the emergency route? What head is needed, where does the stream land, and can the receiver or shared downstream pipe defeat it? | Penetration, sleeve/flange and wall/roof weathering are consequential. Replacing the body may disturb the water boundary even if a cover is removable |
| Roof outlet at a sump/low point | Collects locally into a pipe where surrounding falls physically lead to it | Inlet/guard obstruction, inadequate available head, or downstream backup may raise water against the surrounding roof boundary | Waterproofing flange/clamp or bonded transition, body support and pipe connection. Cleaning the grate is a different task from replacing the embedded body and reinstating the membrane |
| Court point drain/gully | Concentrates paving runoff at a local low point; a lifting grate can expose the debris trap | Do actual falls reach it without ponding at doors? If the single low point stops accepting water, where is the court's surface escape? | Grate/seat, sediment trap, body, paving/waterproofing and pipe connection. Basket cleaning may be local; body renewal can require surrounding floor work |
| Court linear channel | Intercepts a line of surface flow, potentially before a threshold or along a low edge | Does flow enter the channel, bypass its ends, or pass over a blocked grate? Several channel inlets to one outlet remain vulnerable to that one obstruction | Channel joints/end caps/outlet, bearing support, finish level and any waterproofing connection. Lifting covers must expose the actual cleaning path; narrow slots can require separate access points |

None of these is automatically a complete drainage system. In particular, an open facade channel laid above a waterproofed substrate may discharge laterally into that layer. Treating it as a sealed pipe-like carrier can leave the real gully, membrane and lower drainage route missing from the design.

## Access, appearance and maintenance burden

| Arrangement | Inspection and cleaning task | Architectural / occupation cost | Evidence needed before preferring it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exposed outer eaves | Observe during rain; obtain safe access to reach the full gutter and outlet; remove debris and inspect supports/joints | Visible line and downpipe connection; work may need external equipment and clear ground | Working position, tool reach, safe component handling, facade clearance and benign spill territory |
| Inner eaves / valley corners | Reach the actual corner receiver and valley-to-collector throat, not just a long straight gutter | Court access may be constrained by walls, doors, occupation and equipment; visible goods affect the intimate elevation | Task sequence including safe access, debris removal and renewal; no assumption that a narrow court admits a tower or ladder |
| Behind a parapet/cornice or inside roof space | Open the inspection position, reach all debris points and inspect the lining without damaging it | Hidden collection may simplify an elevation while adding access openings, working space and repair disturbance | Full inspection route, visible failure before concealed harm, and replaceability of the lining/outlet; appearance alone does not justify concealment |
| Court point drain | Lift and safely place grate, extract basket/trap contents, inspect and clear onward throat | Local visible fitting; surface falls may complicate furniture or accessible approaches | Actual surface/threshold section, usable low point, lifting field and blockage escape |
| Court channel | Lift each necessary cover or reach from specified access points, remove silt, clear the outlet and refit | A longer visible cut or narrow slot; grates, paving inserts and furniture can obstruct work | Continuous cleaning access and handling mass/space; no furniture, fixed planter or threshold assembly permanently blocks first release |

A guard changes the maintenance task and inlet behaviour; it does not eliminate them. Fine debris, vegetation and ordinary missed cleaning remain challenge cases. No fixed inspection interval or arbitrary blockage percentage is selected. Exposure, nearby vegetation, component instructions and observed performance will determine the schedule. Greater access complexity is a real comparison cost even where sufficient hydraulic capacity could be obtained.

## Application to the current house

The accepted source is a pitched ring around an open court. Outer-eaves collection, inner-eaves collection and court-surface collection must retain separate identities. A failure may transfer water from one to another; that transfer is a scenario to assess, not an undisclosed normal design load.

The source roof-height function also makes the inner diagonal seams valleys rather than hips. For the south-west quadrant, the diagonal runs from the ridge corner toward the court corner; points to either side are higher once past the ridge. The other three corners are symmetric. This is a geometric inference from the current model, not a selected valley lining or flow calculation. The receiver comparison must therefore account for **concentrated arrivals at four inner corners**, alongside distributed flow from inner eaves. The eight drawn arrows do not imply eight equal catchments, four selected outlets or uniform inflow along the inner gutter.

Use three candidates for further coupled design:

- **Exposed collection as the ordinary comparator.** Outer eaves remain visible and locally renewable, with actual runoff capture and spill territories demonstrated. For inner eaves, add deliberate corner-receiver treatment and test the consequence below; exterior exposure to the court does not make a spill harmless.
- **Concealed edge collection as a conditional alternative.** A box/parapet arrangement must earn its concealed-water and access burden through real sections, outlet/overflow levels, renewal and failure territory. It is not prohibited by family name, but no architectural gain can be claimed without showing those costs.
- **Court surface point versus channel collection as an unresolved comparison.** Test both against actual finished falls, the two court doors, furniture/working space and a surface escape. A threshold channel can intercept local water without supplying a whole-court overflow. A point drain can be simple to clean while still leaving a trapped low point.

A high-level inner-roof transfer may avoid dropping that roof runoff into the lower court network. It needs a real route and does not remove rainfall directly onto the court. Conversely, a lower court drop still needs a continuous route through or beneath the surrounding building to the external handoff. This study supplies no invented cross-building fall, service void, invert or accepted site outlet.

## Handoff to coupled design

Each proposed receiver must carry the following information into the conveyance and failure comparison:

| Item | Required distinction |
|---|---|
| Contributing water | Surface/catchment identity, source revision, distributed versus concentrated arrival, effective-area basis and any transfer counted upstream |
| Capture geometry | Actual edge/valley/low-point geometry and available collecting section; concentrated overshoot and end bypass considered |
| Normal route | Collector, inlet and receiving connection with supported demand/capacity basis or an explicit unresolved requirement |
| Emergency route | Independent identity, obstruction being bypassed, physical receiving territory and any downstream common obstruction |
| Levels | Common datum for normal operation, overflow activation, **operating overflow level at required flow**, and first vulnerable boundary; tolerance/deflection and receiving conditions remain explicit |
| Maintenance and renewal | Inspection position, first release, tool/hand/debris-removal envelope, lifting/handling route, temporary protection and reinstated boundaries |
| Material and support seam | Actual roof/collector/runoff contact, movement and fixing duties for the shared material assessment; no inferred compatibility from material names |
| Rejection trigger | What would force a different receiver, split catchment, revised roof/threshold form or simpler visible arrangement |

The next decision is to compose these collection families with actual roof and conveyance candidates. Retain the exposed conventional control until a more involved arrangement shows a useful advantage. Collection may be hidden in the finished house, but its failure, cleaning and renewal paths must remain demonstrable. This comparison establishes those choices and tests; hydraulic acceptance and a selected house network remain unearned.
