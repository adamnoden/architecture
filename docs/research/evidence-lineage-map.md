# House Design Doctrine — Evidence and Intellectual Lineage Map v0.1

**Purpose:** Establish where the doctrine sits in existing architectural thought and professional practice before claims are rewritten for publication.

**Important:** This is not a novelty search and does not claim that any proposition is unprecedented. The aim is to distinguish inherited ideas, established professional practice, extensions, and the potentially distinctive synthesis of the doctrine.

## 1. Major intellectual lineages

### A. Long life, loose fit, low energy — Alex Gordon

**Existing idea:** Sir Alex Gordon, during/around his RIBA presidency in the early 1970s, argued for architecture characterised by “long life, loose fit, low energy.” The phrase remains active in UK architectural discourse and was revisited in the Architectural Design issue on loose-fit architecture.

**Relationship to the doctrine:** Strong ancestor. V7 shares the commitment to long life and future change, but is suspicious of “loose fit” when it becomes generic spatial indeterminacy. The doctrine proposes **selective permanence**: principal rooms, structural order and architectural relationships may be deliberately enduring while services and selected layers remain adaptable.

**Publication implication:** Gordon should appear early in the intellectual genealogy. The book should explain that it is not rejecting loose fit; it is narrowing and spatially locating change.

### B. Support / infill and Open Building — N. John Habraken

**Existing idea:** Habraken’s Supports theory and the Open Building tradition distinguish long-lived collective/support structure from shorter-lived infill and recognise stability and change as separate levels of intervention.

**Relationship to the doctrine:** Very close conceptual relative. V7’s permanent fabric / replaceable systems distinction belongs in this family of thought.

**Difference worth developing:** Habraken’s project is strongly concerned with user agency, mass housing and decision-making levels. V7 is developing a more owner-occupied domestic proposition centred on serviceability, maintenance, failure, material ageing and architectural expression. Its “architectural platform” should therefore be positioned as a descendant/extension, not presented as if support/infill separation has no precedent.

### C. Shearing layers / pace layers — Frank Duffy and Stewart Brand

**Existing idea:** Different parts of a building change at different rates. Duffy’s work and Brand’s later popularisation distinguish slower layers such as structure from faster layers such as services, space plan and contents.

**Relationship to the doctrine:** Directly relevant to V7’s lifespan hierarchy.

**Potential extension:** V7 does more than identify rates of change. It asks the architectural consequence: **what spatial, mechanical and documentary interfaces are required so the fast layer can actually be changed without consuming the slow layer?** This is a promising place to distinguish the doctrine.

### D. Service-life planning — ISO 15686 and BS 7543

**Existing professional framework:** ISO 15686 establishes a systematic framework for service-life planning of buildings and constructed assets. BS 7543 gives UK guidance on durability, design life and predicted service life of buildings and components.

**Relationship to the doctrine:** These standards give professional grounding to the idea that components have different service lives and that this should affect briefing and design.

**Publication implication:** The book should stop using approximate lifespan categories purely rhetorically. Where lifespans matter, it should use established service-life terminology and distinguish **design life, required service life, predicted service life, maintainability and obsolescence**.

### E. Design for disassembly and adaptability — ISO 20887 / circular design

**Existing professional framework:** ISO 20887 sets out principles and strategies for design for disassembly and adaptability. UKGBC circular-economy guidance likewise treats design for disassembly as a project-brief and design-stage concern.

**Relationship to the doctrine:** Supports reversible assembly, accessible interfaces, disassembly sequencing and future adaptation.

**Difference:** V7 is not fundamentally a material-recovery/circularity doctrine. Its first concern is preserving the building in use. Disassembly is valuable chiefly where it protects longer-lived architecture and makes repair/change possible. This distinction should be explicit.

### F. Design for maintenance — CIBSE Guide M and BS 8210

**Existing professional framework:** CIBSE Guide M explicitly addresses guidance for designers and maintainable building services; BS 8210 addresses facilities maintenance management, including planning, inspection and records.

**Relationship to the doctrine:** Strong grounding for access, maintainability, inspection, documentation and lifecycle thinking.

**Potential extension:** V7’s **maintenance geography** is more architectural than conventional maintenance guidance: it proposes that maintenance routes and workspaces shape the plan and section from the outset. This term/concept deserves targeted literature research.

### G. Soft Landings / performance feedback — BSRIA

**Existing professional framework:** Soft Landings carries project intent from briefing through design, construction, handover, initial aftercare and post-occupancy evaluation.

**Relationship to the doctrine:** Strong precedent for commissioning as an integrated process, measured baseline performance and early post-occupancy review.

**Publication implication:** V7 should avoid implying that integrated commissioning/aftercare is novel. Its contribution is to combine performance commissioning with **serviceability and failure-state commissioning**—for example testing drainage, containment, isolation, removal sequences and access.

### H. Building logbooks / passports and durable information

**Existing practice:** CIBSE provides building-logbook templates; building passports and “golden thread” concepts have become increasingly prominent in UK and European building information practice.

**Relationship to the doctrine:** Strong precedent for the building passport.

**Potential extension:** V7’s insistence that the building should remain understandable **even if the digital record is lost** is more distinctive. Physical labels, matching route identifiers and durable diagrams should be framed as an analogue-resilience layer complementing—not replacing—digital building information.

### I. RIBA Plan of Work

**Existing professional framework:** The RIBA Plan of Work structures briefing, design, technical coordination, construction, handover and use across Stages 0–7.

**Relationship to the doctrine:** It should become the delivery scaffold for the project-specific implementation guide, not the conceptual structure of the monograph.

**Publication implication:** Patterns and requirements can later be tagged with the stage at which they must be resolved. This is one of the most direct ways to reduce “transpilation” for a UK architect.

## 2. What currently appears inherited vs distinctive

### Clearly part of established territory

- different building parts have different lifespans;
- long-lived support/structure should be distinguished from shorter-lived infill/services;
- adaptability and disassembly should be considered at design stage;
- maintenance access should be designed rather than improvised;
- commissioning and post-occupancy review improve building performance;
- maintenance records and building logbooks are valuable;
- passive architectural measures should precede unnecessary mechanical complexity;
- water management should use drainage, drying and redundancy rather than faith in perfect seals;
- acoustic, fire and other boundaries require coordinated service penetrations.

These should be cited as established foundations rather than asserted as inventions.

### Potentially distinctive synthesis requiring further research

The following appear to give the doctrine its own voice. They should be researched carefully before any originality claim is made:

1. **Maintenance geography** as a first-class architectural/planning concept linking utility entry, plant, risers, wet rooms, undercroft, roof access and service routes.
2. **Selective permanence** as an explicit alternative to both entangled conventional construction and generic loose-fit architecture.
3. **Designed interfaces as architectural language**: movement, access, wear and replacement interfaces deliberately expressed through cornices, thresholds, trims, panels and metalwork.
4. **Failure visibility as architecture**: passive containment and visible tell-tales designed into the building rather than monitoring treated primarily as an electronic problem.
5. **Physical self-documentation**: the building remaining intelligible through permanent labels, route identifiers and diagrams if external/digital records disappear.
6. **Commodity components inside architecturally bespoke but remanufacturable enclosures/interfaces** as a way of reconciling beauty with long-term repairability.
7. **The combination of undercroft + service spine + service wall + accessible riser in a compact high-quality domestic house**, if the engineering and spatial case survives scrutiny.
8. **Failure-state design review** applied systematically to domestic architecture: leak, blockage, component replacement, lost documentation, service outage, future alteration, etc.
9. **Classical/period architecture as a vehicle for visible maintainability**, rather than treating adaptability and technical legibility as requiring a modern/industrial aesthetic.

The publishable contribution may ultimately be the integration of these nine ideas rather than any one isolated invention.

## 3. Professional frameworks we should actively use

The final work should not merely cite these sources in a bibliography. It should borrow their professional vocabulary where useful.

### Service-life language

Use ISO 15686 / BS 7543 concepts to sharpen discussion of lifespan and durability.

### Disassembly/adaptability language

Use ISO 20887 to test reversible assembly and adaptability claims, while keeping the doctrine’s in-use repair focus distinct from circular end-of-life recovery.

### Maintainability language

Use CIBSE Guide M / BS 8210 to test working clearance, access, replacement, safe maintenance, records and inspection.

### Delivery stages

Use RIBA Plan of Work tags in the implementation document and perhaps in the pattern catalogue.

### Commissioning/aftercare

Use Soft Landings as the professional baseline, then identify additional doctrine-specific commissioning tests.

### Building information

Compare the proposed building passport and physical self-documentation with CIBSE logbooks and current building-passport practice.

## 4. Claims that should be reframed now

### “No service is permitted inside permanent fabric.”

Keep as a memorable governing rule, but define “inside,” “service,” “permanent fabric,” deliberate crossings and unavoidable embedded systems carefully. The published version should withstand trivial counterexamples.

### “Longevity through serviceability.”

Keep. But distinguish **durability** (resistance to degradation) from **serviceability/maintainability** (ability to inspect, repair and replace) and from **functional longevity** (remaining useful).

### “The permanent architecture should remain stable precisely because change has been given deliberate places to occur.”

Promote. This is a strong summary of the relationship between the doctrine and earlier building-layer thinking.

### “Commodity repairability.”

Keep as a doctrine term, but investigate conflicts with proprietary systems that deliver materially better safety/performance. It should be a decision preference rather than a fetish for generic components.

### “Building passport.”

Keep, but acknowledge established logbook/passport practice. The original contribution is the pairing of digital records with physical self-documentation and loss-of-record resilience.

### “Commissioning.”

Expand beyond installed-system performance into **maintainability commissioning**: remove/reinstall repeated panels, prove removal paths, pour-test passive containment, operate isolation, inspect controlled crossings, verify labels against actual routes, etc.

## 6. Research direction from here

The next evidence work should become **claim-specific**, not broad reading. For each future chapter/pattern we should create a small evidence dossier:

- established principle / standard;
- relevant research;
- historical or contemporary precedent;
- known failure modes;
- counterarguments/trade-offs;
- questions requiring specialist modelling or prototyping;
- implication for the doctrine.

Priority dossiers should be:

1. lifespan hierarchy and selective permanence;
2. maintenance geography;
3. accessible service voids / undercroft;
4. designed movement interfaces;
5. water containment + visible tell-tales;
6. removable floor systems;
7. service skirting and vertical joinery routes;
8. window/door replacement interfaces;
9. building self-documentation;
10. commissioning for maintainability.

This is the point at which the work should become progressively more like architectural research and less like editorial restructuring.
