# Repose — Evidence Audit

**Status:** checked against primary papers, systematic reviews and current housing guidance; October 2026.  
**Purpose:** control the claims used in Principle 8 and the surrounding manuscript.  
**Doctrine term:** **repose** means a domestic design objective: reduce unnecessary demands for monitoring, interpretation or corrective action. It is not a validated clinical construct or a single experimentally measured outcome.

## Claim register

| Claim used by the doctrine | Evidence position | Permitted public wording | Do not claim | Principal sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Poor thermal conditions, noise, air quality and visual discomfort can materially impair domestic comfort and health | **Strong / established** | Physical comfort is the first condition of repose; overheating, cold, noise, poor air quality and disabling glare must be addressed directly | That architectural composition can compensate for poor environmental performance | WHO *Housing and Health Guidelines* (2018); WHO *Environmental Noise Guidelines for the European Region* (2018); Homes England *Healthy Homes* (2026) |
| Occupant control can affect satisfaction with indoor conditions | **Supported** | Give occupants intelligible and effective means of adjusting relevant local conditions | That every variable must be manually controlled; that control guarantees comfort | Hellwig (2015); Lechner, Moosmann, Wagner & Schweiker (2021); Homes England *Healthy Homes* (2026) |
| Privacy in housing is multidimensional and materially related to spatial organisation and resident experience | **Supported, context-sensitive** | A dwelling should provide means of regulating exposure and withdrawing where the programme requires it | A universal preferred degree of enclosure or one culturally neutral privacy geometry | Macedo, Ornstein & Elali (2022), systematic review of 71 eligible articles |
| Coherence, fascination and hominess are meaningful dimensions of responses to architectural interiors | **Supported for image-based evaluation** | Repose need not mean low visual richness; coherence and fascination can coexist | That these dimensions are health outcomes, or that a numerical optimum is known for lived dwellings | Coburn et al. (2020), n=798 plus independent replication n=614 |
| Daylight may have restorative effects indoors | **Promising but mixed** | Useful daylight is desirable, balanced against glare, heat, privacy and noise | That more daylight is always better; that daylight reliably produces restoration in every context | Jamrozik et al. (2024), systematic review of 33 studies; 49% of examined relationships were null |
| Humans rapidly infer physical properties such as support and stability from visual scenes | **Strong perceptual evidence** | People perceive support, stability and gravity without formal structural reasoning | That a particular architectural expression is universally preferred | Bi & Yildirim (2026), review of intuitive-physics research |
| Apparent structural equilibrium contributes to domestic repose | **Doctrine hypothesis** | Principal domestic spaces should normally avoid gratuitous apparent precariousness; where structure is expressed, support should read plausibly | That cantilevers cause stress, arches reduce stress, or perceptual structural legibility has demonstrated long-term physiological effects in homes | Architectural inference from Bi & Yildirim (2026); direct domestic evidence remains insufficient |
| Prospect-refuge provides a universal recipe for comfortable interiors | **Not established** | Protected outlook and controllable exposure may be useful project-specific questions | Prospect-refuge as an evolutionary law or fixed interior geometry | Dosen & Ostwald (2016), meta-analysis with mixed evidence, especially for refuge |
| Curvature is inherently calming or safer than rectilinear form | **Not established** | Curvature is an aesthetic variable that may affect preference | “Sharp corners activate threat”; curvature as a health prescription | Vartanian et al. (2013): curvature affected beauty judgments but not approach/avoidance decisions |
| “Neuroarchitecture” can currently translate brain measurements directly into design rules | **Not established** | Neuroscience can contribute evidence, but translation to architecture requires convergent methods and scale-appropriate inference | Treating EEG/fMRI/EDA differences as self-validating architectural prescriptions | Naghibi Rad et al. (2023); Sedes et al. (2026); *Building and Environment* measurement-methods review (2026) |
| Homes support different psychological ambiances in different rooms | **Exploratory** | A dwelling need not impose one emotional register on every room | A universal room-by-room emotional formula | Graham, Gosling & Travis (2015) |

## Public-writing rules

The manuscript may say:

- **the evidence establishes** for physical environmental hazards and performance where the cited guidance/review is direct;
- **research supports** for perceived control, privacy and the identified interior-response dimensions;
- **research suggests** for daylight restoration and other mixed literatures;
- **the doctrine proposes** or **the reference house prefers** for apparent structural equilibrium and other architectural inferences.

The manuscript should not use brain-language merely to strengthen a weak claim. In particular, avoid formulations such as “the brain needs”, “humans evolved to prefer”, “activates the amygdala therefore”, “natural geometry”, “neurologically correct”, or quantified stress claims unsupported by direct evidence.

## Source checks

### Environmental conditions

**World Health Organization (2018), *WHO Housing and Health Guidelines*.**  
https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241550376  
Supports recommendations concerning crowding, low and high indoor temperature, injury and accessibility, and consolidates WHO guidance relevant to air quality and neighbourhood noise.

**World Health Organization (2018), *Environmental Noise Guidelines for the European Region*.**  
https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789289053563  
Supports the treatment of environmental noise, including night noise, as a substantive health and sleep issue.

**Homes England (updated 2026), *Healthy Homes — a foundation for healthier and resilient communities*.**  
https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/healthy-homes/healthy-homes-a-foundation-for-healthier-and-resilient-communities  
Current England-specific guidance organised around inclusivity, amenity, efficiency, comfort and control. Relevant sections explicitly address overheating, ventilation/IAQ, daylight, aspect/views, external and internal noise, logical plan arrangements, dimmable lighting, temperature controls and retreat space.

### Control

**Hellwig, R. T. (2015), “Perceived control in indoor environments: a conceptual approach”, *Building Research & Information* 43(3), 302–315.**  
https://doi.org/10.1080/09613218.2015.1004150  
Conceptual synthesis: perceived control is associated with occupant satisfaction; draws on adaptive comfort, post-occupancy studies and environmental psychology.

**Lechner, S.; Moosmann, C.; Wagner, A.; Schweiker, M. (2021), “Does thermal control improve visual satisfaction?”, *Indoor Air* 31(6), 2329–2349.**  
https://doi.org/10.1111/ina.12851  
Repeated-measures laboratory study (61 participants; 986 sessions). Self-perceived thermal control moderated the relationship between thermal satisfaction and overall satisfaction. Use as supporting evidence, not a universal home-control law.

### Interior response

**Coburn, A. et al. (2020), “Psychological and neural responses to architectural interiors”, *Cortex* 126, 217–241.**  
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2020.01.009  
Experiment 1: 798 participants rated 200 interior images; coherence, fascination and hominess explained 90% of variance in sixteen rating measures. Experiment 2 replicated the dimensional structure with 614 participants. Important limitation: image-based experimental evaluation is not equivalent to long-duration dwelling occupation.

### Privacy

**Macedo, P. F. de; Ornstein, S. W.; Elali, G. A. (2022), “Privacy and housing: research perspectives based on a systematic literature review”, *Journal of Housing and the Built Environment* 37, 653–683.**  
https://doi.org/10.1007/s10901-022-09939-z  
Systematic review of 71 eligible articles from 2000–2021. Supports treating privacy as physical, social and psychological and strongly cautions against culture-free universal prescriptions.

### Daylight and restoration

**Jamrozik et al. (2024), “Restorative effects of daylight in indoor environments — A systematic literature review”, *Journal of Environmental Psychology* 97, 102323.**  
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2024.102323  
Thirty-three studies; 67 unique tested relationships. Forty-nine per cent were null, 42% positive, 6% mixed/other and 3% negative. The correct doctrine conclusion is “promising and worth designing for alongside ordinary visual/thermal performance”, not “daylight is proven restorative”.

### Intuitive physics

**Bi, W. & Yildirim, I. (2026), “Visual Perception of Intuitive Physics and Its Computational Foundations”, *Annual Review of Vision Science* 12, 491–513.**  
https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-vision-110423-024138  
Review characterises visual intuitive physics as rapid, spontaneous and stimulus-driven and surveys perception of properties including stability and physical interaction. It is the evidential foundation for saying that observers perceive apparent structural stability. It does **not** establish the domestic-repose hypothesis.

### Claims deliberately kept out

**Dosen, A. S. & Ostwald, M. J. (2016), “Evidence for prospect-refuge theory: a meta-analysis…”, *City, Territory and Architecture* 3, 4.**  
https://doi.org/10.1186/s40410-016-0033-1

**Vartanian, O. et al. (2013), “Impact of contour on aesthetic judgments and approach-avoidance decisions in architecture”, *PNAS* 110(Suppl 2), 10446–10453.**  
https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1301227110

These sources are useful chiefly as guardrails against overstating attractive theories.

### Neuroarchitecture methodology

**Naghibi Rad, P. et al. (2023), “Exploring Methodological Approaches of Experimental Studies in the Field of Neuroarchitecture: A Systematic Review”, *HERD* 16(2), 284–309.**  
https://doi.org/10.1177/19375867221133135

**Sedes, F. et al. (2026), “Toward evidence-based neuroarchitecture: a systematic mapping and framework for immersive spatial cognition”, *Frontiers in Psychology*.**  
https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1919278

**“A comprehensive review of neuroarchitecture measurement tools and methods” (2026), *Building and Environment* 289, 114103.**  
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2025.114103

Together these justify methodological caution: the field is active, multimethod and increasingly sophisticated, but heterogeneous in stimuli, exposures, instruments and outcomes.

## Audit conclusion

No single paper proves the doctrine’s concept of repose. It should not pretend otherwise.

The defensible position is cumulative:

1. several physical environmental burdens are well established;
2. control, privacy and comprehensibility have meaningful supporting literatures;
3. architectural interiors can be described along dimensions including coherence, fascination and hominess;
4. human vision rapidly extracts physical stability and support;
5. the doctrine combines these into a domestic design objective—**minimise unnecessary vigilance**—while marking the step from evidence to architectural judgement whenever it occurs.

That separation is a condition of using the concept at all.
