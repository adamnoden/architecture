# House Design Doctrine v7 — Editorial and Publication Audit

**Status:** Working editorial analysis, not a rewrite of the doctrine.  
**Source:** `house-design-doctrine-v7(1).md` (21,511 words; 2,345 lines).  
**Purpose:** Identify the professional publication hidden inside v7 before rewriting it.

## 1. Editorial diagnosis

V7 is not principally suffering from weak prose. Its main problem is that several different document types are interleaved in one continuous hierarchy:

1. **Architectural proposition / thesis** — the house as a long-lived architectural platform.
2. **Governing doctrine** — principles intended to survive changes in technology and implementation.
3. **Technical guidance** — moisture, acoustics, fire, ventilation, service distribution, etc.
4. **Reusable architectural patterns** — service risers, service walls, skirting routes, designed penetrations, tell-tales, removable floor cassettes, etc.
5. **Reference implementation** — Georgian language, brass/bronze articulation, masonry wall concept, aluminium windows, engineered timber floors, raised plinth, and other current preferences.
6. **Practice / delivery method** — responsibility, standardisation, prototyping, procurement, commissioning, documentation and change control.
7. **Verification framework** — failure-state design review tests.
8. **Research backlog** — open design work.

All eight are useful. The publication problem is that v7 often gives them comparable rhetorical weight. The final work should preserve the material while giving each mode a distinct place and visual treatment.

The document already contains the crucial editorial distinction between **fixed principles**, **preferred implementations**, and **open design work**. The next version should retain that distinction but refine it into a more architectural hierarchy:

> **Doctrine → Strategy → Pattern → Reference implementation**

Supporting layers sit alongside that hierarchy:

> **Evidence / precedent → Verification → Open research**

This resolves a recurring ambiguity in v7: whether a proposition is intended to be universal, strongly preferred, merely one good way of implementing a principle, or simply the present design direction for the first house.

## 2. The central thesis

The strongest publishable proposition in v7 is:

> A long-lived house should be conceived as an architectural platform: permanent fabric establishes enduring spatial, structural and environmental order, while shorter-lived systems occupy accessible, replaceable and deliberately designed interfaces so that maintenance, failure, technological change and ordinary alteration do not consume the architecture.

This is stronger than the slogan “no services in permanent fabric.” That rule is one consequence of the larger proposition.

The publication should therefore argue for **selective permanence**, not universal flexibility. It should reject both common extremes:

- the supposedly permanent house in which short-lived services and finishes are materially entangled with structure; and
- the generic infinitely flexible shell in which architectural order is weakened in pursuit of hypothetical future change.

V7 already contains this distinction in its discussion of enduring architecture, adaptable layers and replaceable systems. It should become one of the intellectual centres of the book.

## 3. Proposed hierarchy of governing principles

The existing 63-item doctrine register is valuable as a completeness checklist, but it is too granular to act as the public-facing hierarchy of the argument. The 63 statements can provisionally be compressed into ten governing principles. This is an editorial proposal, not yet a rewrite of the doctrine.

### P1 — Build for time

Design the house around differing rates of change. Permanent fabric, architectural elements, services, controls, finishes and consumables should not be treated as if they share one lifespan.

Absorbs: lifespan hierarchy; functional longevity; material ageing; future technology; future occupants; climate change.

### P2 — Preserve the permanent fabric

Shorter-lived systems should not require destructive alteration of longer-lived architecture during ordinary maintenance, replacement or adaptation. Required crossings of permanent fabric are designed in advance.

Absorbs: no services in permanent fabric; structural crossings; fixing infrastructure; replaceable openings.

### P3 — Design the interface

Movement, tolerance, sealing, drainage, fixing, access and replacement occur at explicit architectural interfaces rather than being concealed with brittle finishes, filler, foam or ad-hoc site work.

Absorbs: movement doctrine; slip joints; reversible assembly; window/door interfaces; controlled penetrations.

### P4 — Make failure harmless and visible

Foreseeable failures should be contained, drainable, inspectable and apparent before they damage long-lived fabric. Protection should not depend on perfect seals, permanent sensors or permanent software availability.

Absorbs: moisture redundancy; secondary water containment; visible tell-tales; appliance containment; drainage and drying; passive failure management.

### P5 — Give maintenance a geography

Maintenance is a spatial design problem. Utility entry, plant, risers, horizontal distribution, wet rooms, undercroft, roof access and external service routes should form coherent, usable paths with real working and replacement space.

Absorbs: maintenance geography; plant room; riser; corridor spines; undercroft; roof routes; removal logistics; cleaning access.

### P6 — Prefer ordinary parts in extraordinary arrangements

Long life should rely on widely understood components, materials, tools and trades wherever possible. Bespoke design belongs mainly at architectural interfaces and should form repeatable, remanufacturable families rather than unique mechanisms.

Absorbs: commodity repairability; standardisation hierarchy; component families; remanufacturability; complexity control; prototyping.

### P7 — Let permanence be architectural

The house may possess a strong and deliberately enduring spatial order. Adaptability should be concentrated in the layers that benefit from change rather than making every wall or room movable.

Absorbs: spatial permanence; lifetime adaptability; future lift provision; adaptable fit-out; principal rooms/stairs/openings.

### P8 — Let passive architecture do the first work

Moisture, temperature, air quality, acoustic privacy, security and resilience should be addressed first through geometry, material, mass, separation, drainage, shading, compartmentation and other architectural means before dependence on active systems or controls.

Absorbs: passive environmental control; primary weather protection; acoustic planning; physical security; passive survivability.

### P9 — Make the building legible across generations

The house should remain understandable in its physical construction and through durable records. Systems are labelled, routes are predictable, isolation is obvious, commissioning establishes a baseline, and later alterations preserve the record.

Absorbs: physical self-documentation; building passport; circuit/service identifiers; commissioning; inspection; change control; manual fallback.

### P10 — Resolve technology as architecture

Serviceability and technical legibility should not force an industrial or temporary-looking domestic environment. Technical requirements should be integrated through proportion, joinery, metalwork, thresholds, cornices, grilles, panels and other architectural elements of genuine quality.

Absorbs: beauty as design priority; concealed-but-accessible infrastructure; functional brass/bronze language; domestic/classical character.

**Important:** life safety, legal compliance, structural integrity and basic health should remain explicit **constraints on all ten principles**, not become optional principles alongside them.

## 5. What should be promoted

Several ideas deserve more prominence than they currently receive.

### 5.1 Selective permanence

The distinction between enduring architecture, adaptable building layers and replaceable systems is a more sophisticated proposition than generic “flexibility.” It should sit near the beginning of the book, not halfway through governing doctrine and later under human adaptability.

### 5.2 Maintenance geography

This is one of v7's best terms and one of the most architectural ideas in the document. It converts maintenance from a collection of access panels into plan and section. It should become a major chapter concept and likely a full-house axonometric drawing.

### 5.3 The interface as an architectural element

V7 treats interfaces as places where movement, tolerance, sealing, access, replacement and expression are coordinated. This can unify many apparently unrelated details: cornices, thresholds, window reveals, floor-panel edges, penetrations and service panels.

### 5.4 Failure as a design state

The doctrine repeatedly asks where water goes after the first defence fails, whether plant can actually be removed, whether a blocked drain can be cleared, and whether later alterations preserve boundaries. This should become an explicit method rather than remaining distributed across moisture, services and review tests.

### 5.5 The building that explains itself

Physical identifiers, predictable routes, durable diagrams, commissioning records and a building passport form a coherent stewardship idea. This is stronger than “documentation” and deserves its own architectural treatment.

### 5.6 Ordinary parts, deliberate architecture

The standardisation/remanufacturability section resolves a serious weakness that could otherwise undermine the whole project: a house designed for repair becoming dependent on exotic bespoke parts. The slogan-level concept is powerful: **unusual building logic; ordinary components**.

## 6. What should be demoted from doctrine to strategy, pattern or reference implementation

These ideas may be excellent, but presenting them as universal doctrine weakens the publication because a reader can dispute the implementation and mistakenly believe they have disproved the thesis.

Likely candidates include:

- Georgian/classical language;
- brass and bronze as the visible interface language;
- ~215 mm dense masonry inner leaf;
- engineered timber joists;
- thermally broken aluminium window frames;
- gutterless eaves;
- one primary riser as the normal arrangement;
- the exact undercroft/service-trench configuration;
- skirting as the particular horizontal electrical route;
- door surrounds as the particular vertical route;
- removable floor cassettes;
- manifold-per-fixture distribution as the specific water topology;
- the precise form of bathroom source extraction;
- a permanent masonry fireplace/chimney;
- a specific future-lift strategy.

The correct editorial move is **not to delete these**. They should become patterns or the first reference implementation, where their architectural quality can be explored much more richly through drawings.

## 8. Patterns need a standard template

Each published pattern should use the same architecture so another architect can consume it rapidly:

1. **Problem** — what recurring failure or design conflict exists?
2. **Intent** — what must the pattern achieve?
3. **Principles served** — links back to P1–P10.
4. **Configuration** — plan/section/axonometric drawing.
5. **Interfaces** — structure, moisture, fire, acoustic, services, finishes.
6. **Failure state** — what happens when the short-lived component fails?
7. **Access / replacement sequence** — how is it actually maintained?
8. **Variants** — legitimate alternatives.
9. **Limits / contraindications** — when should this pattern not be used?
10. **Evidence / precedent** — relevant research, standards, precedents and analogous practice.
11. **Reference-house expression** — how the pattern is expressed in the proposed Georgian house, if applicable.

This format is likely to remove large amounts of repetitive prose while increasing technical precision.

## 9. Evidence-status system

V7 currently uses one authoritative voice for statements with different epistemic status. The published work should mark the distinction without becoming academically fussy.

Proposed evidence classes:

- **Established technical principle** — supported by building physics, engineering, safety practice, regulation or strong professional consensus.
- **Established design practice** — widely used architectural/construction approach with documented precedent.
- **Derived design proposition** — conclusion argued from the doctrine but not itself an established professional rule.
- **Reference-house preference** — a deliberate aesthetic or project choice.
- **Research question** — unresolved and requiring testing, specialist input, modelling or prototyping.

The text need not literally print these labels beside every sentence. They should govern writing, citation and page design.

## 10. Areas requiring especially hard evidential review

Before publication, the following should be researched rather than merely polished:

- the relationship to Open Building, support/infill thinking, shearing layers and long-life/loose-fit traditions;
- design for disassembly and adaptability literature;
- building-service life cycles and replacement frequencies;
- actual maintenance and defect data for domestic buildings;
- moisture redundancy, drained assemblies and drying potential;
- service cavities and airtightness practice;
- hygrothermal implications of the preferred wall and roof concepts;
- undercroft moisture behaviour in the UK climate;
- acoustic consequences of removable floor/service systems;
- fire compartmentation in accessible/alterable service voids;
- accessibility and lifetime-home precedents;
- commissioning and building-logbook/passport practice;
- actual long-life precedents: historic houses, institutional buildings and contemporary adaptable construction;
- construction cost, floor-area penalty and maintenance economics;
- whether specific proposed custom interface systems truly improve lifecycle outcomes versus excellent conventional detailing.

These are not reasons to weaken the thesis. They are where the thesis earns professional credibility.

## 12. Material that should not simply survive by inertia

A professional rewrite should actively challenge some material rather than assume v7 is correct because it is comprehensive.

Examples:

- “No service is permitted inside permanent fabric” is intellectually strong but may need precise definitions and exceptions so it cannot be defeated by trivial counterexamples.
- “No brittle finish should bridge independently moving assemblies” needs careful scoping: the engineering issue is expected differential movement and acceptable deformation, not an aesthetic prohibition on every conventional junction.
- passive leak containment needs proof that it does not create stagnant water, hygiene, condensation, corrosion or maintenance problems worse than the failures it addresses;
- large accessible service voids create fire, acoustic, moisture and pest consequences that need equal design weight;
- removable floors and panels must prove stiffness, acoustic performance, tolerance, long-term rattle resistance and fire behaviour;
- physically generous maintenance space consumes costly floor/sectional area and needs a defensible proportionality argument;
- repeated accessible interfaces can themselves multiply seals, joints, tolerances and failure points;
- “commodity repairability” can conflict with high-performance proprietary assemblies and must be framed as a preference subject to whole-life performance rather than an absolute rejection.

These are precisely the questions a good publication should expose and test.

## 13. What v8 should *not* be

The next iteration should not be a line edit of v7.

If v7 is edited in place without changing information architecture, it will become a cleaner version of the same hybrid document. Instead:

1. preserve v7 as the source corpus;
2. create a new publication outline;
3. map every v7 section into that outline;
4. split mixed passages into doctrine, strategy, pattern, reference implementation, evidence claim or open research;
5. only then begin prose rewriting.

Nothing useful needs to be lost. The aim is to change the reader's cognitive load and the authority of each statement.
