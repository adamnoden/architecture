## Context

A multi-storey house contains enough vertically connected service demand that independent floor-by-floor routing would create repeated structural crossings, long branches, inaccessible joints or poorly coordinated shafts.

The pattern may be unnecessary in a single-storey house, a very small dwelling, or a plan where only one or two ordinary stacks/chases already resolve vertical demand cleanly.

## Problem

Vertical services are often treated as whatever remains after room planning: a soil stack boxed into one corner, ventilation squeezed elsewhere, cables drilled through floors and later additions finding new paths.

Each system may work individually while the building accumulates repeated penetrations, inaccessible junctions, fire/acoustic discontinuities and routes future trades cannot understand.

A domestic house does not justify a commercial services shaft merely because larger buildings use one. The problem is to create **enough deliberate vertical geography without turning the house into plant infrastructure**.

## Candidate pattern

> **Where several service systems need to pass between storeys, reserve a deliberate vertical service zone aligned with service-intensive rooms. Size and subdivide it for the real systems and boundary obligations; make access available where it materially improves inspection, isolation, maintenance or replacement.**

The invariant is the **planned vertical zone**, not a particular shaft construction, enclosure size or promise of continuous access.

## Forces

- vertical concentration reduces repeated structural crossings;
- drainage and ventilation benefit from direct, low-resistance vertical routes;
- water, electrical, ventilation and drainage systems have different support/separation requirements;
- a continuous void can transmit fire, smoke, sound and pests if boundaries are not deliberately reinstated;
- access consumes area and may itself weaken fire/acoustic performance;
- some services need inspection at specific nodes rather than along their full length;
- large spare zones invite later uncontrolled use;
- domestic architecture should not acquire an institutional corridor/shaft character;
- future systems will not have exactly the same dimensions as present ones.

## Relationship to other patterns

Likely composition, pending corpus audit:

- normally follows a **Plant Room as Service Hub** where a hub exists;
- is strengthened by stacking or clustering high-service rooms;
- commonly feeds **High-Service-Room Service Wall** occurrences;
- reduces or organises the crossings later resolved by **Designed Structural Penetration**;
- may reduce the required depth/extent of a **Horizontal Service Spine** by keeping bulky gravity/ventilation routes vertical.

Do not encode these as canonical graph edges until the candidate is admitted.

## Variants / implementation families

### A. Accessible service cupboard / riser

A bounded zone with access doors/panels at useful storeys. Appropriate where several maintainable nodes cluster there.

### B. Split wet and dry vertical zones

Soil/water/ventilation geography separated from electrical/data or other services. Useful where separation and boundary control outweigh the cost of multiple routes.

### C. Narrow stack/chase with local access nodes

A smaller permanent route with access only at valves, rodding points, fans, dampers or other actual maintenance locations.

### D. Joinery-integrated vertical route

Where scale and service class permit, a real architectural cupboard or joinery element can provide the zone without pretending a commercial riser exists.

These are implementation families, not separate patterns unless future work shows materially different recurring problems/forces.

## Proportionality

The zone earns permanent area only where it materially reduces:

- repeated structural penetrations;
- long gravity/duct branches;
- destructive future access;
- inaccessible maintenance nodes;
- route confusion across generations.

A large empty shaft reserved for unspecified futures fails the proportionality test.

## Boundary debt

Technical design must explicitly resolve, where relevant:

- floor/ceiling fire separation and cavity barriers;
- smoke paths;
- acoustic flanking;
- air/thermal boundary crossings where the zone reaches envelope/roof;
- moisture/condensate;
- pest pathways;
- structural support of pipes/ducts/cables;
- service separation and safe working access.

The zone is not a boundary-free exception merely because it is “for services”.

## Permanent-fabric impact

The pattern intentionally concentrates a smaller number of designed floor/roof/wall interfaces rather than licensing arbitrary later drilling.

Permanent openings and supports should be recorded. Spare capacity should be justified, bounded and robustly closed where unused.

## Workmanship / tolerance

- define the clear service envelope after structure, fire/acoustic linings and supports are included;
- establish controlling datums before manufactured duct/pipe support systems arrive;
- ensure access openings expose the component that actually needs work rather than only the void;
- make cavity closure/fire stopping inspectable at suitable construction hold points;
- do not rely on the final trade to reconcile cumulative dimensional conflict inside the riser.

## Occupation / repose impact

The zone should normally recede into secondary circulation, service rooms or genuine domestic cupboards.

Check plumbing noise, fan/duct noise, vibration, access-door rattles and light leakage. A technically efficient riser that makes bedrooms sound like a plant room fails the house.

## Failure modes

- shaft sized from centre lines and unusable once insulation/supports are included;
- every service is allowed into one mixed void without separation logic;
- access door exists but valves/fans cannot be worked on or withdrawn;
- continuous void becomes a fire/acoustic path;
- future contractors drill new routes because the intended zone is full or undocumented;
- oversized zone becomes storage and blocks access;
- route sits far from actual wet/service demand and therefore fails to shorten branches;
- architecture is distorted merely to preserve a technical shaft that no longer earns its area.

## Evidence direction

The underlying proposition is consistent with established building-services maintainability and planned-service practice, but this project does **not** claim that a dedicated riser is standard or necessary in ordinary detached houses.

Relevant evidence anchors:

- CIBSE, *Guide M1: Guidance for designers* (2023) and *Guide M: Maintenance engineering and management* (2023): building-services design should account for operation and maintenance over service life;
- Approved Document B, Volume 1 (current England guidance): service penetrations, shafts/ducts and cavities create fire-separation obligations rather than exempting them;
- Approved Document H: sanitary pipework and drainage have real sizing, protection, rodding/inspection and access constraints;
- Approved Document F, Volume 1 (2026): dwelling ventilation routes and equipment remain performance systems whose geometry, commissioning and access cannot be assumed from architectural intent alone.

## Does not prove

- that every two-storey house needs a dedicated riser;
- that all service classes should share one enclosure;
- that a larger riser is inherently more adaptable;
- that access must exist along every millimetre of a service route;
- that the provisional Reference House reservation is correctly sized;
- that fire, acoustic, ventilation, drainage or structural performance follows merely from reserving the zone.

## Reference House application

`RH-CAND-VSR-01` currently reserves one east/rear vertical zone between `G-SVC-01` and `U-SVC-01`, with a split wet/dry-riser option retained as the principal comparator.

The current test favours one deliberate zone because it aligns lower plant/utility, upper bathrooms/service access and rear/north roof termination while reducing arbitrary floor crossings.

This application is evidence that the candidate is useful, not proof that its implementation is optimal.

## Formalisation boundary

Potentially formal consequences include:

- zone continuity between relevant storeys;
- clearance envelope;
- relationship to service-demand nodes;
- crossing count;
- access/working volumes at declared maintenance nodes;
- boundary transitions and evidence obligations.

Architectural judgement remains necessary for proportionality, domestic character, repose, visual integration and whether the reserved area is worth its cost.

## Candidate decision

**The proposition passes the conceptual admission test, but retain candidate status through the Phase-6 corpus audit before assigning a permanent HSA ID.**

Reason: the project has proved that the pattern is useful in two internally distinct whole-house studies (the Reference House fixture and H1 comparator), but has not yet challenged naming/scope against the full pattern corpus or external professional review.
