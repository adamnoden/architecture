# HSA-P-018 — Failure-Tolerant Wet Service Room

## Context

A utility, laundry or other service room contains several water-bearing appliances, valves, traps, filters or joints whose failures are credible over the life of the house.

## Problem

These rooms are often treated as dry rooms containing wet equipment. A failed hose, pump, trap or appliance can therefore send water into adjacent finishes, joinery and concealed fabric before isolation or repair begins.

## Pattern

**Where water-service density and consequence justify it, make the room itself tolerant of credible local water failure: keep isolation accessible, manage likely leakage, provide drainage/drying where proportionate, and preserve working space around the equipment.**

The invariant is room-scale failure tolerance, not one floor drain, tray or waterproofing product.

## Forces

- full wet-room construction has cost, embodied material and threshold consequences;
- appliance failures can release substantial water quickly;
- drainage is useful only if falls, traps and discharge remain serviceable;
- containment can hide slow leakage if poorly detailed;
- equipment replacement and repair need real working space;
- the room should remain ordinary domestic architecture, not a plant enclosure.

## Variants

Depending on risk and geometry, the pattern may use local appliance containment, a robust floor with controlled falls, a drain, visible leakage paths, low upstands, accessible isolation or combinations. Do not stack all measures by default.

## Proportionality

Use room-scale provision where several high-consequence water components are concentrated and retrofit after failure would be costly. A single low-risk appliance in a forgiving location may need only local measures.

## Boundary debt

Check waterproofing, floor/wall junctions, thresholds, drainage, acoustic separation and any penetrations. Water management should not silently compromise adjacent fire or air boundaries.

## Permanent-fabric impact

Falls, drains and waterproof layers can become long-lived room infrastructure. Their provision should be justified by credible failure scenarios and remain inspectable/repairable where practical.

## Workmanship and tolerance

Test actual low points, threshold levels, drain falls and appliance clearances in built geometry. A nominally drained room that ponds away from the outlet creates false confidence.

## Occupation / repose impact

The room should remain easy to use and clean. Technical measures should recede into a robust ordinary utility-room architecture rather than demanding constant attention.

## Failure modes

- drain sits above the actual low point;
- waterproofing terminates behind inaccessible joinery;
- appliance isolation is blocked;
- containment traps slow leaks out of sight;
- thresholds send failure water into adjacent rooms;
- equipment can be reached but not withdrawn or repaired.

## Evidence

Wet-room and service-room practice, together with overseas water-failure guidance reviewed in Phase 6, supports the underlying principle that concentrated water risk should be made visible, containable and maintainable. The room-scale HSA pattern remains evidence-supported rather than universal.

## Does not prove

The pattern does not establish that every bathroom or utility room requires a floor drain or full tanking, nor that local failure measures replace sound appliance and plumbing installation.

## Reference House application

The Reference House treats the utility/laundry as the strongest candidate for this pattern because multiple water appliances and service controls can be concentrated there. Bathrooms may use only the parts of the logic justified by their own construction and failure scenarios.

## Formalisation boundary

Water-bearing occurrences, isolation access, containment extent, drainage destination and thresholds can be represented formally. Waterproofing adequacy, fall performance and proportionality remain technical/architectural evidence questions.
