# The Long-Life House — Core Pattern Catalogue
## Twelve patterns for first publication development

**Draft v0.2 — repose integration**

These are the first patterns worth developing into full illustrated spreads. They were selected because they either have strong professional foundations or express the doctrine with relatively low implementation risk.

A pattern is not a requirement. Each must pass the project's proportionality and boundary-debt tests.

Every pattern should also be reviewed for **workmanship robustness** where construction variability is relevant. The developed pattern spread should identify the controlling datum, credible incoming tolerance, adjustment/recovery method, remediation threshold and any operation that intentionally depends on specialist craft. A pattern should not export an unresolved dimensional problem to the last trade.

Every pattern that affects occupied space should also record its **occupation / repose impact**. The review should ask whether the pattern changes noise, glare, privacy, local control, apparent solidity, visual hierarchy or the amount of technical information presented to the room. Maintainability does not excuse a result that keeps the occupant's attention on the building's systems.

# Pattern 01 — Controlled utility entry

**Status:** Established  
**Principles:** 2 Preserve permanent fabric; 5 Maintenance geography; 10 Legibility

## Problem

Incoming water, electricity, communications and other utilities often acquire unrelated routes and penetrations because each system is designed or installed separately. Later additions create further openings through the envelope and structure.

## Pattern

Create a deliberate utility-entry zone in which incoming services cross the building envelope through coordinated, documented interfaces and then transition into the internal distribution system.

Where proportionate, the entry zone may include spare sleeves or capacity for foreseeable future infrastructure.

## Forces

- Different utilities have different statutory and supplier requirements.
- Penetrations must preserve water, air, thermal, fire, pest and security performance.
- Too much spare capacity wastes material and can itself create weak points.
- Utility providers may control parts of the route outside the architect's normal detail.

## Design requirements

- coordinate routes before structure/envelope construction;
- separate incompatible services as required;
- make principal isolation accessible;
- use deliberate sleeves/penetrations rather than later ad-hoc drilling;
- identify routes permanently and in the building record;
- seal unused future provisions robustly;
- keep the transition from external/buried infrastructure to inspectable internal infrastructure as short and clear as practical.

## Failure modes

Oversized unsealed openings; water tracking through sleeves; rodent entry; later services ignoring the controlled entry because the reserved capacity is unusable; utility meters/isolators becoming inaccessible behind storage or joinery.

## Evidence

Historic England recommends common service routes, existing openings, accessible routes and spare capacity where useful in order to reduce repeated fabric damage. Approved Document R demonstrates the regulatory legitimacy of deliberately planned physical communications infrastructure from building access point to network termination point.

## Does not prove

Historic-building conservation guidance does not prove that every new house should consolidate every utility into one literal penetration. Supplier constraints and separation requirements may make several adjacent controlled crossings preferable.

## Reference-house direction

A side/rear intake zone adjacent to the plant/service hub, architecturally subordinate but fully accessible.

**Evidence anchors:** Historic England, *Installing New Services*; Approved Document R.

# Pattern 02 — Plant room as service hub

**Status:** Established  
**Principles:** 1 Build for time; 5 Maintenance geography; 6 Ordinary parts; 10 Legibility

## Problem

Domestic plant is frequently distributed among kitchen cupboards, lofts, airing cupboards, external walls and inaccessible corners. Individual items may be technically accessible while the system as a whole is difficult to understand, isolate or replace.

## Pattern

Concentrate major distribution, controls and replaceable plant into a deliberately designed service hub where system relationships are legible and working/replacement space has been checked.

## Forces

- A dedicated room consumes valuable floor area.
- Concentration can create noise, heat and fire risks.
- Some equipment is better placed near loads or outside.
- Future plant may have different dimensions and service requirements.

## Design requirements

- define actual working clearances rather than an abstract “plant room” label;
- draw replacement envelopes for the largest items;
- provide drainage where credible failures require it;
- manage heat rejection, ventilation and acoustic isolation;
- provide lighting and durable labelling;
- preserve access to isolation without moving stored possessions;
- maintain a clear relationship with the principal riser/distribution route.

## Failure modes

Plant room becoming general storage; door too small for replacement; equipment packed so tightly that filters/valves cannot be reached; noise transmitted structurally; floor drain or containment neglected; future plant forced elsewhere because the room was dimensioned only for the first equipment set.

## Evidence

CIBSE Guide M treats maintainability and continued satisfactory performance as design concerns. Arup's circular design guidance requires access and enough working space to undo and remove building-services components.

## Does not prove

Commercial facilities-management standards do not justify commercial-scale plant space in a house. The room must be sized by domestic tasks and replacement scenarios.

## Reference-house direction

Compact service hub adjacent to the main vertical route, with a clear equipment-replacement path to an external door.

**Evidence anchors:** CIBSE Guide M/M1; Arup Circular Buildings Toolkit.

# Pattern 03 — Horizontal service spine

**Status:** Supported  
**Principles:** 2 Preserve permanent fabric; 5 Maintenance geography; 6 Ordinary parts

## Problem

Room-by-room routing produces a web of services crossing structure, acoustic boundaries and finished rooms. Later additions repeat the damage.

## Pattern

Bias horizontal distribution toward a coherent service edge, corridor, ceiling/floor band or other shared route from which local branches enter rooms.

## Forces

- The shortest individual route is not always the best whole-building route.
- Shared routes concentrate fire, acoustic and maintenance consequences.
- Large ducts and gravity drainage cannot always share the same shallow zone.
- A domestic corridor should not become visually or dimensionally institutional.

## Design requirements

- coordinate all relevant disciplines on one route plan;
- separate services physically where required;
- stop the open void at compartment boundaries;
- reserve spare capacity only where likely to be useful;
- provide local access at sensible intervals;
- avoid making every room part of the maintenance route.

## Failure modes

Continuous open void acting as sound/smoke/pest path; route too small once insulation/bends/clearances are included; access blocked by fitted furniture; all services forced into one zone despite incompatible geometry.

## Evidence

Historic England explicitly recommends shared/common routes where new services would otherwise repeatedly scar fabric. Approved Document R uses planned vertical and horizontal physical infrastructure in larger residential buildings as a normal means of distributing communications.

## Does not prove

A multi-dwelling service route does not prove that a detached house needs a commercial corridor ceiling or raised floor. The transferable principle is route coherence.

## Reference-house direction

Use ordinary circulation and service-heavy edges as the default horizontal topology; deepen only locally where ducts/drainage require it.

**Evidence anchors:** Historic England, *Installing New Services*; Approved Document R.

# Pattern 04 — High-service-room service wall

**Status:** Supported  
**Principles:** 2 Preserve permanent fabric; 4 Failure architecture; 5 Maintenance geography

## Problem

Bathrooms, kitchens and utilities concentrate water, drainage, valves, ventilation, power and controls. Conventional fit-out often seals high-risk components behind tile, stone and cabinetry.

## Pattern

Where plan geometry permits, arrange high-service fixtures against a deliberately accessible service zone. The occupied face can remain fully finished; valves, cisterns, junctions, selected wastes and other shorter-lived components are reached from a cupboard, corridor panel, service room or other dry side.

## Forces

- The service zone consumes thickness/area.
- Acoustic privacy must be preserved.
- Fire and water boundaries must remain coherent.
- Not every pipe joint warrants access.
- A back-access panel can conflict with joinery or circulation.

## Design requirements

- decide which components genuinely need recurrent access;
- provide real tool clearance;
- preserve acoustic/fire performance of the separating wall;
- keep waterproofing strategy independent from routine service opening where practical;
- ensure failed water cannot run unseen into the service zone;
- coordinate removal sequences for concealed cisterns, valves and fans.

## Failure modes

Panel exists but component cannot be withdrawn; wall becomes an acoustic weakness; access side is blocked by shelving; waterproofing relies on panel seals; maintenance requires entering another private room.

## Evidence

Accessible service walls and plumbing voids are mature building practice; Open Building and service-layer precedents support separating fast-changing service/infill layers from slower support. The doctrine's contribution is to make this an architectural planning device in a domestic house.

## Does not prove

The pattern does not justify doubling every bathroom wall. It is strongest where several high-consequence components can share one accessible zone.

## Reference-house direction

Stack bathrooms/utility strategically so one or two service walls do substantial work.

# Pattern 05 — Designed structural penetration

**Status:** Established  
**Principles:** 2 Preserve permanent fabric; 3 Design the interface; 11 Resolve technology as architecture

## Problem

Services frequently reach structure after structural design is substantially complete. The result is later drilling, notching, chasing or oversized openings that are then cosmetically filled.

## Pattern

Treat required crossings as designed interfaces established during structural and service coordination. Size, location, sleeve/collar and boundary performance are defined before construction.

## Forces

- Future unknown services cannot all be predicted.
- Oversized sleeves may weaken boundaries or structure.
- Different penetrations require different fire, acoustic, moisture and movement treatment.
- “Spare holes” can become uncontrolled routes.

## Design requirements

- structural engineer owns structural adequacy;
- define allowable services and fill ratio;
- coordinate fire/acoustic/air/water/pest requirements;
- provide robust blanking for unused capacity;
- make important penetrations identifiable and inspectable where practical;
- document no-drill zones around them.

## Failure modes

Trade enlarges the opening; sleeve becomes a rigid acoustic bridge; fire stopping is hidden and later disturbed; external penetration falls inward and admits water; spare opening becomes pest route.

## Evidence

Approved Document P explicitly identifies the interaction of electrical service penetrations with structure, fire safety, moisture and sound. This is ordinary multidisciplinary coordination elevated into a repeatable interface family.

## Reference-house direction

A limited penetration catalogue with repeatable sizes/details rather than unique site improvisations.

**Evidence anchors:** Approved Document P; Approved Documents B/C/E as boundary context.

# Pattern 06 — Water-damage-safe service route

**Status:** Established principle  
**Principles:** 4 Failure architecture; 5 Maintenance geography; 9 Passive first

## Problem

A concealed pressurised-water leak can remain undetected while damaging timber, insulation, finishes and structure.

## Pattern

Arrange concealed water distribution so credible leakage is either contained within a safe route, directed to a detectable location, automatically isolated, or otherwise prevented from silently wetting vulnerable construction.

The pattern describes performance, not one product.

## Variants

- pipe-in-pipe;
- drained manifold/service cabinet;
- fully accessible distribution;
- local containment beneath high-risk joints;
- passive fall to safe visible/detectable point;
- leak sensing and automatic isolation as an additional layer.

## Forces

- containment can trap dirt or condensation;
- drainage routes need falls and maintenance;
- external tell-tales can freeze, stain or admit pests;
- sensors can fail or lose power;
- additional pipework/material has cost and carbon.

## Design requirements

- minimise concealed joints first;
- place accessible isolation near the system served;
- ensure containment itself can drain/dry;
- distinguish normal condensate from leakage;
- test the protective path before handover;
- document what a detected/discharged condition means.

## Failure modes

Leak path blocked; containment has no fall; sensor gives false confidence; water exits somewhere ambiguous; secondary tray corrodes; pipe insulation hides leakage.

## Evidence

Norwegian TEK17 guidance provides a direct mature precedent for arranging water installations so leakage is easy to detect and does not damage building elements. Approved Document G's visible tundish discharge is a narrower British example of making abnormal water discharge apparent.

## Does not prove

It does not prove that a continuous metal trough should run beneath every pipe in a UK house.

## Reference-house direction

Investigate pipe-in-pipe or accessible distribution first; use local passive containment where consequence justifies it.

**Evidence anchors:** Norwegian TEK17 §15-5; Approved Document G.

# Pattern 07 — Permanent opening / replaceable window

**Status:** Supported  
**Principles:** 2 Preserve permanent fabric; 3 Design the interface; 7 Architectural permanence

## Problem

The architectural opening and surrounding masonry can outlast the window frame, glazing, seals and ironmongery, yet ordinary installation can assimilate the frame into plaster, foam and sealant so complete replacement damages sound surrounding work.

## Pattern

Treat the opening as enduring architecture and the window as a replaceable assembly mounted within a deliberate interface that manages fixing, drainage, thermal continuity, airtightness and movement.

## Forces

- high-performance installation requires excellent air/water/thermal continuity;
- additional subframes can introduce thermal bridges and cost;
- concealed fixings are visually desirable but must remain reachable;
- external weathering seals will still need renewal.

## Design requirements

- distinguish frame weathering from building-side drainage;
- mechanically define the mounting zone;
- avoid brittle finish bridging moving frame/masonry interface;
- provide a replacement sequence for full frame, not only glazing;
- ensure removal of internal trim does not destroy primary air/water layers;
- protect masonry edges during repeated work.

## Failure modes

Subframe creates thermal bridge; sill drainage blocked; sealant becomes sole weather defence; internal reveal cannot be removed cleanly; future frame system no longer fits the opening geometry.

## Evidence

Conservation guidance strongly supports reversibility and minimising damage to enduring fabric. Design-for-disassembly guidance supports accessible reversible connections between layers with different service lives.

## Does not prove

There is no claim that every window needs a proprietary removable subframe. The required outcome is replaceability of the shorter-lived assembly without reconstructing the opening.

## Reference-house direction

Develop one 1:1 jamb/head/sill prototype and test water, air, thermal bridge and replacement sequence before multiplying the detail.

# Pattern 08 — Movement / slip junction

**Status:** Established principle  
**Principles:** 3 Design the interface; 11 Resolve technology as architecture

## Problem

Independently moving assemblies are routinely connected by brittle plaster, grout or filler, transferring predictable movement into cracks and edge damage.

## Pattern

Where relative movement is expected, use a junction that allows it: deliberate gap, overlap, slip piece, movement joint, flexible replaceable seal or other technically appropriate interface.

The joint should define **bounded movement**, not vague looseness. Required support and restraint remain deterministic. Where useful, separate support, restraint, movement, sealing and visible finish rather than asking one brittle edge condition to perform all functions.

## Forces

- movement allowance can be visually conspicuous;
- acoustic/fire/air boundaries may still need continuity;
- flexible sealants are themselves maintenance items;
- oversized movement gaps collect dirt and look crude.

## Design requirements

- quantify expected movement where technically significant;
- fix cover pieces to one controlling assembly rather than bridging both;
- use geometry to absorb tolerance;
- make replaceable seals accessible;
- protect fragile finish edges;
- coordinate the visual language with the architecture;
- state which assembly controls the joint and which is permitted to move;
- prevent paint, filler or later decoration from silently locking the slip condition.

## Architectural resolution

The movement allowance should become a controlled architectural line rather than a defect to be hidden.

A conventional shadow, overlap or trim may be the quietest answer. In the reference house a fine brass or bronze element may be appropriate where it performs a real role in covering, protecting or articulating the joint. Metal is optional; the truthful joint is not.

## Assembly and replacement sequence

Record how the cover/trim is installed, what it is fixed to, how the movement gap remains free, how any seal is renewed, and how later decorators are prevented from bridging the joint.

## Failure modes

Trim inadvertently fixed through both assemblies; paint bridges the slip joint; sealant is treated as permanent; movement gap is too small; detail rattles or shadows badly.

## Evidence

Movement joints and differential-movement detailing are standard practice across masonry, facades and structures. The proposed architectural contribution is to treat domestic internal junctions with the same intentionality.

## Reference-house direction

Conventional quiet joints by default; functional cornice is a separate experimental expression, not the base pattern.

# Pattern 09 — Accessible rainwater route

**Status:** Established principle  
**Principles:** 4 Failure architecture; 5 Maintenance geography; 9 Passive first

## Problem

Rainwater systems are high-consequence maintenance items. Blockage, undersizing or hidden failure can wet large areas of fabric before occupants understand the source.

## Pattern

Arrange roof drainage so collection, outlets, overflows and principal downstream routes can be inspected, maintained and—where failure occurs—shed or reveal water without driving it into concealed fabric.

## Forces

- external pipework affects elevations;
- gutters require safe access;
- concealed/parapet drainage may be architecturally unavoidable;
- climate change can alter rainfall intensity;
- open discharge can create splashback or icing.

## Design requirements

- size for project-specific rainfall/exposure;
- provide deliberate overflow/failure paths;
- make debris-prone points cleanable;
- design safe access or eliminate the maintenance item;
- coordinate discharge with ground drainage;
- keep water away from vulnerable plinth/wall interfaces.

## Failure modes

Overflow enters cavity; downpipe blockage is invisible; maintenance requires unsafe ladder position; leaf guard itself blocks; ground gully saturates the wall base.

## Evidence

Historic England's resilient rainwater guidance explicitly stresses inspection, maintenance, capacity, climate resilience and the access problems created by concealed downpipes. It also recognises deep projecting eaves without rainwater goods as valid on some buildings.

## Does not prove

Gutterless eaves are not universally superior. Exposure, entry points, paving, landscape and architecture determine suitability.

## Reference-house direction

Compare external gutters/downpipes against deep-eaves discharge objectively rather than preserving v7's earlier preference.

**Evidence anchor:** Historic England, *Resilient Rainwater Systems*.

# Pattern 10 — Source-capture kitchen extract

**Status:** Established  
**Principles:** 9 Passive first; 5 Maintenance geography

## Problem

Cooking produces heat, water vapour, grease and airborne contaminants. Once dispersed through the room and house, these loads are harder to remove.

## Pattern

Capture the cooking plume close to source with an appropriately sized hood/canopy and discharge it through a deliberate, maintainable route where project conditions permit.

## Forces

- high extract can depressurise an airtight dwelling;
- route length/bends affect performance;
- external termination affects facade and neighbours;
- grease requires recurring cleaning;
- combustion appliances can create pressure interactions;
- noise can discourage occupants from using the system.

## Design requirements

- hood geometry should actually capture the plume;
- provide washable/replaceable grease filtration;
- keep duct route smooth and cleanable;
- provide make-up/replacement air as required;
- locate fan for acceptable noise and replacement access;
- commission actual airflow;
- coordinate with whole-house ventilation and combustion.

## Failure modes

Attractive shallow hood misses plume; flexible duct accumulates grease; fan too noisy so user leaves it off; replacement air omitted; concealed duct cannot be cleaned.

## Evidence

Approved Document F establishes the ventilation-performance framework for dwellings and requires commissioning/measurement of mechanical ventilation in new homes. The doctrine adds the architectural emphasis on source capture, serviceable route and actual use.

## Reference-house direction

A visually integrated hood/chimney-piece form can be explored only after capture geometry and duct performance are fixed.

**Evidence anchor:** Approved Document F 2026.

# Pattern 11 — Roof maintenance route

**Status:** Established principle  
**Principles:** 1 Build for time; 5 Maintenance geography; 4 Failure architecture

## Problem

Roof outlets, chimneys, flashings, rooflights and rooftop equipment require maintenance, yet ordinary domestic design often leaves future workers to invent access with ladders and temporary measures.

## Pattern

Where roof maintenance cannot be designed out, provide an intentional route from inside or outside the building to safe working positions, with appropriate means of controlling fall risk and enabling component replacement.

## Forces

- permanent access hardware affects appearance and waterproofing;
- roof geometry may make fixed anchors insufficient;
- maintenance frequency may be low;
- permanent access can create a security route.

## Design requirements

- first minimise roof-mounted maintainable equipment;
- define who needs to reach what and how often;
- provide safe approach and landing positions;
- coordinate hatches, guard/edge strategy and temporary-access provisions;
- consider lifting/replacement of major equipment, not just inspection;
- secure the maintenance route against unauthorised access.

## Failure modes

Anchor exists but cannot support actual work sequence; hatch opens onto unsafe slope; access route crosses fragile rooflight; equipment can be serviced but not removed; security ignored.

## Evidence

HSE HSG33 addresses roof work across new buildings, repair, maintenance and cleaning, and explicitly includes clients, designers and specifiers in planning safe roof work.

## Reference-house direction

Rear/secondary roof access only if it materially improves real maintenance tasks; otherwise design for safe external scaffold/MEWP interfaces.

**Evidence anchor:** HSE HSG33.

# Pattern 12 — Physical service index

**Status:** Proposed  
**Principles:** 10 Legibility; 5 Maintenance geography; 6 Ordinary parts

## Problem

Digital building records are powerful but can become inaccessible through lost accounts, obsolete software, poor handover or ownership change. Conversely, extensive physical labelling can become stale and misleading.

## Pattern

Maintain a deliberately small physical index in the building containing stable identifiers and essential operational information, linked to a richer digital building passport.

Typical physical content:
- main water/electrical/fuel isolation;
- route and riser identifiers;
- principal distribution-board references;
- emergency/service contact or record location;
- stable identifiers matching labels on actual components.

## Forces

- physical information must be updated after alterations;
- too much information becomes visual noise;
- QR codes/URLs are not durable by themselves;
- detailed records belong in an editable information system.

## Design requirements

- use stable IDs rather than verbose descriptions where possible;
- place the index where maintenance begins;
- pair physical IDs with drawings and digital records;
- include change-control responsibility in the house manual;
- use durable, ordinary-readable formats.

## Failure modes

Plate becomes false after alteration; handwritten additions destroy clarity; digital record uses different naming; identifiers disappear during redecoration.

## Evidence

RIBA's Plan for Use explicitly recommends layered building information: simple user guide, quick-start/emergency overview and detailed O&M information. The Building Safety Regulator's golden-thread regime—although not applicable to an ordinary detached house—provides a strong professional precedent for information being current, accessible, usable and transferable.

## Distinctive contribution

The physical index is the doctrine's resilience extension: enough critical information remains at the asset even if its external information systems fail.

## Reference-house direction

One restrained service board/plate in the plant hub, with labels repeated at corresponding components.

**Evidence anchors:** RIBA *Plan for Use Guide*; BSR golden-thread guidance.

# Catalogue-wide editorial controls

## Pattern maturity

Each final spread must show two independent labels:

**Evidence:** Established / Supported / Proposed / Experimental.  
**Maturity:** Drawn / Calculated / Costed / Mocked-up / Maintenance-tested / Built / Observed in use.

A pattern can be evidence-supported but project-immature.

## Mandatory “does not prove” box

Any pattern relying materially on institutional, heritage or overseas precedent should state what that precedent **does not** establish for a new London house.

This prevents precedent laundering.

## Mandatory boundary ledger

Every pattern involving an opening, void or removable element must explicitly check:
structure / fire / smoke / acoustic / air / vapour / water / thermal / pest / security.

## Mandatory proportionality note

Every pattern must say what future task it exists to support and why permanent provision is justified.

## Mandatory permanent-fabric impact

Every pattern must state what irreversible work, anchors, penetrations, chases or embedded components it requires in permanent fabric and why those interventions are justified.

## Mandatory architectural resolution

Every visible or occupant-facing pattern must answer:

> **How does this become architecture rather than equipment?**

The answer may be visually quiet. The requirement is that technical accessibility, movement or replacement is compositionally resolved rather than excused as an unavoidable technical appearance.

## Mandatory assembly and replacement sequence

Every pattern involving a replaceable component must identify:

- what is removed first;
- the fixing/release method;
- required tools and working clearance;
- the withdrawal path;
- boundaries disturbed by the work;
- the method for reliable reinstatement.

## Tectonic honesty check

Pattern development must distinguish concealment from falsification. A concealing architectural element is legitimate when it has a genuine material/function and the condition behind it is resolved. Cosmetic continuity must not substitute for movement, access or construction detailing.
