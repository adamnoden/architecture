# The Long-Life House — Core Pattern Catalogue
## Twelve patterns for first publication development

**Draft v0.3 — editorial refinement**

These are the first patterns worth developing into full illustrated spreads. Each has either a strong professional basis or a relatively low-risk way of expressing the doctrine.

A pattern is not a requirement. Each must pass the project's proportionality and boundary-debt tests.

Where construction variability matters, review **workmanship robustness**: controlling datum, credible incoming tolerance, adjustment or recovery method, remediation threshold and any operation that intentionally depends on specialist craft. Do not export unresolved dimensional conflict to the last trade.

Where occupied space is affected, record the **occupation / repose impact**: noise, glare, privacy, local control, apparent solidity, visual hierarchy and technical presence. Maintainability does not excuse a room that keeps calling attention to its systems.

# Pattern 01 — Controlled utility entry

**Status:** Established  
**Principles:** 2 Preserve permanent fabric; 5 Maintenance geography; 10 Legibility

## Problem

Incoming water, electricity, communications and other utilities often acquire unrelated penetrations because each system is designed separately. Later additions create more openings through envelope and structure.

## Pattern

Create a deliberate utility-entry zone where incoming services cross through coordinated, documented interfaces before joining internal distribution. Where proportionate, reserve sleeves or capacity for foreseeable future infrastructure.

## Forces

- utilities have different statutory and supplier requirements;
- penetrations must preserve water, air, thermal, fire, pest and security performance;
- spare capacity can waste material or create weak points;
- utility providers may control parts of the external route.

## Design requirements

- coordinate routes before structure and envelope construction;
- separate incompatible services as required;
- keep principal isolation accessible;
- use designed sleeves or penetrations rather than later drilling;
- identify routes physically and in the building record;
- blank unused capacity robustly;
- keep the transition from buried/external to inspectable internal infrastructure short and legible.

## Occupation / repose impact

Concentrate meters, isolators and labels where they are accessible without becoming a visual or acoustic presence in principal rooms. Check external intake equipment for hum, drafts or indicator light where these could affect occupied space.

## Failure modes

Unsealed openings; water tracking through sleeves; rodent entry; reserved capacity proving unusable; meters or isolators becoming inaccessible behind storage.

## Evidence

Historic England recommends common routes, existing openings, accessible routes and useful spare capacity to reduce repeated fabric damage. Approved Document R demonstrates the legitimacy of planned physical communications infrastructure from building access point to network termination point.

## Does not prove

Conservation guidance does not establish that every utility should share one literal penetration. Supplier and separation requirements may justify several adjacent controlled crossings.

## Reference-house direction

Side/rear intake zone beside the plant/service hub, subordinate in appearance but fully accessible.

**Evidence anchors:** Historic England, *Installing New Services*; Approved Document R.

# Pattern 02 — Plant room as service hub

**Status:** Established  
**Principles:** 1 Build for time; 5 Maintenance geography; 6 Ordinary parts; 8 Repose; 10 Legibility

## Problem

Domestic plant is often scattered among cupboards, lofts and external walls. Individual items may be reachable while the system remains difficult to understand, isolate or replace.

## Pattern

Concentrate major distribution, controls and replaceable plant in a service hub where system relationships, working space and replacement paths are explicit.

## Forces

- dedicated space has an area cost;
- concentration can create noise, heat and fire risks;
- some equipment belongs nearer loads or outside;
- future plant may differ in size and service requirements.

## Design requirements

- draw real working clearances and replacement envelopes;
- provide drainage where credible failures require it;
- manage heat rejection, ventilation and acoustic isolation;
- provide lighting and durable labelling;
- keep isolation accessible without moving storage;
- maintain a clear relationship with the principal riser/distribution route.

## Occupation / repose impact

The hub is beneficial only if fan, pump, compressor and structure-borne noise, heat leakage and alarms are kept from principal rooms. Routine service access should not depend on passing through bedrooms or principal sitting rooms where a reasonable alternative exists.

## Failure modes

Room becomes storage; door is too small for replacement; equipment blocks filters or valves; vibration transmits structurally; containment is omitted; future plant has nowhere to go.

## Evidence

CIBSE Guide M treats maintainability and continued performance as design concerns. Arup's circular-design guidance requires access and enough working space to undo and remove services components.

## Does not prove

Commercial facilities standards do not justify commercial-scale plant space in a house. Size the hub from domestic tasks and replacement scenarios.

## Reference-house direction

Compact hub beside the main vertical route with a clear replacement path to an external door.

**Evidence anchors:** CIBSE Guide M/M1; Arup Circular Buildings Toolkit.

# Pattern 03 — Horizontal service spine

**Status:** Supported  
**Principles:** 2 Preserve permanent fabric; 5 Maintenance geography; 6 Ordinary parts; 8 Repose

## Problem

Room-by-room routing creates repeated crossings of structure, acoustic boundaries and finished rooms. Later additions repeat the damage.

## Pattern

Bias horizontal distribution toward a coherent service edge, corridor or ceiling/floor band from which local branches enter rooms.

## Forces

- the shortest individual route may not be the best whole-building route;
- shared routes concentrate fire, acoustic and maintenance consequences;
- ducts and gravity drainage may need more depth than other services;
- domestic circulation should not become institutional.

## Design requirements

- coordinate disciplines on one route plan;
- separate incompatible services as required;
- stop open voids at compartment boundaries;
- reserve spare capacity only where useful;
- provide local access at sensible intervals;
- avoid making every room part of the maintenance route.

## Occupation / repose impact

A shared route should reduce disturbance elsewhere without turning circulation into a technical interior. It must not become a path for speech, plumbing noise, smoke or equipment hum. A corridor containing the spine should still read first as domestic circulation.

## Failure modes

Continuous void becomes sound/smoke/pest path; route is too small once insulation and bends are included; furniture blocks access; incompatible services are forced together.

## Evidence

Historic England recommends shared/common routes where services would otherwise repeatedly scar fabric. Approved Document R uses planned vertical and horizontal infrastructure in larger residential buildings.

## Does not prove

A detached house does not thereby need a commercial corridor ceiling or raised floor. The transferable principle is route coherence.

## Reference-house direction

Use ordinary circulation and service-heavy edges as the default topology; deepen locally where ducts or drainage require it.

**Evidence anchors:** Historic England, *Installing New Services*; Approved Document R.

# Pattern 04 — High-service-room service wall

**Status:** Supported  
**Principles:** 2 Preserve permanent fabric; 4 Failure architecture; 5 Maintenance geography; 8 Repose

## Problem

Bathrooms, kitchens and utilities concentrate water, drainage, valves, ventilation, power and controls. Conventional fit-out often seals the highest-risk components behind tile, stone or cabinetry.

## Pattern

Where plan geometry permits, arrange high-service fixtures against an accessible service zone. The occupied face remains finished; valves, cisterns, selected wastes and other shorter-lived components are reached from a cupboard, corridor panel, service room or other dry side.

## Forces

- the zone consumes thickness or area;
- acoustic privacy and fire/water boundaries must survive;
- not every joint warrants access;
- back access can conflict with joinery or circulation.

## Design requirements

- decide which components need recurrent access;
- provide real tool and withdrawal clearance;
- preserve acoustic and fire performance;
- keep waterproofing independent from routine service opening where practical;
- prevent failed water from running unseen into the service zone;
- coordinate removal of concealed cisterns, valves and fans.

## Occupation / repose impact

Do not export plumbing or extract noise, maintenance activity or loss of privacy into the adjoining room. The finished occupied face should remain a convincing room surface, with panel lines and hardware subordinate unless deliberately composed.

## Failure modes

Panel opens but component cannot be withdrawn; wall becomes an acoustic weakness; shelving blocks access; waterproofing relies on the panel seal; maintenance requires another private room.

## Evidence

Accessible service walls and plumbing voids are mature practice. Open Building and service-layer precedents support separating fast-changing services from slower support.

## Does not prove

The pattern does not justify doubling every bathroom wall. It is strongest where several high-consequence components share one zone.

## Reference-house direction

Stack bathrooms and utility strategically so one or two service walls do substantial work.

# Pattern 05 — Designed structural penetration

**Status:** Established  
**Principles:** 2 Preserve permanent fabric; 3 Design the interface; 11 Resolve technology as architecture

## Problem

Services often reach structure after structural design is substantially complete, leading to later drilling, notching, chasing or oversized openings.

## Pattern

Treat required crossings as designed interfaces established during structural/service coordination. Define size, location, sleeve or collar and boundary performance before construction.

## Forces

- future services cannot all be predicted;
- oversized sleeves may weaken structure or boundaries;
- penetrations have different fire, acoustic, moisture and movement obligations;
- spare holes can become uncontrolled routes.

## Design requirements

- structural engineer owns structural adequacy;
- define allowable services and fill ratio;
- coordinate fire/acoustic/air/water/pest requirements;
- blank unused capacity robustly;
- make important penetrations identifiable and inspectable where practical;
- document no-drill zones.

## Occupation / repose impact

Protect the acoustic and environmental performance of the construction crossed. Resolve exposed collars, grilles or covers as part of the room rather than residual engineering work.

## Failure modes

Opening enlarged on site; sleeve becomes an acoustic bridge; hidden fire stopping is later disturbed; external penetration falls inward; spare opening becomes a pest route.

## Evidence

Approved Document P explicitly identifies interactions between electrical penetrations, structure, fire, moisture and sound.

## Reference-house direction

Limited penetration catalogue with repeatable sizes/details rather than unique site improvisations.

**Evidence anchors:** Approved Document P; Approved Documents B/C/E as boundary context.

# Pattern 06 — Water-damage-safe service route

**Status:** Established principle  
**Principles:** 4 Failure architecture; 5 Maintenance geography; 9 Passive first

## Problem

A concealed pressurised-water leak can remain unnoticed while damaging timber, insulation, finishes and structure.

## Pattern

Arrange concealed water distribution so credible leakage is contained, directed to a detectable location, automatically isolated, or otherwise prevented from silently wetting vulnerable construction. The pattern specifies performance, not one product.

## Variants

- pipe-in-pipe;
- drained manifold/service cabinet;
- fully accessible distribution;
- local containment beneath high-risk joints;
- passive fall to a safe detectable point;
- leak sensing and automatic isolation as an additional layer.

## Forces

- containment can trap dirt or condensation;
- drainage routes need fall and maintenance;
- tell-tales can freeze, stain or admit pests;
- sensors can fail or lose power;
- added material has cost and carbon.

## Design requirements

- minimise concealed joints first;
- keep isolation accessible near the system served;
- ensure containment can drain and dry;
- distinguish normal condensate from leakage;
- test the protective path before handover;
- document what a detected or discharged condition means.

## Occupation / repose impact

Detection should be clear when something is wrong without turning alarms, inspection fittings or channels into a constant presence. Pumps, valves and sensors should be checked for nuisance noise and light. The pattern should reduce hidden-leak vigilance, not replace it with another burden.

## Failure modes

Blocked leak path; no fall; sensor gives false confidence; ambiguous discharge point; corroded tray; insulation conceals leakage.

## Evidence

Norwegian TEK17 provides a mature precedent for arranging water installations so leakage is detectable and does not damage building elements. Approved Document G's visible tundish discharge is a narrower British example.

## Does not prove

It does not establish a continuous metal trough beneath every pipe in a UK house.

## Reference-house direction

Investigate pipe-in-pipe or accessible distribution first; add local passive containment where consequence justifies it.

**Evidence anchors:** Norwegian TEK17 §15-5; Approved Document G.

# Pattern 07 — Permanent opening / replaceable window

**Status:** Supported  
**Principles:** 2 Preserve permanent fabric; 3 Design the interface; 7 Architectural permanence; 8 Repose

## Problem

The opening and surrounding masonry can outlast frames, glazing, seals and ironmongery, yet ordinary installation may fuse the frame into plaster, foam and sealant so replacement damages sound work.

## Pattern

Treat the opening as enduring architecture and the window as a replaceable assembly within a deliberate interface managing fixing, drainage, thermal continuity, airtightness and movement.

## Forces

- high-performance installation needs robust air/water/thermal continuity;
- subframes can add thermal bridges and cost;
- concealed fixings should remain reachable;
- external seals still need renewal.

## Design requirements

- distinguish frame weathering from building-side drainage;
- define the mounting zone mechanically;
- avoid brittle finish bridging moving frame and masonry;
- draw full-frame replacement, not only glazing replacement;
- keep primary air/water layers intact during internal trim removal where possible;
- protect masonry edges during repeated work.

## Occupation / repose impact

The window should support daylight and outlook while allowing glare, solar gain, privacy, ventilation and external noise to be moderated intelligibly. Technical interfaces are acceptable only if the finished opening remains solid, quiet and composed.

## Failure modes

Subframe creates thermal bridge; sill drainage blocks; sealant becomes sole weather defence; reveal cannot be removed cleanly; future frame no longer fits the opening geometry.

## Evidence

Conservation guidance supports reversibility and minimising damage to enduring fabric. Design-for-disassembly guidance supports reversible connections between layers with different service lives.

## Does not prove

Every window does not need a proprietary removable subframe. The required outcome is replacement of the shorter-lived assembly without reconstructing the opening.

## Reference-house direction

Develop one 1:1 jamb/head/sill prototype and test water, air, thermal bridge and replacement sequence before multiplying the detail.

# Pattern 08 — Movement / slip junction

**Status:** Established principle  
**Principles:** 3 Design the interface; 11 Resolve technology as architecture

## Problem

Independently moving assemblies are often joined by brittle plaster, grout or filler, transferring predictable movement into cracking and edge damage.

## Pattern

Where relative movement is expected, use a deliberate gap, overlap, slip piece, movement joint, flexible replaceable seal or other suitable interface.

Define **bounded movement**, not vague looseness. Required support and restraint remain deterministic. Where useful, separate support, restraint, movement, sealing and finish.

## Forces

- movement allowance can be conspicuous;
- acoustic/fire/air boundaries may still need continuity;
- flexible seals are maintenance items;
- oversized gaps collect dirt and look crude.

## Design requirements

- quantify movement where significant;
- fix cover pieces to one controlling assembly rather than bridging both;
- use geometry to absorb tolerance;
- keep replaceable seals accessible;
- protect fragile finish edges;
- state which assembly controls the joint and which may move;
- prevent paint, filler or later decoration from locking the slip condition.

## Architectural resolution

Movement should read as a controlled architectural line rather than a defect. A conventional shadow, overlap or trim may be the quietest answer. Brass or bronze is appropriate only where it performs real work.

## Assembly and replacement sequence

Record how the cover or trim is installed, what it is fixed to, how movement remains free, how seals are renewed and how later decoration avoids bridging the joint.

## Occupation / repose impact

The joint should not click, rattle or become a persistent visual disturbance. Where visible, it should read as an intentional boundary rather than evidence of accidental separation.

## Failure modes

Trim fixed through both assemblies; paint bridges the slip; sealant treated as permanent; gap undersized; detail rattles or shadows badly.

## Evidence

Movement joints and differential-movement detailing are standard practice across masonry, facades and structures. The contribution here is to bring the same intentionality to domestic internal junctions.

## Reference-house direction

Conventional quiet joints by default; the functional cornice remains an experimental expression rather than the base pattern.

# Pattern 09 — Accessible rainwater route

**Status:** Established principle  
**Principles:** 4 Failure architecture; 5 Maintenance geography; 9 Passive first

## Problem

Blockage, undersizing or hidden failure in rainwater systems can wet large areas of fabric before the source is understood.

## Pattern

Arrange roof drainage so collection, outlets, overflows and principal downstream routes can be inspected and maintained, and so credible failure sheds or reveals water without driving it into concealed fabric.

## Forces

- external pipework affects elevations;
- gutters need safe access;
- concealed drainage may be architecturally unavoidable;
- climate change can alter rainfall intensity;
- open discharge can create splashback or icing.

## Design requirements

- size for project-specific rainfall and exposure;
- provide deliberate overflow/failure paths;
- make debris-prone points cleanable;
- provide safe access or eliminate the maintenance item;
- coordinate discharge with ground drainage;
- keep water away from vulnerable plinth/wall interfaces.

## Occupation / repose impact

Review intrusive rain noise, splash, staining and maintenance close to bedrooms, windows and entrances. The route should remain inspectable without making the facade read primarily as drainage infrastructure.

## Failure modes

Overflow enters cavity; blockage remains invisible; access requires unsafe ladder work; leaf guard itself blocks; ground gully wets the wall base.

## Evidence

Historic England's resilient-rainwater guidance stresses inspection, maintenance, capacity, climate resilience and access, while also recognising deep projecting eaves without rainwater goods on some buildings.

## Does not prove

Gutterless eaves are not universally superior. Exposure, entry points, paving, landscape and architecture determine suitability.

## Reference-house direction

Compare external gutters/downpipes with deep-eaves discharge rather than preserving v7's earlier preference by default.

**Evidence anchor:** Historic England, *Resilient Rainwater Systems*.

# Pattern 10 — Source-capture kitchen extract

**Status:** Established  
**Principles:** 9 Passive first; 5 Maintenance geography; 8 Repose

## Problem

Cooking produces heat, vapour, grease and airborne contaminants. Once dispersed through the house, these are harder to remove.

## Pattern

Capture the plume close to source with an appropriately sized hood or canopy and discharge it through a deliberate, maintainable route where project conditions permit.

## Forces

- high extract can depressurise an airtight dwelling;
- route length and bends reduce performance;
- termination affects facade and neighbours;
- grease needs recurring cleaning;
- combustion appliances can create pressure interactions;
- noise can discourage use.

## Design requirements

- size hood geometry to capture the plume;
- provide washable or replaceable grease filtration;
- keep duct route smooth and cleanable;
- provide replacement air as required;
- locate fan for acceptable noise and service access;
- commission actual airflow;
- coordinate with whole-house ventilation and combustion.

## Occupation / repose impact

The system must be quiet and simple enough to use. Controls should be obvious, low settings genuinely quiet, lighting integrated and cleaning straightforward. High nominal performance is irrelevant if occupants avoid the hood because of noise or drafts.

## Failure modes

Shallow hood misses plume; flexible duct accumulates grease; fan noise discourages use; replacement air omitted; duct cannot be cleaned.

## Evidence

Approved Document F establishes dwelling ventilation performance and requires commissioning/measurement of mechanical ventilation. The pattern adds the architectural emphasis on source capture, serviceable route and actual use.

## Reference-house direction

Explore a visually integrated hood or chimney-piece only after capture geometry and duct performance are fixed.

**Evidence anchor:** Approved Document F 2026.

# Pattern 11 — Roof maintenance route

**Status:** Established principle  
**Principles:** 1 Build for time; 5 Maintenance geography; 4 Failure architecture

## Problem

Roof outlets, chimneys, flashings, rooflights and rooftop equipment need maintenance, yet domestic design often leaves future workers to invent access later.

## Pattern

Where roof maintenance cannot be designed out, provide an intentional route to safe working positions, with appropriate fall-risk control and replacement access.

## Forces

- permanent access hardware affects appearance and waterproofing;
- roof geometry may make anchors insufficient;
- maintenance may be infrequent;
- access can create a security route.

## Design requirements

- minimise maintainable roof equipment first;
- define who must reach what and how often;
- provide safe approach and landing positions;
- coordinate hatches, edge strategy and temporary-access provision;
- consider equipment replacement, not only inspection;
- secure the route against unauthorised access.

## Occupation / repose impact

Hatches, ladders and anchors should not dominate principal rooms, reduce privacy or create avoidable drafts and noise. Assess rooftop equipment separately for visual, vibration and acoustic effects.

## Failure modes

Anchor does not suit actual work sequence; hatch opens onto unsafe slope; route crosses fragile rooflight; equipment can be serviced but not removed; security is ignored.

## Evidence

HSE HSG33 addresses roof work across new construction, repair, maintenance and cleaning, and includes clients, designers and specifiers in planning safe work.

## Reference-house direction

Use rear/secondary roof access only where it materially improves real tasks; otherwise design credible scaffold or MEWP interfaces.

**Evidence anchor:** HSE HSG33.

# Pattern 12 — Physical service index

**Status:** Proposed  
**Principles:** 10 Legibility; 5 Maintenance geography; 6 Ordinary parts

## Problem

Digital records can become inaccessible through lost accounts, obsolete software or poor handover. Extensive physical labelling can become stale and misleading.

## Pattern

Keep a deliberately small physical index at the building containing stable identifiers and essential operational information, linked to a richer digital building passport.

Typical physical content:

- main water/electrical/fuel isolation;
- route and riser identifiers;
- principal distribution-board references;
- emergency or record-location information;
- stable identifiers matching labels on actual components.

## Forces

- physical information must be updated after alterations;
- too much becomes visual noise;
- QR codes or URLs are not durable by themselves;
- detailed records belong in an editable information system.

## Design requirements

- prefer stable IDs to verbose descriptions;
- place the index where maintenance begins;
- pair IDs with drawings and digital records;
- assign change-control responsibility in the house manual;
- use durable, ordinarily readable formats.

## Occupation / repose impact

Concentrate the index where service activity begins rather than distributing labels through domestic rooms. Stable identifiers should reduce visible technical signage elsewhere.

## Failure modes

Plate becomes false after alteration; additions destroy clarity; digital record uses different naming; identifiers disappear during redecoration.

## Evidence

RIBA's Plan for Use recommends layered information: simple user guide, quick-start/emergency overview and detailed O&M information. The Building Safety Regulator's golden-thread regime, though not applicable to an ordinary detached house, provides a strong precedent for information remaining current, accessible, usable and transferable.

## Distinctive contribution

The physical index is the doctrine's resilience extension: enough critical information remains at the asset if external information systems fail.

## Reference-house direction

One restrained service board or plate in the plant hub, with identifiers repeated at corresponding components.

**Evidence anchors:** RIBA *Plan for Use Guide*; BSR golden-thread guidance.

# Catalogue-wide editorial controls

## Pattern maturity

Each final spread must show two independent labels:

**Evidence:** Established / Supported / Proposed / Experimental.  
**Maturity:** Drawn / Calculated / Costed / Mocked-up / Maintenance-tested / Built / Observed in use.

A pattern can be evidence-supported but project-immature.

## Mandatory “does not prove” box

Any pattern relying materially on institutional, heritage or overseas precedent should state what that precedent **does not** establish for a new London house. This prevents precedent laundering.

## Mandatory boundary ledger

Every pattern involving an opening, void or removable element must check:
structure / fire / smoke / acoustic / air / vapour / water / thermal / pest / security.

## Mandatory proportionality note

Every pattern must state the future task it supports and why permanent provision is justified.

## Mandatory permanent-fabric impact

Every pattern must state what irreversible anchors, penetrations, chases or embedded components it requires in permanent fabric and why.

## Mandatory architectural resolution

Every visible or occupant-facing pattern must answer:

> **How does this become architecture rather than equipment?**

The answer may be quiet. Technical accessibility, movement or replacement still needs compositional resolution.

## Mandatory assembly and replacement sequence

Every pattern involving a replaceable component must identify:

- what is removed first;
- fixing or release method;
- required tools and working clearance;
- withdrawal path;
- boundaries disturbed;
- method for reliable reinstatement.

## Tectonic honesty check

Pattern development must distinguish concealment from falsification. A concealing architectural element is legitimate when it has a real material/function and the condition behind it is resolved. Cosmetic continuity must not substitute for movement, access or construction detailing.
