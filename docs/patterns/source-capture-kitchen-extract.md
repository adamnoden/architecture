# HSA-P-010 — Source-Capture Kitchen Extract

## Context

Use where cooking generates heat, vapour, grease and airborne contaminants that can be captured close to the hob/cooking source before dispersing through the dwelling.

The pattern addresses the **local cooking plume**. It does not replace whole-house ventilation or purge ventilation requirements.

## Problem

Once cooking emissions spread through the kitchen and adjoining rooms, they are harder to remove. A nominally powerful extract system can still perform poorly if the hood does not intercept the plume, the duct is restrictive, replacement air is ignored or noise discourages occupants from using it.

## Pattern

> **Capture cooking emissions close to source with hood/canopy geometry suited to the plume, then discharge them through a deliberate, low-resistance and maintainable route where project conditions permit.**

The invariant is source capture plus usable/maintainable discharge—not a particular hood style or fan product.

## Forces

- high extract can depressurise an airtight dwelling;
- route length and bends reduce delivered airflow;
- termination affects façade, neighbours and recirculation risk;
- grease requires recurring cleaning;
- combustion appliances can create pressure interactions;
- high fan noise discourages use;
- the hood/canopy is visually prominent in a kitchen;
- duct depth can affect structure and cabinetry;
- recirculating hoods do not provide the same pollutant/moisture removal as extract to outside.

## Relationships

[Coherent Horizontal Service Route](coherent-horizontal-service-route.md) should not be interpreted as requiring the grease extract duct to join a general service spine. A short direct route is often better.

[Designed Structural Penetration](designed-structural-penetration.md) applies where the extract duct must cross significant structure or permanent boundaries.

A future bathroom source-extract pattern remains separate because grease, hood geometry and cooking-plume capture create distinct forces.

## Variants / implementation families

- wall-mounted hood/canopy with direct wall discharge;
- island canopy with vertical/ceiling route;
- architectural chimney-piece containing ordinary replaceable hood/fan components;
- remote inline fan with accessible grease filtration at source;
- short roof discharge where wall termination is unsuitable.

Downdraft or recirculating systems should be assessed on actual capture/removal performance rather than appearance alone.

## Design requirements

- size and position the hood/canopy to intercept the cooking plume;
- provide washable or replaceable grease filtration;
- keep the duct route smooth, appropriately sized and cleanable where required;
- minimise unnecessary bends/flexible duct;
- provide replacement/make-up air as required by the actual dwelling/system;
- locate fan/equipment for acceptable noise and service access;
- commission actual airflow;
- coordinate with whole-house ventilation, airtightness and any combustion appliances;
- design the external terminal to avoid nuisance/recirculation and allow maintenance.

## Proportionality

The route should be as direct as performance and architecture reasonably allow. Large concealed ceiling zones or major structural interventions are not justified merely to preserve a preferred hood location if changing the kitchen arrangement would solve the problem more simply.

## Boundary debt

Check:

- air/thermal continuity at external termination;
- condensation and grease within ductwork;
- fire/combustibility implications as applicable;
- structure at penetrations/supports;
- acoustic/vibration transfer;
- weathering at terminal;
- pressure interaction with ventilation/combustion systems.

## Permanent-fabric impact

A deliberate external penetration or roof termination may be justified because kitchen extract is a predictable long-life building need even though the fan/hood itself is replaceable. The building-side route/opening should survive equipment renewal where practical.

## Workmanship / tolerance

Duct routes must be coordinated using actual bend radii, insulation, supports and cabinetry/ceiling depth. Avoid site-built chains of crushed flexible duct and improvised reducers caused by nominal drawings that leave no real installation envelope.

## Occupation / repose impact

The system must be quiet and simple enough to use. Controls should be obvious, lower settings genuinely quiet, task lighting integrated and cleaning straightforward. High nominal performance is irrelevant if occupants avoid the hood because of noise or drafts.

## Architectural resolution

The visible hood can be an ordinary appliance, integrated joinery or an architectural chimney/canopy. The architectural treatment should follow capture geometry rather than shrinking or disguising the hood until it no longer works.

## Maintenance / replacement sequence

Record:

1. grease-filter removal/cleaning;
2. fan access and isolation;
3. duct inspection/cleaning route where required;
4. hood/fan replacement without destructive kitchen reconstruction;
5. terminal access;
6. recommissioning airflow after replacement.

## Failure modes

- hood too shallow/small to capture plume;
- flexible or undersized duct creates high resistance and grease accumulation;
- fan noise discourages use;
- replacement air ignored;
- duct cannot be cleaned or fan cannot be withdrawn;
- cabinetry blocks filters;
- external terminal recirculates pollutants or creates nuisance;
- visually integrated hood compromises capture geometry.

## Evidence

Approved Document F establishes dwelling ventilation performance and mechanical-ventilation commissioning/measurement requirements. The architectural contribution of this pattern is to resolve source-capture geometry, route maintainability and actual usability rather than treating extract as a nominal fan-rate specification alone.

**Evidence anchor:** Approved Document F 2026 and associated ventilation design/commissioning guidance.

## Does not prove

This pattern does not establish one hood airflow rate, one make-up-air strategy or one termination location for every kitchen. It also does not mean kitchen extract can be designed independently from whole-house ventilation and combustion safety.

## Reference House application

The Phase-5 Reference House run deliberately gives the east-side kitchen a direct external extract route rather than making grease extract travel around the courtyard through the general horizontal service topology. Any architectural chimney-piece comes only after capture geometry and duct performance are fixed.

## Formalisation boundary

Potentially formal consequences include:

- hood/hob relative geometry;
- duct continuity, diameter and bend count;
- fan/terminal identities;
- penetration/boundary obligations;
- maintenance access;
- commissioned airflow evidence.

Whether the hood is visually integrated, proportionate and quiet enough for ordinary domestic use remains architectural/product judgement.
