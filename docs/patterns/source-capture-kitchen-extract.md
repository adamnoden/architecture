# HSA-P-010 — Source-Capture Kitchen Extract

## Context

Cooking releases heat, water vapour, grease and airborne contaminants at a concentrated source.

## Problem

Once the plume disperses through the room and house it is harder to remove efficiently. Nominally powerful extract systems can still perform poorly because hood geometry, duct resistance, replacement air, noise or cleaning access are wrong.

## Pattern

**Capture the cooking plume close to source with an appropriately sized hood or canopy and discharge it through a deliberate, maintainable route where project conditions permit.**

The architectural problem is the whole capture relationship: source, hood geometry, duct route, replacement air, fan, termination and maintenance.

## Forces

- high extract rates can depressurise an airtight dwelling;
- route length and bends reduce actual airflow;
- facade/roof termination affects architecture and neighbours;
- grease requires recurring cleaning;
- combustion appliances can create pressure interactions;
- excessive noise discourages use.

## Design requirements

- size hood/canopy geometry to intercept the plume;
- provide washable or replaceable grease filtration;
- keep the duct route smooth, short where practical and cleanable;
- provide replacement air where required;
- locate fans and terminals for acceptable noise and service access;
- commission actual airflow;
- coordinate with whole-house ventilation and any combustion system.

## Proportionality

Source capture is usually justified because the pollutant source is intense and local. Complexity beyond ordinary extract must still be justified by actual cooking use, building airtightness and route conditions.

## Boundary debt

Ducts crossing walls, roofs or fire/acoustic boundaries must reinstate those boundaries deliberately. External terminals also create weather, pest and facade obligations.

## Permanent-fabric impact

Prefer one coordinated extract route over later coring or repeated changes. Fans, filters and controls should remain replaceable without reconstructing the route.

## Workmanship and tolerance

Avoid flexible ducting used as a substitute for coordination. Duct joints, gradients where condensation is possible, terminal clearances and access points should be buildable with ordinary competent workmanship.

## Occupation and repose

The system must be quiet and simple enough to use. Controls should be obvious, low settings genuinely quiet, lighting integrated and cleaning straightforward. High nominal performance is irrelevant if occupants avoid the hood because of noise or drafts.

## Failure modes

- shallow hood misses the plume;
- flexible or convoluted duct accumulates grease and resistance;
- fan noise discourages use;
- replacement air is omitted;
- duct cannot be cleaned;
- terminal causes nuisance, staining or recirculation.

## Evidence

Approved Document F establishes dwelling ventilation performance and commissioning/measurement expectations for mechanical ventilation. Established kitchen-ventilation practice supports source capture; HSA adds the architectural emphasis on capture geometry, maintainable route and actual use.

## Does not prove

One airflow number or hood type is not universally correct. Appliance type, cooking intensity, room geometry, airtightness and route resistance determine the design.

## Reference House application

The Reference House may integrate the hood architecturally—potentially into a chimney-like composition—but only after capture geometry and duct performance are fixed.

## Formalisation boundary

Source/hood relationship, duct continuity, route geometry, terminal and access occurrences can be represented formally. Capture effectiveness, acoustics and whole-house pressure interactions still require engineering evidence and commissioning.
