# HSA-P-011 — Roof Maintenance Route

## Context

Roof outlets, chimneys, flashings, rooflights and rooftop equipment may need inspection, cleaning, repair or replacement during the life of the building.

## Problem

Domestic roofs are often drawn as completed forms while future workers are left to invent access later. A nominal roof hatch or anchor can exist without providing a safe approach, landing, working position or replacement path.

## Pattern

**Where roof maintenance cannot be designed out, establish an intentional route from ordinary access to safe working positions, with fall-risk control and replacement access appropriate to the actual tasks.**

The route may use internal access, external scaffold/MEWP provision, temporary systems or combinations. Permanent hardware is not automatically preferred.

## Forces

- permanent access hardware affects appearance, security and waterproofing;
- roof geometry may make simple anchors insufficient;
- maintenance may be infrequent;
- equipment replacement needs more space than inspection;
- access can create an unauthorised security route.

## Design requirements

- minimise maintainable roof equipment first;
- define who must reach what, for which task and how often;
- provide a credible approach and landing condition;
- coordinate hatches, roof slope, fragile elements and edge strategy;
- consider equipment removal/replacement, not only inspection;
- secure any internal/permanent route against unauthorised use;
- preserve external ground/scaffold/MEWP access where that is the more proportionate solution.

## Proportionality

Rare low-consequence work may be better served by planned temporary access than permanent roof infrastructure. Permanent provision earns itself where tasks are frequent, difficult to improvise safely or especially sensitive to building geometry.

## Boundary debt

Roof hatches, anchors, walkways and equipment supports can affect weathering, insulation, airtightness, thermal continuity and structure. Access provision must not create a larger durability problem than the maintenance task it solves.

## Permanent-fabric impact

Penetrations and anchors should be sparse and designed. Where temporary access is the strategy, preserve the geometry and supporting ground that makes it feasible rather than embedding unnecessary hardware.

## Workmanship and tolerance

Hatch clearances, ladder/step geometry, anchor positions and safe landing areas must be checked against built geometry. Roof access should not depend on perfect nominal dimensions or workers improvising around obstructions.

## Occupation and repose

Hatches, ladders and anchors should not dominate principal rooms, reduce privacy or create avoidable drafts and noise. Rooftop plant must be considered separately for visual, vibration and acoustic effects.

## Failure modes

- anchor does not suit the actual work sequence;
- hatch opens onto an unsafe slope or edge condition;
- route crosses fragile rooflights;
- equipment can be serviced but not removed;
- future landscaping or extensions remove scaffold/MEWP access;
- security is ignored.

## Evidence

HSE HSG33 treats roof work across construction, repair, maintenance and cleaning and places duties on clients, designers and specifiers to plan safe work. The HSA pattern translates that into whole-life roof/access geometry.

## Does not prove

HSG33 does not imply that every house needs a permanent roof hatch, walkway or anchor system. The task-specific access strategy remains project-dependent.

## Reference House application

Rear/secondary roof access is used only where it materially improves real maintenance tasks. Otherwise the Reference House preserves credible scaffold/MEWP interfaces and external maintenance geography.

## Formalisation boundary

Maintenance tasks, target components, access paths, working volumes and replacement envelopes can be represented formally. Safe system selection and detailed fall-protection design remain competent professional work.
