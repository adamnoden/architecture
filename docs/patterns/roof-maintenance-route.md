# HSA-P-011 — Roof Maintenance Route

## Context

Use where roof outlets, chimneys, flashings, rooflights, terminals or rooftop equipment will require inspection, cleaning, repair or replacement during the building's life and those tasks cannot reasonably be designed out.

The route may be permanent, partly permanent or intentionally rely on temporary access equipment. The pattern does not imply a fixed ladder or roof hatch on every house.

## Problem

Domestic roofs routinely contain maintenance items while the building design provides no credible way to reach them. Future workers then improvise from ladders, fragile surfaces or unsuitable roof slopes, and replacement of larger components can be harder than ordinary servicing.

A nominal roof hatch is not meaningful access if it opens onto an unsafe position or the worker still cannot reach the task.

## Pattern

> **Where roof maintenance cannot be designed out, establish an intentional route from ordinary building/site access to safe task-specific working positions, including realistic inspection, repair and replacement needs.**

The invariant is maintenance geography, not permanent access hardware.

## Forces

- permanent access hardware affects appearance, security and waterproofing;
- roof geometry may make a hatch or anchor insufficient;
- maintenance may be infrequent enough that scaffold/MEWP access is more proportionate;
- chimneys, outlets and equipment create different working positions;
- fragile rooflights and steep slopes constrain routes;
- replacement can require much more handling space than inspection;
- safe external access can conflict with planting, bays, projections and courtyards.

## Relationships

[Accessible Rainwater Route](accessible-rainwater-route.md) often creates specific roof maintenance tasks at gutters, outlets and overflows.

[Ground-Supported Façade Access](ground-supported-facade-access.md), once its canonical migration is complete, can provide the site/ground conditions needed for scaffold, ladder or MEWP approaches where internal roof access is not the best answer.

The broader external-maintenance plan should coordinate the two rather than assuming every task starts from inside the roof.

## Variants / implementation families

- internal hatch to a safe landing/roof zone;
- permanent stair/ladder only where frequency and geometry justify it;
- designed anchorage/edge-protection strategy for a defined work method;
- scaffold-ready façade/ground conditions;
- MEWP-accessible site geometry;
- replaceable component positioned near a secondary/rear roof edge reachable from temporary equipment;
- designing out rooftop equipment entirely.

## Design requirements

- minimise maintainable roof equipment first;
- list the real roof tasks and expected frequency/consequence;
- define how a person reaches each task;
- provide safe approach and landing/working positions;
- coordinate hatches, slopes, fragile surfaces and edge strategy;
- consider component replacement, not only inspection;
- preserve a material-handling route for tools/replacement parts;
- secure internal/permanent access against unauthorised use;
- coordinate temporary-access assumptions with final landscape, gates, bays and projections.

## Proportionality

Infrequent tasks may be better served by deliberately preserved scaffold/MEWP access than by permanent roof ladders, walkways or anchors. Conversely, a regularly cleaned outlet hidden behind difficult roof geometry may justify permanent safe access.

The task should determine the infrastructure.

## Boundary debt

Check:

- roof waterproofing/air/thermal continuity at hatches/anchors;
- structural capacity of anchorage and working surfaces;
- fall risk and fragile surfaces;
- security;
- drafts/acoustic effects from access hatches;
- condensation around roof openings;
- fire separation where attic/roof access affects it.

## Permanent-fabric impact

Permanent hatches, anchors and structural supports should exist only where they materially improve a defined work method. Record their location, intended load/use and inspection requirements.

Where temporary access is the preferred strategy, preserve the site/geometry needed to deploy it rather than embedding unused hardware.

## Workmanship / tolerance

Roof hatches, ladders and anchors are safety-critical interfaces. Their as-built position must match the actual roof geometry/work method, not just a schematic plan symbol. Verify clear opening, landing position, edge distances and waterproofing before completion.

## Occupation / repose impact

Hatches, ladders and anchors should not dominate principal rooms, reduce privacy or create avoidable drafts/noise. Rooftop equipment should be reviewed separately for visual, vibration and acoustic effects.

## Architectural resolution

Place permanent access on secondary/rear architecture where practical, but do not create a convoluted unsafe route merely to hide it. A simple roof without permanent access hardware can be the better architectural solution if temporary access is deliberately supported.

## Maintenance / replacement sequence

For each roof task, record:

1. site/building approach;
2. access equipment and setup location;
3. transition onto/near the roof;
4. safe working position and fall controls;
5. tool/material handling;
6. replacement/removal path where relevant;
7. protection of roof finishes;
8. reinstatement and inspection after work.

## Failure modes

- anchor does not suit the actual work sequence;
- hatch opens onto unsafe slope;
- route crosses fragile rooflight;
- component can be serviced but not removed;
- landscape later blocks scaffold/MEWP setup;
- permanent ladder creates a security route;
- workers still need improvised access beyond the designed route;
- waterproofing around permanent hardware becomes a new maintenance liability.

## Evidence

HSE HSG33 addresses roof work across construction, repair, maintenance and cleaning, and makes clients/designers/specifiers part of planning safe roof work. HSA's architectural contribution is to make that future work method part of early building/site geometry rather than a contractor problem discovered after handover.

**Evidence anchor:** HSE HSG33, *Health and safety in roof work*.

## Does not prove

The pattern does not establish that every house needs a roof hatch, permanent anchor system or fixed ladder. Safe temporary access may be the more proportionate solution for infrequent tasks.

## Reference House application

The Reference House currently prefers rear/secondary roof access only where it materially improves real tasks; otherwise it preserves credible scaffold/MEWP access through the external maintenance plan. Roof terminals and equipment should remain near maintainable secondary geography where performance permits.

## Formalisation boundary

Potentially formal consequences include:

- maintenance-task identities;
- approach/working/withdrawal volumes;
- access-path continuity;
- roof hazard/fragile-zone exclusions;
- component replacement paths;
- scaffold/MEWP setup reservations.

Selection of the safest/proportionate work method still requires competent construction/maintenance judgement and cannot be proven by geometry alone.
