# HSA-P-009 — Accessible Rainwater Route

## Context

Roofs, gutters, outlets, overflows and downstream drainage must manage recurring rainfall while remaining exposed to debris, ageing and changing climate loads.

## Problem

Blockage, undersizing or hidden failure can wet large areas of fabric before the source is understood. Domestic roof drainage is often treated as an elevation afterthought rather than a maintainable water-management system.

## Pattern

**Arrange rainwater collection, outlets, overflows and principal downstream routes so debris-prone points can be inspected and maintained, and credible failure reveals or sheds water without driving it into concealed vulnerable fabric.**

The pattern does not prescribe gutters over deep eaves, or vice versa.

## Forces

- external pipework affects elevations;
- gutters and outlets need safe maintenance access;
- concealed drainage may sometimes be architecturally unavoidable;
- rainfall intensity and exposure are project-specific;
- overflow and open discharge can create splashback, staining or icing.

## Design requirements

- size for project-specific rainfall and exposure;
- provide deliberate overflow/failure paths;
- make debris-prone points cleanable;
- provide safe access or eliminate the maintenance item;
- coordinate discharge with ground drainage;
- keep concentrated water away from vulnerable wall-base interfaces;
- avoid concealed routes whose first visible symptom is internal damage.

## Proportionality

Access and redundancy should reflect blockage likelihood, consequence and roof geometry. A simple low eaves detail may need far less permanent access infrastructure than a complex roof with internal outlets.

## Boundary debt

Check weathering, cavity/moisture continuity, air/thermal interfaces at penetrations, and local splash/drainage consequences at the wall base and ground.

## Permanent-fabric impact

Brackets, outlets, chases and internal rainwater pipes can all consume long-lived fabric. Prefer routes that remain inspectable and replaceable without dismantling the envelope.

## Workmanship and tolerance

Falls, outlet heights and overflow levels must survive normal construction variation. A few millimetres of site error should not reverse drainage toward the building.

## Occupation and repose

Review rain noise, splash, staining and maintenance activity near bedrooms, windows and entrances. The route should remain inspectable without making the facade read primarily as drainage infrastructure.

## Failure modes

- overflow enters a cavity or roof build-up;
- blockage remains invisible;
- access requires unsafe improvisation;
- leaf guards become blockage points themselves;
- discharge wets the wall base;
- concealed internal route leaks without a visible failure path.

## Evidence

Historic England guidance on resilient rainwater systems stresses inspection, maintenance, capacity, climate resilience and access while recognising that different architectural forms use different rainwater strategies.

## Does not prove

External gutters, internal drainage and deep/gutterless eaves each remain context-dependent. The evidence does not establish one universal roof-drainage type.

## Reference House application

The Reference House compares ordinary external gutters/downpipes with deeper-eaves alternatives rather than preserving any earlier preference automatically. The chosen route must also coordinate with external maintenance geography.

## Formalisation boundary

Outlet locations, route continuity, overflow paths, maintenance access and discharge destinations can be represented formally. Hydraulic adequacy, exposure and architectural composition remain project-specific evidence/judgement.
