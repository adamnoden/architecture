# HSA-P-009 — Accessible Rainwater Route

## Context

Use wherever roofs, gutters, outlets, downpipes, concealed drains or equivalent geometry collect and discharge rainwater close to vulnerable building fabric.

The pattern applies whether the architectural solution uses conventional gutters/downpipes, internal/concealed drainage or—where exposure and site conditions support it—deep eaves and open discharge.

## Problem

Blockage, undersizing or hidden failure in rainwater systems can wet large areas of fabric before the source is understood. Domestic architecture often resolves the visible roofline first and leaves cleaning, overflow, access and failure paths as later technical details.

A system can therefore perform well on the day of completion yet fail badly when leaves, debris, extreme rainfall or inaccessible joints enter the picture.

## Pattern

> **Arrange rainwater collection, outlets, overflows and downstream discharge as a legible route that can be inspected and maintained, and give credible blockage or exceedance a deliberate path that reveals or sheds water without driving it into concealed vulnerable fabric.**

The invariant is accessible, fail-legible water routing—not one gutter type.

## Forces

- external pipework affects elevations;
- gutters and outlets require safe recurring access;
- concealed drainage may sometimes be architecturally unavoidable;
- rainfall intensity and climate exposure vary materially by project;
- guards/filters can themselves block;
- overflow water can cause splashback, icing, erosion or nuisance;
- site drainage and landscape determine whether discharged water is actually taken away;
- gutters/downpipes are ordinary maintainable components, not permanent fabric.

## Relationships

[Roof Maintenance Route](roof-maintenance-route.md) may provide the safe access needed for debris-prone roof components.

[Ground-Supported Façade Access](ground-supported-facade-access.md), once canonical migration is complete, may support inspection/repair of upper rainwater goods from outside.

The pattern should coordinate with site drainage and wall-base moisture design, but HSA deliberately rejected the old generic `Perimeter Dry Zone` catalogue slot because wall-base drainage solutions are context-sensitive and can conflict.

## Variants / implementation families

- external eaves gutter + external downpipe;
- hopper/downpipe arrangement;
- concealed box gutter with visible emergency overflow;
- internal outlet with inspectable/maintainable access;
- deep projecting eaves/open discharge where exposure, entrances, paving and landscape make it appropriate;
- sacrificial/visible overflow spout at a concealed collection point.

## Design requirements

- size collection/discharge for project-specific rainfall and exposure;
- provide deliberate overflow/exceedance paths;
- make debris-prone points cleanable;
- provide safe maintenance access or design out the maintainable item;
- coordinate discharge with ground/site drainage;
- keep water away from vulnerable plinth/wall interfaces;
- make concealed collection points inspectable enough that blockage is not discovered first by internal damp;
- distinguish normal discharge from an abnormal overflow signal where a visible tell-tale is used.

## Proportionality

Permanent access hardware or elaborate secondary drainage should correspond to actual task frequency/consequence. Equally, hiding ordinary gutters behind architecture that makes them unsafe to clean is not a valid economy.

## Boundary debt

Resolve as applicable:

- roof/wall weathering continuity;
- penetrations of air/thermal layers;
- overflow routes across façade details;
- freeze/icing risk;
- splashback/soil erosion;
- below-ground drainage capacity and inspection;
- acoustic effects from rainwater flow;
- safe access/fall risk.

## Permanent-fabric impact

The pattern should keep shorter-lived outlets, gutters and downpipes replaceable without rebuilding primary roof/wall construction where practical. Concealed outlet bodies or embedded drainage interfaces that must become permanent should be documented and justified.

## Workmanship / tolerance

Falls, outlet levels and overflow heights must be physically buildable within roof tolerances. A nominal fall that disappears through construction variation is not a drainage strategy.

Set clear datums for gutter/outlet levels, verify falls before concealment and protect drainage openings from construction debris.

## Occupation / repose impact

Review intrusive rain noise, splash, staining and maintenance close to bedrooms, windows and entrances. The route should remain inspectable without making the façade read primarily as drainage infrastructure.

## Architectural resolution

External rainwater goods can be explicit architectural elements or visually recessive ordinary components. Concealed systems are legitimate only when access, overflow and failure behaviour remain resolved.

Geometry should do the first work: drips, falls, overhangs and outward failure paths should reduce reliance on sealant or sensors.

## Maintenance / replacement sequence

Record:

1. how debris-prone points are reached;
2. how guards/outlets are cleaned;
3. where overflow becomes visible;
4. how downpipes/outlets are disconnected and replaced;
5. what roof/façade boundaries are disturbed;
6. how downstream drains are rodded/inspected;
7. how final landscape/site levels preserve access and discharge.

## Failure modes

- overflow enters a cavity or wall build-up;
- blockage remains invisible until internal damp appears;
- access requires unsafe improvised ladder work;
- leaf guard itself blocks;
- nominal overflow sits above a vulnerable threshold/detail;
- ground gully or splash wets the wall base;
- concealed fall is lost through construction tolerance;
- mature planting prevents maintenance.

## Evidence

Historic England's resilient-rainwater guidance stresses inspection, maintenance, capacity, climate resilience and access, while recognising that some buildings can use deep projecting eaves without conventional rainwater goods where context permits.

**Evidence anchor:** Historic England, *Resilient Rainwater Systems* / related rainwater guidance.

## Does not prove

Gutterless eaves are not universally superior; nor are external gutters automatically appropriate. Exposure, entries, paving, landscape, roof geometry and site drainage determine the right implementation.

## Reference House application

The Reference House should compare conventional external gutters/downpipes with any deep-eaves/open-discharge option rather than preserving an earlier doctrine preference by default. Courtyard drainage must be resolved as a first-class external-water problem rather than hidden beneath the service topology.

## Formalisation boundary

Potentially formal consequences include:

- collection/outlet/downstream route continuity;
- falls and overflow elevations;
- maintenance access geometry;
- roof/wall boundary transitions;
- discharge relationship to site drainage;
- inspection/rodding nodes.

Selection of visible versus concealed goods, acceptable façade expression and context-specific wall-base treatment remain architectural/site judgements.
