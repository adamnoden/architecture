# HSA-P-001 — Controlled Utility Entry

## Context

A house receives water, electricity, communications and potentially other utilities across the site and weather envelope. Different suppliers may control different external routes, but each service eventually crosses into permanent construction and joins internal distribution.

## Problem

When utilities are coordinated separately, each tends to acquire its own late penetration, isolation arrangement and improvised route. Later additions repeat the damage. The result is unnecessary disturbance of envelope and structure, poor records and difficult future alteration.

## Pattern

**Create a deliberate utility-entry condition where incoming services cross through coordinated, documented interfaces before joining internal distribution. Reserve future capacity only where it is proportionate and can be blanked safely.**

The pattern does not require every utility to share one literal penetration. Supplier rules, separation, fire, water or security requirements may justify several adjacent controlled crossings.

## Forces

- utilities have different statutory and supplier requirements;
- penetrations must preserve water, air, thermal, fire, pest and security performance;
- spare capacity can waste material or create weak points;
- buried and external routes may be partly outside the designer's control;
- isolation must remain accessible after fit-out and occupation.

## Relationships

This is an upstream service-topology pattern. It establishes where external networks become controlled internal infrastructure.

A [Plant Room as Service Hub](plant-room-service-hub.md) commonly completes the transition by concentrating internal isolation, distribution and replaceable plant, but neither pattern is a hard prerequisite for the other.

## Design requirements

- coordinate plausible utility routes before structure and envelope construction;
- separate incompatible services where required;
- keep principal isolation accessible;
- use designed sleeves or penetrations rather than later drilling;
- identify routes physically and in the building record;
- blank unused capacity robustly;
- keep the transition from buried/external infrastructure to inspectable internal infrastructure short and legible where practical.

## Proportionality

Permanent spare capacity is justified only for credible future services whose later entry would otherwise damage difficult-to-alter fabric. A speculative array of empty holes is not resilience.

## Boundary debt

Check weather, water, air, thermal, fire, acoustic, pest and security continuity at every entry. The entry zone should reduce uncontrolled penetrations, not concentrate several poorly resolved ones into one vulnerable location.

## Permanent-fabric impact

The pattern intentionally creates permanent crossings. Their value lies in being few, deliberate, documented and designed with the fabric rather than cut through it later.

## Workmanship and tolerance

Define sleeve/penetration geometry, fall where relevant, sealing sequence and acceptable variation before site work. A useful controlled entry should make omission or poor reinstatement visible rather than burying it behind later finishes.

## Occupation and repose

Meters, isolators and labels should remain accessible without becoming visual or acoustic clutter in principal rooms. Check intake equipment for hum, drafts and indicator light where these could affect occupied space.

## Failure modes

- unsealed openings;
- water tracking through sleeves;
- rodent entry;
- reserved capacity proving unusable;
- incompatible services crowded together;
- isolation becoming inaccessible behind storage or later joinery.

## Evidence

Historic England guidance on installing services supports common routes, existing openings, accessible routes and useful spare capacity where these reduce repeated fabric damage. Approved Document R provides a narrower UK precedent for planned communications infrastructure from building access point to network termination point.

## Does not prove

These precedents do not establish that every domestic utility should share one entry or that spare capacity is always worthwhile. Supplier requirements and project geometry remain controlling.

## Reference House application

The Reference House uses a side/rear intake condition close to the principal service hub so the transition into internal distribution can remain short, accessible and subordinate to the main elevations.

## Formalisation boundary

Potentially formalisable consequences include identified entry occurrences, declared boundary transitions, isolation access and permitted penetrations. Whether utilities share one zone or use several nearby entries remains project-specific architectural and engineering judgement.
