# HSA-P-001 — Controlled Utility Entry

## Context

Use where water, electricity, communications or other utilities cross from site/external infrastructure into the building. The pattern is most valuable where several services, future additions or sensitive envelope/structural boundaries would otherwise generate unrelated penetrations.

It does not require every utility to share one opening.

## Problem

Incoming utilities are often designed separately. Each system then acquires its own penetration, and later additions create still more openings through envelope and structure.

The result is not merely visual untidiness. Every crossing can create structural, weather, air, thermal, fire, pest, security and maintenance obligations.

## Pattern

> **Create a deliberate utility-entry zone where incoming services cross through coordinated, documented interfaces before joining internal distribution. Reserve spare sleeves or capacity only where a foreseeable future use justifies them.**

The invariant is coordinated entry geography, not a single proprietary box or common hole.

## Forces

- utilities have different statutory and supplier requirements;
- water, air, thermal, fire, pest and security performance must survive the crossing;
- utility providers may control parts of the external route;
- useful spare capacity can avoid later destructive work;
- unnecessary spare openings waste material, weaken boundaries or become uncontrolled routes;
- buried external work and finished internal construction have very different maintenance conditions.

## Relationships

This pattern is normally resolved before the internal service topology is fixed. It often sits beside a [Plant Room as Service Hub](plant-room-service-hub.md), but neither pattern requires the other.

Each actual crossing may invoke [Designed Structural Penetration](designed-structural-penetration.md) where structure or a significant boundary is crossed.

These are sequence/composition relationships, not reasons to force graph edges where a project can legitimately arrange them differently.

## Variants / implementation families

- several adjacent supplier-specific entries within one coordinated zone;
- sleeves or ducts brought to a common external service area;
- a meter/intake cabinet feeding an internal plant/service hub;
- one or more spare blanked sleeves where a credible future service is known;
- direct entry into a dedicated service room where supplier and boundary conditions allow it.

## Design requirements

- coordinate entry routes before structure and envelope construction;
- separate incompatible services as required;
- keep principal isolation accessible;
- use designed sleeves, ducts or penetrations rather than later drilling where practical;
- identify principal routes physically and in the building record;
- blank unused capacity robustly;
- keep the transition from buried/external to inspectable internal infrastructure short and legible;
- coordinate external excavation/replacement access with landscape, thresholds and site logistics.

## Proportionality

Permanent entry infrastructure should correspond to real utilities and credible foreseeable additions. A large speculative intake chamber or collection of unused sleeves is not justified merely because future change is possible.

## Boundary debt

Check, as applicable:

- structure;
- fire and smoke;
- weather and bulk water;
- airtightness;
- thermal continuity;
- moisture/cavity behaviour;
- pests;
- security;
- supplier separation requirements.

The fact that several crossings occupy one area does not allow their boundary obligations to be merged indiscriminately.

## Permanent-fabric impact

The pattern intentionally permits a small number of deliberate crossings through permanent construction so that future services do not create repeated ad-hoc drilling elsewhere.

Record sleeves, ducts, no-drill zones, blanked capacity and any permanent embedded interfaces.

## Workmanship / tolerance

Define sleeve/opening geometry and boundary reinstatement before the relevant structure/envelope work is complete. Supplier equipment, masonry, ducts and cabinets may arrive to different tolerances; provide a clear controlling datum and a bounded adjustment/sealing zone rather than leaving the last trade to improvise.

## Occupation / repose impact

Meters, isolators and labels should be accessible without becoming a visual or acoustic presence in principal rooms. Check intake equipment for hum, drafts and indicator light where these could affect occupied space.

## Architectural resolution

Entry equipment belongs on a secondary/service elevation or within real architecture where practical. Concealment is legitimate; the crossing and maintenance access behind it must remain real and resolved.

## Maintenance / replacement sequence

For each utility, show how a future trade reaches the intake, isolates the service, opens the relevant enclosure, withdraws/replaces equipment or a route component, and reinstates all affected boundaries without disturbing unrelated utilities.

## Failure modes

- unsealed openings;
- water tracking through sleeves;
- rodent entry;
- spare capacity proving unusable;
- supplier equipment obstructing another route;
- meters or isolators becoming inaccessible behind storage or landscaping;
- later additions bypassing the coordinated zone because the reserved geometry was unrealistic.

## Evidence

Historic England recommends common routes, existing openings, accessible routes and useful spare capacity where services would otherwise repeatedly damage fabric. Approved Document R demonstrates the legitimacy of planned physical communications infrastructure from a building access point to network termination.

**Evidence anchors:** Historic England, *Installing New Services*; Approved Document R.

## Does not prove

Conservation and communications guidance do not establish that every utility should share one literal penetration or that every new detached house needs spare capacity for every imaginable service. Supplier, safety and separation requirements may justify several adjacent controlled crossings.

## Reference House application

The Phase-5 Reference House run uses provisional occurrence `RH-P001-01`: an east-side utility-entry zone beside the plant/service hub, reached from the front boundary along the service side. Actual utility records can invalidate that occurrence without invalidating this pattern.

## Formalisation boundary

Potentially formal consequences include:

- utility-entry occurrence identity and location;
- envelope/boundary transitions;
- separation constraints where known;
- isolation/access geometry;
- route continuity into internal distribution;
- evidence obligations created by penetrations.

Whether the entry zone is proportionate, architecturally subordinate and appropriately placed on the site remains architectural/project judgement.
