# HSA-P-021 — Source-Capture Bathroom Extract

## Context

Bathing and showering create a short-duration, concentrated moisture source in a room whose surfaces and adjacent construction are vulnerable to prolonged high humidity.

## Problem

A generic extract fan can satisfy nominal provision yet remove moisture slowly because the terminal is poorly located, transfer air is weak, the duct route is resistive, the fan is noisy or maintenance access is poor.

## Pattern

**Capture bathroom moisture close to the source with a deliberately placed extract point and a short, maintainable air path, supported by a clear transfer-air route and controls quiet/simple enough to be used consistently.**

The invariant is the source-to-terminal-to-discharge relationship, not one fan type or ceiling position.

## Forces

- shower/bath moisture is concentrated but intermittent;
- electrical safety constrains equipment locations;
- extract needs replacement air;
- long/bent ducts reduce performance and complicate condensate management;
- fan noise discourages use;
- external terminals affect façade/roof and can admit weather/pests;
- over-extraction can create drafts and interact with whole-house ventilation.

## Design requirements

- place the extract point to intercept moisture before broad room dispersal where practical;
- provide a deliberate transfer-air path;
- keep duct routes short and smooth where possible;
- manage condensate where route/temperature requires it;
- make fan, filter/grille and controls maintainable;
- commission actual airflow;
- coordinate with the whole-house ventilation strategy.

## Proportionality

The pattern applies where mechanically assisted bathroom extraction is part of the environmental strategy. Passive-first does not mean refusing mechanical extraction where moisture control requires it.

## Boundary debt

Duct penetrations must resolve air, thermal, weather, fire/acoustic and pest duties as applicable. A route through cold space may create condensation obligations.

## Permanent-fabric impact

Prefer one coordinated duct/terminal route over later coring or improvised ceiling routes. Replaceable fans and grilles should not require destructive work on the surrounding finish.

## Workmanship and tolerance

Duct geometry, terminal clearances, condensate fall and access should survive normal installation variation. Flexible ducting should not be used to rescue unresolved route geometry.

## Occupation and repose impact

Low noise matters because an extractor that occupants avoid is functionally weak. Controls should not require continuous attention; automatic assistance may be useful where it remains understandable and does not create nuisance operation.

## Failure modes

- extract point is remote from the moisture plume;
- transfer air is inadequate;
- duct is long, crushed or sharply bent;
- condensate accumulates;
- fan noise discourages use;
- grille/fan cannot be cleaned or replaced;
- terminal causes drafts, staining or recirculation.

## Evidence

Approved Document F establishes ventilation performance and commissioning expectations. Phase 6 admitted a separate bathroom pattern because moisture source geometry, transfer air, condensate, noise and maintenance differ materially from grease-laden kitchen extraction.

## Does not prove

The pattern does not establish one terminal position, fan type or ventilation system for every bathroom. Whole-house ventilation strategy and room geometry remain controlling.

## Reference House application

The Reference House keeps bathroom extract routes short and aligned with the service topology, while treating quiet operation and maintainable fan/duct access as part of the room design rather than an afterthought.

## Formalisation boundary

Source/terminal relationship, duct route, transfer-air occurrence and maintenance access can be represented formally. Actual moisture-removal performance, acoustics and commissioning remain technical evidence.
