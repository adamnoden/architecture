# HSA-P-008 — Movement / Slip Junction

## Context

Use where two assemblies or materials can move differently through structural deflection, moisture, thermal change, settlement, vibration or construction tolerance and a brittle finish would otherwise bridge them.

The pattern is unnecessary where the materials genuinely act as one stable assembly and relative movement is negligible within the finish capacity.

## Problem

Independently moving assemblies are commonly made visually continuous with plaster, grout, filler or paint. Predictable relative movement is then transferred into cracking, edge damage or seal failure.

The opposite mistake is to use vague “flexibility” as a substitute for required structural restraint.

## Pattern

> **Where relative movement is expected, give it a bounded, deliberate interface: a gap, overlap, slip piece, movement joint, replaceable flexible seal or other suitable detail. Keep required support and restraint deterministic.**

Where useful, separate support, restraint, movement, sealing and finish rather than asking one brittle junction to perform all of them.

## Forces

- movement allowance can be visually conspicuous;
- fire, acoustic, air and water boundaries may still need continuity;
- flexible seals are maintenance items;
- oversized gaps collect dirt and look crude;
- undersized gaps lock up or crack;
- trims can rattle if not controlled;
- fixing both sides of a cover piece defeats the slip condition;
- decoration can inadvertently bridge a carefully designed joint.

## Relationships

This pattern often appears at replaceable-window, wall/ceiling, floor/wall, panel and threshold interfaces.

It should not be confused with structural freedom. A structural strategy must establish what is supported/restrained first; this pattern manages only the movement that is intentionally permitted.

## Variants / implementation families

- open shadow/reveal;
- overlap or cover strip fixed to one side only;
- sliding/slip trim;
- conventional movement joint;
- replaceable elastomeric/flexible seal;
- concealed gap behind architectural moulding;
- sacrificial joint material where appropriate.

## Design requirements

- quantify expected movement where significant;
- identify which assembly controls the joint and which may move;
- fix cover pieces to one controlling assembly unless a tested detail says otherwise;
- use geometry to absorb credible construction tolerance;
- keep replaceable seals accessible;
- protect fragile finish edges;
- preserve required fire/acoustic/air/water continuity independently;
- prevent paint, filler or later decoration from locking the movement condition.

## Proportionality

Do not introduce movement joints everywhere merely because buildings move. Use them where relative movement is credible enough that brittle continuity is likely to fail or where later disassembly requires a deliberate interface.

## Boundary debt

Depending on location, resolve:

- fire/smoke continuity;
- acoustic sealing/flanking;
- airtightness;
- weather/water management;
- thermal continuity;
- pest control;
- structural restraint/support;
- wear and cleanability.

A visible gap does not excuse a failed hidden boundary.

## Permanent-fabric impact

The pattern often reduces damage by keeping brittle finish away from a movement line. Any permanent tracks, anchors or embedded joint formers should be sparse and assigned to the slower assembly that actually controls the interface.

## Workmanship / tolerance

Set a real expected movement/tolerance range, not a nominal “5 mm gap” detached from construction accuracy. Protect the joint during decorating and define acceptance when incoming work is outside the recoverable range.

The detail should remain recognisable to a competent installer without requiring undocumented site interpretation.

## Occupation / repose impact

The joint should not click, rattle, catch dirt or become a persistent visual disturbance. Where visible, it should read as an intentional architectural boundary rather than accidental separation.

## Architectural resolution

Movement should become a controlled line, shadow, overlap or conventional trim. Brass/bronze can be appropriate where the metal performs real work, but technical visibility is not a virtue by itself. The quietest conventional detail should win when it performs best.

## Assembly / replacement sequence

Record:

1. which assembly establishes the controlling datum;
2. when the gap/movement allowance is formed;
3. what the trim/seal is fixed to;
4. how movement remains free after finishes are complete;
5. how seals/trims are renewed;
6. how later decoration avoids bridging the joint.

## Failure modes

- trim fixed through both assemblies;
- paint/plaster/filler bridges the slip condition;
- sealant treated as permanent;
- gap undersized;
- movement allowance so large that the joint looks unresolved;
- detail rattles, clicks or shadows badly;
- required structural restraint is omitted under the banner of movement;
- hidden boundary continuity fails behind a visually neat joint.

## Evidence

Movement joints and differential-movement detailing are standard practice across masonry, facades, structures and fit-out. HSA's contribution is to apply the same intentional decomposition to ordinary domestic junctions and replaceable layers rather than letting brittle finish conceal the relationship.

## Does not prove

The pattern does not justify sliding freedom where structural restraint is required, nor does it establish brass/bronze or any other expressive detail as superior to an ordinary movement joint.

## Reference House application

The current Reference House direction uses conventional quiet slip/movement joints by default. A functional cornice remains an experimental architectural expression of this pattern and does not have independent pattern status.

## Formalisation boundary

Potentially formal consequences include:

- joint identity and controlling assembly;
- declared movement range;
- fixing-side constraints;
- gap/overlap geometry;
- boundary obligations;
- replaceable-seal lifecycle.

Whether the line is visually calm, proportionate and appropriate to the room remains architectural judgement.
