# HSA-P-008 — Movement / Slip Junction

## Context

Two assemblies meet but are expected to move differently because of material behaviour, structural movement, tolerance, installation sequence or different support conditions.

## Problem

Brittle plaster, grout, filler or trim is often used to bridge the junction as if both assemblies were one. Predictable relative movement then appears as cracking, tearing, edge damage or noisy rubbing.

## Pattern

**Where relative movement is expected, give it a deliberate bounded interface: gap, overlap, slip piece, movement joint, flexible replaceable seal or another suitable junction. Keep required support and restraint deterministic.**

Movement is not looseness. The interface should separate support, restraint, movement, sealing and finish where those duties differ.

## Forces

- movement allowance can become visually conspicuous;
- fire, acoustic, air or moisture boundaries may still need continuity;
- flexible seals are maintenance items;
- oversized gaps look crude and collect dirt;
- later decoration can accidentally lock the intended slip condition.

## Design requirements

- quantify expected movement where significant;
- state which assembly controls the joint and which may move;
- fix cover pieces to one controlling assembly rather than bridging both;
- use geometry to absorb construction tolerance;
- keep replaceable seals accessible;
- protect fragile finish edges;
- prevent filler, paint or later trim from defeating movement.

## Proportionality

Use the pattern where movement is credible and consequential. Do not introduce elaborate joints to celebrate hypothetical movement that ordinary construction can safely absorb.

## Boundary debt

Where the junction carries fire, smoke, acoustic, air, vapour or moisture duties, those must remain continuous through the movement range rather than being delegated to brittle finish.

## Permanent-fabric impact

Usually modest. The main benefit is preventing repeated damage to adjacent long-lived fabric and finish by allowing movement at the intended interface.

## Workmanship and tolerance

The joint must tolerate realistic site variation without relying on a perfect nominal gap. Define adjustment and finish sequence so later trades cannot unknowingly bridge the interface.

## Occupation and repose

The joint should not click, rattle or become a persistent visual disturbance. Where visible, it should read as an intentional architectural boundary rather than evidence of accidental separation.

## Failure modes

- trim fixed through both assemblies;
- paint or filler bridges the slip;
- sealant is treated as permanent;
- gap is undersized;
- detail rattles or creates distracting shadow lines;
- movement provision masks inadequate structural restraint.

## Evidence

Movement joints and differential-movement detailing are established practice across masonry, facades and structures. The HSA contribution is to carry the same intentionality into domestic internal interfaces and replaceable layers.

## Does not prove

A visible metal joint is not inherently more honest or better. Conventional quiet overlaps, shadow gaps or flexible seals may be superior where they perform the required work.

## Reference House application

The Reference House uses conventional quiet joints by default. Brass/bronze is considered only where it performs real wear, slip or protection work.

## Formalisation boundary

Expected movement range, controlling assembly, permitted movement and boundary duties can be recorded formally. Whether the visible joint is proportionate and compositionally quiet remains architectural judgement.
