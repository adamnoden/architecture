# HSA-P-015 — Accessible Room Service Route

## Context

A room needs local electrical, data, control or other small-scale distribution after primary building routes have reached its vicinity.

## Problem

Local distribution is commonly achieved by chasing permanent walls or hiding cables and small pipes wherever a cavity happens to exist. Future alteration repeats the damage or creates undocumented routes.

## Pattern

**Where local service demand warrants it, route suitable room-scale services through removable architectural layers or joinery rather than routinely chasing permanent fabric.**

The invariant is the accessible room-side route, not service skirting, architrave wiring or any other one implementation.

## Variants

- removable/serviceable skirting;
- door-surround or architrave route;
- removable wall-side joinery;
- shallow dry service panels or rails integrated into real room architecture.

Do not turn every moulding or item of joinery into infrastructure merely because it can carry a cable.

## Forces

- routes must remain architecturally quiet;
- different service classes require separation and protection;
- accessible layers consume depth and can become visually technical;
- heavy or wet services may be inappropriate for shallow room routes;
- ordinary fixing and furniture must not damage hidden services.

## Proportionality

Use the pattern where change frequency, service density or permanent-fabric value justifies it. A conventional concealed route may remain better for a stable low-demand room.

## Boundary debt

Do not use local accessibility to bypass fire, acoustic, wet-zone or envelope boundaries. Room routes should normally remain on the safe side of primary boundaries and cross them only through designed interfaces.

## Permanent-fabric impact

The purpose is to shift repeated change into replaceable layers. Permanent anchors for joinery/rails may still be necessary, but should be sparse and deliberate.

## Workmanship and tolerance

The route should remain accessible after normal flooring, decoration and joinery installation. Define no-fix/service zones clearly enough that later ordinary work does not create a hidden hazard.

## Occupation / repose impact

The room should read as architecture first. Skirtings, architraves and joinery may perform technical work, but should not acquire the appearance, rattle or visual density of commercial trunking.

## Failure modes

- service route becomes an indiscriminate catch-all;
- later furniture blocks access;
- service segregation is lost;
- removable trim is painted or filled shut;
- technical module pressures distort room proportions;
- occupants bypass the intended route because ordinary fixing points are unclear.

## Evidence

Mature trunking and service-zone practice support the underlying accessibility principle. The domestic architectural integration of those routes remains HSA's design proposition.

## Does not prove

This pattern does not establish that all room wiring should be exposed or removable, or that every traditional moulding should carry services.

## Reference House application

The Reference House may use substantial skirting and selected door/joinery zones as genuine service routes where they coincide with the Georgian room grammar, while leaving other walls technically quiet.

## Formalisation boundary

Declared route zones, permitted service classes, access points and no-fix relationships can be represented formally. Whether the route remains visually convincing and proportionate is architectural judgement.
