## Document scope and status

This document sets out the design doctrine for a new house intended to remain beautiful, safe, healthy, adaptable, understandable, repairable and structurally intact over a very long life.

It is not a site-specific construction specification. A separate implementation document should translate the doctrine into a particular plot, plan, structure, set of dimensions, calculations and product choices. The likely first implementation is a relatively compact detached house on a medium-sized London plot, probably no more than five bedrooms, but the doctrine is intended to remain useful independently of that project.

The document distinguishes between three kinds of statement:

- **Fixed principles** are intended to survive changes in plan, technology and construction method.
- **Preferred implementations** are current design directions that appear to satisfy the doctrine well but remain open to engineering and site-specific revision.
- **Open design work** identifies questions that are understood but not yet resolved.

The language is deliberate:

- **must** indicates a requirement that follows directly from the doctrine;
- **should** indicates a strong design preference that may be displaced by a better solution;
- **may** indicates an option rather than a requirement.

The document should be read as a whole. Individual details are not independent: structure, services, moisture, movement, maintenance and architectural expression are expected to be designed together.

The unifying model is the house as a long-lived architectural platform: permanent architecture provides the durable framework within which shorter-lived systems can change without destructive rebuilding.

### Interpretation and precedence

This document is a design doctrine, not a substitute for statutory requirements, engineering calculations, specialist design or site-specific construction information.

Where provisions appear to conflict, they should be interpreted in the following order:

1. life safety, legal compliance, structural integrity and basic health;
2. the fixed principles of the doctrine;
3. the detailed system guidance in the body of this document;
4. current preferred implementations;
5. explanatory examples and text diagrams.

Open design work records unresolved questions and is not itself a construction requirement.

The doctrine register near the end of the document is a compact summary of the fixed principles. The detailed sections define their intended meaning. If a short register entry appears ambiguous, the corresponding detailed section governs.

### Text-diagram conventions

Text diagrams in this document are schematic and not to scale. They communicate topology, layering, access, movement or service relationships; they are not fabrication drawings and do not establish dimensions, structural adequacy, fire ratings, waterproofing details or regulatory compliance unless the accompanying text explicitly says so.

Unless labelled otherwise:

- arrows indicate flow, hierarchy or intended movement rather than a dimension;
- solid blocks or heavy lines indicate a boundary, structure or substantial layer;
- boxes indicate assemblies, zones or replaceable components;
- penetrations shown through a boundary are deliberate controlled crossings, not open voids.

The written requirement takes precedence over the diagram.

### Key terms

**Permanent fabric** means the parts of the building intended to have the longest life and to remain substantially undisturbed: foundations, primary masonry, major structural members, primary floor and roof structure, and the long-lived structural or masonry portions of the external envelope. Explicitly replaceable elements such as windows, doors, seals, many roof coverings and service components are not made permanent merely because they contribute to the enclosure.

**Architectural platform** means the long-lived physical framework of structure, envelope, movement interfaces, service routes, access, drainage, fixing infrastructure and spare capacity within which shorter-lived systems can be installed, maintained, altered and replaced without consuming the permanent fabric.

**Replaceable systems** means services, plant, controls, finishes, fittings and other components whose useful life is materially shorter than that of the permanent fabric.

**Designed interface** means a deliberate junction between assemblies that manages movement, tolerance, sealing, access, replacement or load transfer rather than concealing the junction with filler or brittle finish.

**Service zone** means an accessible space deliberately reserved for building services.

**Maintenance geography** means the spatial organisation of plant, risers, wet rooms, undercrofts, roof access, external services and other maintenance-intensive elements into coherent and predictable routes.

**Functional longevity** means the ability of the house to remain useful as occupants, technology, climate and patterns of domestic life change, without requiring destructive alteration of the permanent architecture.

## Governing doctrine

### Design priorities

The design hierarchy is:

1. **Longevity through serviceability.** The permanent building should survive because shorter-lived components can move, wear, fail, be maintained and eventually be replaced without damaging it.
2. **Beauty and architectural integrity.** Technical requirements should be resolved through architecture and craftsmanship wherever possible rather than accepted as visually intrusive compromises.
3. **Other performance objectives.** Thermal performance, construction efficiency, flexibility, automation and similar objectives remain relevant, but they are subordinate to the first two priorities. Life safety, legal compliance, structural integrity and basic health are constraints on the design rather than tradeable preferences.

Longevity and serviceability are treated as a single objective. A structure cannot realistically be long-lived if ordinary maintenance repeatedly requires cutting into it.

### The house as a long-lived platform

The house should be conceived as a long-lived architectural platform rather than as a finished static object.

Its permanent fabric provides structure, environmental protection, movement accommodation, service routes, access, drainage, fixing infrastructure, standard interfaces and capacity for future change. Shorter-lived services, equipment, finishes and technologies are expected to move, wear, fail, become obsolete and be replaced repeatedly within that framework.

The permanent architecture should therefore remain stable precisely because change has been given deliberate places to occur. Replaceable systems should attach to, pass through or occupy the platform through designed interfaces rather than becoming materially entangled with it.

This principle does not imply a visually modular or technical architecture. The completed rooms may appear solid, classical and permanent. Modularity belongs primarily to the construction logic behind the finished architecture.

### Permanent fabric and services

**No service is permitted inside the permanent fabric of the building.**

Permanent fabric includes, broadly:

- foundations;
- primary masonry;
- major beams;
- primary floor structure;
- roof structure;
- the long-lived structural or masonry portions of the external envelope.

Replaceable systems include, broadly:

- water supply and drainage;
- gas;
- electricity;
- heating;
- ventilation;
- communications and controls;
- future service technologies.

Services may pass through permanent fabric only through openings designed as part of the structural design. Where a crossing is necessary, it must use a deliberately designed sleeve, opening or service penetration. Structural design and service design must therefore proceed together. Joists should not be drilled retrospectively because a pipe needs a route, and masonry should not be chased because another socket is required.

The building should be arranged so that shorter-lived systems are accessible, identifiable, isolatable and replaceable without destructive work to longer-lived fabric.

### Movement and interfaces

The building is expected to expand, contract, deflect, settle and otherwise move during its life. These movements should be accommodated at designed interfaces rather than expressed as cracks or damage.

**No brittle finish should bridge two assemblies that are expected to move independently.**

**Joints between independently moving or replaceable assemblies should remain mechanically and visually articulated rather than being concealed beneath brittle finishes.**

The general construction logic is:

```text
ASSEMBLY A
    │
    ▼
┌──────────────────────────────┐
│      DESIGNED INTERFACE      │
│ movement • tolerance • seal  │
│ fixing • access • renewal    │
└──────────────────────────────┘
    │
    ▼
ASSEMBLY B
```

### Failure tolerance

The building should be designed around foreseeable failure as well as normal operation. Where practical, a fault should become visible, containable and accessible before it damages permanent fabric.

Short-lived protective components may fail; the surrounding architecture should not depend on their permanent perfection.

### Maintenance geography

Maintenance should have an architectural plan of its own. Plant, risers, wet rooms, undercrofts, roof access, external utility routes and other maintenance-intensive elements should be organised into coherent paths rather than scattered arbitrarily through the building.

A trade should be able to reach the relevant system by an obvious route, with adequate working space, without turning formal occupied rooms into work sites.

### Commodity repairability

The house should avoid technological lock-in.

Ordinary systems should not depend unnecessarily on:

- proprietary consumables;
- obscure fittings;
- manufacturer-specific tooling;
- cloud services;
- discontinued control systems;
- scarce specialists.

A competent plumber, electrician, carpenter or other established trade should encounter familiar materials, standard interfaces and legible construction. Specialist work that is inherently regulated, such as gas work, remains specialist work; the architecture should nevertheless make that specialist's access and understanding straightforward.

Product selection should favour systems that can be repaired or replaced using widely available parts and skills. A technically elegant system that becomes unserviceable when one manufacturer disappears is generally inferior to a simpler system with broad trade familiarity.

### Lifespan hierarchy

The building should not be divided only into "permanent" and "replaceable" parts. Components should be understood in broad lifespan bands.

For example:

- foundations and primary masonry may be intended to last for centuries;
- roof coverings, windows and major joinery may last for many decades with maintenance;
- plant and mechanical equipment may last for decades;
- controls and electronics may have substantially shorter lives;
- coatings, seals and decorative finishes may require regular renewal.

Assemblies should respect these differences. A short-lived component should not be placed somewhere that requires damage to a longer-lived component when replacement becomes necessary.

As a general rule, **an inaccessible component should not have a materially shorter expected life than the assembly that conceals it**. If its life is shorter, it should be made accessible, inspectable or independently replaceable.

### Functional longevity and appropriate permanence

Long life requires more than physical survival. The house should remain useful as households, bodies, technologies and environmental conditions change.

Adaptability should therefore be concentrated where change is likely and valuable: services, fit-out, secondary partitions, controls, equipment and selected circulation provisions. The doctrine does not require every wall, room or architectural relationship to become movable.

The principal spatial order of the house may be deliberately permanent. Major rooms, structural bays, stairs, facade openings, axes, proportions and other defining architectural elements should be capable of enduring for generations. Flexibility should occur within and around that order rather than by reducing the house to a generic open shell.

The design should distinguish between:

- **enduring architecture**, whose permanence gives the house character, order and structural clarity;
- **adaptable building layers**, which may reasonably change with occupants or use;
- **replaceable systems**, which are expected to wear out or become obsolete repeatedly.

Modularity is a means of preserving the enduring architecture, not an objective in itself.

## Design governance, standardisation and bespoke work

The doctrine requires unusual coordination, but it should not result in a house composed of hundreds of unrelated one-off inventions. Excessive bespoke design would create the same long-term dependency that the doctrine is intended to remove: future owners could become dependent on lost drawings, vanished manufacturers, unique spare parts or specialist knowledge.

The preferred approach is therefore to make the **building logic unusual but the individual components as ordinary as practical**.

Custom design should be concentrated at interfaces, access systems and architectural elements where it produces a material improvement in serviceability, durability or beauty. Ordinary components should remain ordinary wherever they already satisfy the doctrine.

### Standardisation hierarchy

When choosing how to solve a detail, the design team should generally prefer solutions in the following order:

1. a widely available standard component used conventionally;
2. a standard component installed within a deliberately designed accessible arrangement;
3. a bespoke interface or enclosure made from standard materials, sections, fasteners and fabrication methods;
4. a repeatable custom component family used in multiple locations;
5. a unique custom mechanism or proprietary system only where the preceding options cannot satisfy the requirement.

A bespoke architectural enclosure around a commodity valve, fan, cable, switch or appliance is generally preferable to a bespoke valve, fan, connector or control system.

The long life of the house should not depend upon the continued availability of unique consumables, special tools, private software, patented mechanisms or irreplaceable spare parts.

### Families rather than one-off details

Where custom work is justified, it should be developed as a small number of repeatable component and interface families rather than a large collection of unrelated details.

Likely families include:

- removable skirting and low-level service distribution;
- access doors and wall panels;
- floor-access cassettes and their supporting frames;
- structural and service penetrations;
- window and door mounting interfaces;
- movement and slip joints;
- ventilation and inspection grilles;
- fixing rails and heavy-load attachment points;
- service labels, diagrams and inspection covers.

A family may vary in size or architectural profile while retaining the same fixing logic, tolerances, access method and replacement sequence.

The design should seek repetition especially in concealed technical work. Bespoke visible craftsmanship may legitimately vary more than the hidden mechanism that supports it.

### Remanufacturability

A custom component should not be considered maintainable merely because it is removable. It should also be possible to repair or reproduce it after the original fabricator, architect or supplier no longer exists.

Important custom assemblies should therefore be defined by sufficient durable information to allow a competent future workshop to reproduce them. As appropriate, this should include:

- overall dimensions and critical tolerances;
- material and finish specifications;
- load and performance requirements;
- standard fastener sizes and fixing positions;
- interface dimensions;
- disassembly sequence;
- replaceable wear parts;
- fabrication drawings or equivalent records.

Where a component can be made from common sheet, plate, timber, stone, extrusions or machined sections using ordinary fabrication processes, this is preferable to a unique mechanism dependent on a single supplier.

### Interface control

The most important custom design work occurs at interfaces between disciplines. Those interfaces should have explicit ownership rather than being left to whichever trade arrives last on site.

For important junctions, the technical design should define, as applicable:

- which component is permanent and which is replaceable;
- who is responsible for the interface;
- structural loads and allowable movement;
- moisture and drainage requirements;
- airtightness and vapour-control continuity;
- fire and acoustic requirements;
- pest barriers;
- electrical separation or bonding;
- tolerances and adjustment;
- fixing method;
- installation sequence;
- access and tool clearances;
- inspection and replacement sequence.

The architect or lead designer should retain responsibility for the coherence of the whole platform. Specialist engineers and fabricators should design the parts within their competence, but no interface should become an uncoordinated gap between appointments.

### Prototyping and first articles

Repeated or high-consequence custom details should be tested at full scale before they are multiplied through the building.

Appropriate candidates include:

- removable floor cassettes;
- service skirting;
- door-surround electrical routes;
- wall-to-ceiling movement joints;
- window and door mounting interfaces;
- wet-room service walls;
- unusual access panels or grilles.

A prototype should be assessed not only for appearance but also for installation tolerance, stiffness, rattling, wear, water behaviour where relevant, acoustic behaviour, tool access and the time required to remove and reinstall it.

Where practical, the people who will maintain the assembly should participate in this review. A detail that appears elegant on a drawing but is awkward to open with normal tools has not met the doctrine.

For major repeated assemblies, the first production installation should be treated as a first article and reviewed before the remainder are fabricated or installed.

### Design responsibility and specialist input

The house requires an architect or lead designer who understands the platform as a complete system and remains responsible for architectural and technical coordination.

Detailed engineering should be undertaken by the appropriate disciplines. This will normally include structural and building-services engineers and may, where justified, include specialists in acoustics, building physics, fire, drainage, roof access, waterproofing, joinery and metalwork.

Fabricators and specialist contractors should be involved early where they possess manufacturing knowledge that materially affects the design. Shop drawings and specialist design should develop the architect's requirements rather than silently redefine the underlying interface.

An architectural technologist or technically focused architect is likely to be important during detailed design because the success of the project depends heavily on repeatable construction details, tolerances and coordination.

### Procurement and preservation of design intent

The procurement strategy should preserve responsibility for the interfaces and principles established by the doctrine.

Cost control and buildability review are necessary, but substitutions should be evaluated against the underlying performance requirement rather than accepted merely because they reduce initial construction effort. A change that replaces an accessible mechanical joint with adhesive, removes an inspection route, buries a service or creates dependence on a proprietary component is not equivalent merely because it performs the same immediate function.

The architect or lead designer should remain involved through technical design, specialist coordination, mock-ups and construction of critical details. Early trade involvement is desirable for unusual repeated assemblies.

The precise contractual procurement route is a project-specific decision, but responsibility for the architectural platform should not be transferred so diffusely that no party remains accountable for its coherence.

### Complexity control

Every unique detail creates future documentation, fabrication and maintenance obligations. Bespoke work should therefore have a clear reason to exist.

A proposed custom element should be challenged with the following questions:

- What problem does the custom element solve that a standard solution cannot?
- Does it improve long-term serviceability, durability, safety or architectural quality enough to justify its complexity?
- Can the same interface or component family be reused elsewhere?
- Does it contain a unique mechanism that could instead be replaced by simple geometry, gravity, standard fasteners or a commodity component?
- Can it be maintained or remade without the original supplier?
- Does its presence simplify the building over its life, or merely make initial construction more elaborate?

The objective is not maximum novelty. It is a small, coherent set of well-resolved building systems that can be understood, repeated, repaired and reproduced.

## Architectural character and materials

The technical doctrine is not intended to produce an industrial-looking house. The architecture should remain recognisably domestic, composed and beautiful while allowing its construction and service logic to remain legible.

### Architectural character

The intended architectural language is classical and period-informed, with Georgian architecture as the primary reference.

The house should not resemble a contemporary engineering demonstrator merely because its engineering is unusual. Technical features visible to occupants should be resolved as architectural elements and opportunities for craftsmanship.

Examples include:

- substantial removable timber skirting;
- brass or bronze edge details;
- cast-metal ventilation grilles;
- well-made access doors and panels;
- timber panelling concealing service walls;
- engraved brass diagrams and labels;
- carefully patterned removable floor panels;
- finely detailed movement joints and slip interfaces.

Technical infrastructure may remain openly technical inside plant rooms, risers and other dedicated service spaces. Within occupied rooms it should generally be concealed by high-quality, removable architectural elements rather than exposed or buried.

### Materials and ageing

Material selection should consider not only initial appearance and nominal service life but also the manner in which a material ages.

Preference should be given, where technically appropriate, to materials whose expected ageing mode is wear, patination, repair or local replacement rather than embrittlement, delamination, peeling or wholesale disposal.

This favours materials such as brick, stone, timber, lime-based finishes, slate, brass and bronze where they are appropriate to the function. It does not prohibit modern materials; it requires their ageing and replacement behaviour to be understood before they are incorporated into a long-lived assembly.

Visible wear can be acceptable or even desirable where it produces patina rather than failure. Hidden degradation that remains undetectable until surrounding fabric is damaged should be designed out wherever possible.

#### Timber and moisture

Timber may be used extensively where it can remain dry, inspectable and maintainable. It should not be used as the exposed or concealed component of an assembly in which routine wetting, persistent dampness or recurring leakage is an expected operating condition.

Where timber is used near the external envelope, wet rooms or water services, the surrounding detail should keep water away from it by geometry, drainage and separation rather than relying solely on coatings or sealants. Timber should not be the sacrificial weathering layer of the building.

This principle does not preclude timber floors, stairs, joinery, panelling, roof structure or other dry construction. It requires those elements to be protected by the assemblies around them so that their normal service condition is dry.

## Movement, joints and designed interfaces

### Expected movement

A building is a collection of materials and assemblies that move differently throughout their lives.

Movement arises from, among other things:

- thermal expansion and contraction;
- changes in moisture content;
- timber shrinkage and seasonal movement;
- structural deflection;
- creep;
- settlement;
- vibration;
- wind loading;
- differing material properties;
- replacement of shorter-lived components.

The design should accommodate normal movement without visible cracking or damage to adjacent finishes. A crack at a wall-to-ceiling junction is usually evidence that a finish was required to bridge a moving interface, not that the building should somehow have remained perfectly still.

This document sometimes refers to allowing the building to "breathe" in the mechanical sense: normal movement should be allowed to occur harmlessly at designed interfaces. This is separate from moisture, airtightness and vapour-control design.

### Designed interfaces

Assemblies should not simply terminate against one another and then be disguised with plaster, filler or sealant.

A designed interface should perform whichever functions are required at that junction, which may include:

- allowing relative movement;
- controlling construction tolerances;
- transferring loads where appropriate;
- maintaining air or water seals;
- allowing inspection;
- allowing disassembly;
- protecting vulnerable edges;
- providing a replaceable wear component;
- expressing the junction architecturally.

The interface should be treated as a first-class building element rather than an afterthought.

### Brittle finishes

Brittle finishes should not bridge assemblies that are expected to move independently.

This applies especially to:

- wall-to-ceiling junctions;
- wall-to-floor junctions;
- masonry-to-timber junctions;
- masonry-to-steel junctions;
- structural frame-to-infill junctions;
- window and door reveals;
- built-in joinery against masonry;
- bathroom and wet-room interfaces;
- kitchen worktops and upstands;
- stair connections;
- roof-to-wall and roof-to-joinery interfaces;
- removable service panels and floor cassettes.

Wet plaster, rigid grout, brittle filler and decorative caulk should not be used as substitutes for movement detailing. Where a flexible sealant or gasket is genuinely necessary, it should be accessible and treated as a replaceable maintenance item rather than as permanent fabric.

### Slip interfaces

Where two assemblies meet but are expected to move differently, a slip interface should be considered in preference to a rigid cosmetic bridge.

A slip interface should typically:

- be positively fixed to one assembly;
- overlap or register against the second assembly without unnecessarily restraining it;
- conceal a deliberate movement allowance;
- keep the movement gap visually controlled;
- remain inspectable or replaceable where practical.

A wall-to-ceiling junction might work conceptually as follows:

```text
SECTION — WALL / CEILING JUNCTION

CEILING ASSEMBLY  ─────────────────────────────────────
                                      ⇆ relative movement
                           deliberate gap
                                  │
                         ┌────────▼────────┐
                         │ overlap / slip  │  ← covers ceiling edge
                         │ trim            │
                         └────────┬────────┘
                                  │ fixed to wall side only
WALL / CORNICE   █████████████████┴██████████████████

The trim conceals the gap while leaving wall and ceiling free to move independently.
```

The visible element should not be rigidly fixed across both moving assemblies. Its purpose is to cover and articulate movement rather than prevent it.

### Cornices as functional joints

The traditional cornice should be reconsidered as a functional wall-to-ceiling junction rather than as decoration alone.

A restrained classical moulding could incorporate a fine brass or bronze slip interface. The result should remain appropriate to a Georgian interior while allowing the ceiling and wall assemblies to move independently.

Timber or mechanically fixed moulding components should be considered where they provide advantages over monolithic decorative plaster, including:

- tolerance of movement;
- mechanical rather than wet fixing;
- removal without damage to surrounding finishes;
- local replacement;
- controlled interfaces with both ceiling and wall.

The detailing should be visually restrained. A narrow brass or bronze line can express the joint without making the technical mechanism visually dominant.

### Brass and bronze as interface materials

Brass and bronze should have a coherent functional role rather than being applied as arbitrary decoration.

They may be used selectively at:

- slip joints;
- wall-to-ceiling interfaces;
- removable floor-panel borders;
- thresholds;
- service-access panel edges;
- ventilation and inspection grilles;
- skirting transitions;
- removable joinery;
- inspection covers;
- engraved diagrams and labels.

Used consistently, these materials can provide a subtle visual cue that a junction has been designed for movement, access, disassembly or maintenance. Not every technical joint requires visible metal; visible metal should correspond to a real construction purpose.

### Tolerances and adjustment

Construction should not depend on adjoining materials meeting at mathematically perfect lines that can only be maintained with filler or sealant.

Reveals, rebates, overlaps, shadow gaps, trims and slip pieces should absorb realistic construction tolerances and normal movement while preserving visual order.

Where possible, the ability to adjust or replace the interface should be preferred to requiring unrealistic precision from the permanent structure.

### Reversible assembly and disassembly

Access alone is not sufficient. Important shorter-lived components should have a clear removal sequence.

The design should consider, before construction:

- what must be removed before a component can be reached;
- whether those preceding components are themselves removable without damage;
- whether there is physical space to use the necessary tools;
- whether the component can be withdrawn from the building once disconnected;
- whether replacement requires wet trades or destructive finishing work.

Replaceable layers should generally favour mechanical fixing, gravity seating, clips, screws, bolts, removable beads and other reversible means of assembly where those methods are technically appropriate.

Adhesives, expanding foams and irreversible bonding should not be used merely because they are convenient during construction. They may still be appropriate where their function and future replacement consequences are understood.

### Replaceable interfaces

Durability does not require every component to last indefinitely. It requires wear and failure to be directed into parts that can be renewed without harming the parts around them.

Examples include:

- a replaceable gasket protecting a durable frame;
- a removable metal trim protecting a plaster edge;
- a service sleeve preventing repeated drilling through structure;
- a replaceable threshold insert protecting adjacent stone or timber;
- a removable floor border protecting the permanent supporting frame.

The replaceable element should normally be simpler and less costly to renew than the permanent assembly it protects.

## Moisture and environmental control

### General moisture strategy

Moisture should be treated as one of the principal physical threats to the long life of the building. The objective is not to keep every material permanently dry. Brick, stone, slate and other weathering materials may be intentionally exposed to water. The objective is to ensure that water only reaches materials and locations designed to receive it, cannot become trapped in vulnerable assemblies, and has a deliberate route to drain or dry.

Every important envelope, wet-room and water-service assembly should answer five questions before construction:

- how can moisture reach the assembly;
- what is the first means by which it is shed or excluded;
- where does water go if that first defence is bypassed;
- how can the assembly dry after incidental wetting or construction moisture;
- how will abnormal moisture become visible and accessible before permanent fabric is damaged.

The design should distinguish between materials that are intended to tolerate periodic wetting and dry again, and materials whose normal service condition should remain dry. Timber, insulation, interior finishes, electrical equipment and similar vulnerable components should not be placed where their durability depends on the permanent success of a short-lived seal or coating.

### Moisture mechanisms

Moisture control should consider the different mechanisms by which water enters and moves through a building rather than treating all dampness as the same problem. These include:

- bulk rainwater and wind-driven rain;
- plumbing leaks and appliance failures;
- surface water and groundwater;
- capillary movement through porous materials;
- water vapour moving through assemblies;
- humid air leaking into colder construction and condensing;
- condensation on cold internal surfaces and services;
- condensate intentionally produced by plant;
- construction moisture introduced by concrete, mortar, plaster, cleaning and exposure during the build.

Each mechanism should have an explicit control strategy appropriate to the assembly. A detail that prevents rainwater ingress may do nothing to prevent interstitial condensation, and a vapour-resistant layer may be harmful if it traps construction moisture or prevents an assembly from drying.

### Drainage and drying

Assemblies should not rely on perfect exclusion of water where a robust drainage and drying strategy can also be provided. Where incidental wetting is credible, the detail should provide a safe path out and should avoid forming moisture traps between impermeable layers.

The preferred sequence is:

1. reduce unnecessary wetting;
2. shed bulk water using geometry and gravity;
3. drain water that passes the first line of defence;
4. allow wetted materials and cavities to dry in a controlled direction;
5. keep vulnerable materials outside predictable wetting zones;
6. make abnormal moisture visible;
7. provide access to investigate and repair the source.

Drying capacity is part of durability. A wall, roof, floor or service void should not be assumed to remain perfectly dry throughout a century of use. It should be able to recover from limited wetting without trapping moisture against vulnerable materials.

### Airtightness and vapour control

Airtightness, vapour control and service distribution should be designed together. The primary airtight layer should be continuous, legible in drawings and physically protected from routine alteration. Where practical, service zones should sit on the room side of that layer so that adding or replacing sockets, pipes, controls or data cabling does not repeatedly perforate the environmental envelope.

Vapour-control layers should be selected and positioned for the actual wall, roof and floor build-up rather than applied as a generic rule. The design should avoid creating assemblies that can admit moisture but have no credible drying direction. Unusual or highly insulated build-ups should be checked for both surface and interstitial condensation risk, not merely for thermal transmittance.

Air leakage should be treated separately from vapour diffusion. Warm humid air moving through a small unintended opening can carry moisture into cold parts of an assembly even where the nominal material build-up appears satisfactory. Junctions, penetrations and interfaces therefore require the same care as the broad wall and roof areas.

### Ground moisture and the undercroft

The raised ground floor and service undercroft require an explicit moisture strategy. The undercroft must not become an accidental cool, stagnant space in which humid air condenses on structure or services. It should be clearly designed either as part of the conditioned envelope or as a deliberately ventilated external-type space, with materials and service insulation appropriate to that decision.

Ground-level design should include, as appropriate:

- capillary breaks between soil, foundations and walls;
- ground moisture barriers and drainage appropriate to site conditions;
- external ground levels and paving falling away from the building;
- protection against splashback at the plinth;
- clear separation between soil, planting and vulnerable wall zones;
- accessible drainage around any below-ground service trench;
- insulation of cold water and other services where condensation is credible;
- inspection access sufficient to identify dampness, standing water or biological growth.

Future landscaping should not be able to bridge damp-proof or drainage details simply by raising soil or paving against the building. The relevant external levels and drainage zones should therefore be physically legible and documented.

### Perimeter dry zone

The base of the external wall should have a deliberate, inspectable separation from ordinary soil, planting and paving. Rather than relying only on a nominal difference between ground level and damp-proofing, the implementation should investigate a continuous dry perimeter zone around the raised plinth.

This may take the form of a drained gravel or stone rill, shallow areaway, open drainage margin or a combination of these. The zone should:

- keep soil and planting away from the wall;
- reduce splashback onto the plinth;
- make the base of the building visible for inspection;
- make it physically difficult for later landscaping to bridge moisture-control details;
- drain positively and remain accessible for clearing sediment and debris;
- accommodate roof-water discharge and visible service tell-tales only where this can be done without creating local saturation or hidden connections;
- remain separated from the undercroft by properly detailed, vermin-resistant boundaries.

At entrances and terraces, the dry zone may be crossed by stone bridges, removable metal grilles or other architecturally deliberate elements rather than simply filled in. The detail should form part of the classical plinth and landscape composition rather than reading as a defensive trench added after the architecture is complete.

### Construction moisture

The building should not be closed up merely to maintain programme while wet construction remains trapped within assemblies. Concrete, mortar, plaster and other wet trades should be allowed to dry sufficiently before moisture-sensitive finishes, insulation layers or low-permeability coverings are installed where trapping is a credible risk.

Construction-stage moisture checks should form part of commissioning. Measurement at this stage is preferable to relying on permanent electronic monitoring later. The as-built record should note any assemblies whose drying depends on temporary ventilation, heating or sequencing.

### Primary weather protection

Sealants, adhesives and membranes should not be the primary defence against predictable water ingress or movement where geometry, gravity, overlap, drainage, flashings, cavities or mechanical detailing can perform that function.

Roofs, windows, thresholds, external penetrations and wet areas should be detailed so that water is naturally shed, drained or redirected before relying on a sealant. Sealants and membranes may remain necessary, but where possible they should provide secondary protection rather than being the single element whose failure causes damage to permanent fabric.

Examples of preferred first-line measures include:

- adequate falls;
- overlapping materials;
- drips and throatings;
- flashings;
- drained cavities;
- open drainage paths;
- capillary breaks;
- externally accessible rainwater goods;
- mechanically formed upstands and laps.

### Visible and harmless failure

Where practical, assemblies should be arranged so that failure becomes visible before concealed damage occurs.

Examples include:

- a water-service containment trough discharging to a visible external tell-tale;
- a roof overflow that discharges visibly rather than into a concealed wall;
- window drainage paths that remain open and inspectable;
- condensate routes that are traceable and accessible;
- removable panels that expose vulnerable connections without destructive investigation.

A fault should not be allowed to remain hidden merely because it can be concealed elegantly. The architectural objective is to conceal normal operation while preserving clear evidence of abnormal operation.

### Roof rainwater management

Rainwater management should be simple, visible and readily maintainable.

Gutterless overhanging eaves are preferred where the architecture, ground drainage and exposure genuinely support them.

Rain may discharge into:

- gravel margins;
- stone rills;
- planted drainage areas;
- soakaways;
- controlled courtyard or light-court water features where appropriate.

Where gutters or downpipes are required, they should be external, visible, reachable and easy to inspect and clean. Concealed gutters and inaccessible rainwater pipes should be strongly avoided unless there is a compelling architectural reason and a demonstrably maintainable access strategy.

A courtyard or light court may participate in rainwater management but should not act as the sole drainage path for large roof areas without independent overflow and failure provisions.

Roof drainage should be arranged so that blockage or overflow becomes visible and discharges harmlessly rather than driving water into concealed fabric.

Rainwater goods, chimney flashings, rooflights and other exposed details should be located and detailed with the roof-maintenance route in mind. A technically accessible gutter is not sufficiently maintainable if cleaning it still requires an improvised ladder position or unnecessary scaffolding that could have been designed out.

## Thermal comfort, climate resilience and indoor environmental quality

The environmental design should preserve comfort and health without making the house dependent on continuously operating complex machinery. Mechanical systems are expected to assist a well-designed building rather than compensate for avoidable architectural problems.

### Passive environmental control

The building form, orientation, openings, mass and external shading should be used to moderate environmental conditions before active heating or cooling is added.

The site-specific design should consider:

- solar gain by orientation and season;
- external shading and the depth of reveals;
- glazing area and solar properties;
- thermal mass and its actual ability to exchange heat with occupied rooms;
- insulation continuity and thermal bridges;
- useful cross-ventilation and stack ventilation;
- secure night-purge ventilation where appropriate;
- operable windows as a deliberate mode of use rather than an accidental backup;
- landscaping and external surfaces that influence local heat gain.

External shading should generally be preferred to attempting to remove unwanted solar heat after it has already entered the building. Where shutters, awnings or other movable shading are used, they should follow the wider principles of mechanical simplicity, repairability and architectural integration.

### Summer overheating

Summer comfort should be treated as a design condition rather than as a future appliance problem.

The implementation should model credible present and future summer conditions and should demonstrate how the house limits overheating through architectural measures before relying on active cooling. Particular attention should be paid to attic rooms, large areas of glazing, west-facing rooms and spaces with high internal gains.

Where mechanical cooling is justified, its distribution, condensate, maintenance access, acoustic effects and replacement path should follow the normal service doctrine. The building should not require concealed refrigerant lines or inaccessible condensate routes merely because cooling was considered late.

### Winter resilience and passive survivability

A failure of heating, electricity or ventilation equipment should normally cause a gradual reduction in comfort rather than an immediate threat to the building or occupants.

Envelope performance, thermal mass, compartmentalisation, controllable openings and other passive characteristics should provide a useful period of resilience during equipment failure or interruption of utilities. The design should consider credible hot-weather and cold-weather outage scenarios as part of the site-specific performance work.

This does not require the house to remain fully comfortable without services. It requires that failure modes be proportionate and that the permanent architecture contributes materially to survivability.

### Indoor air quality

Indoor environmental quality should address contaminants as well as temperature and humidity.

The preferred hierarchy is:

1. avoid or reduce pollutant sources where practical;
2. capture unavoidable contaminants close to their source;
3. provide deliberate background ventilation;
4. filter incoming air where site conditions justify it;
5. monitor or automate only as a supplementary layer where useful.

Material selection should consider emissions during realistic use, curing and ageing. The design should avoid introducing persistent indoor pollution merely because a mechanical ventilation system can dilute it.

Cooking, combustion, bathrooms, laundry and other strong local sources should use the source-capture strategies described elsewhere in the doctrine.

### Fresh-air intake and exhaust geography

Fresh-air intakes and exhaust terminals should be planned as part of the architecture and the site plan.

Intakes should be located to reduce the risk of drawing in:

- combustion exhaust;
- kitchen discharge;
- drainage odours;
- vehicle exhaust;
- dust and pollutants from busy roads or service areas;
- moisture or contamination from other building exhausts.

Exhausts should discharge where they will not immediately recirculate into windows, courtyards or fresh-air intakes or cause persistent staining and wetting of the facade.

Filters, screens and terminals should remain accessible for inspection, cleaning and replacement. Air quality should not depend upon a filter that can only be reached by dismantling permanent construction.

### Site gases and ground conditions

Radon, ground gases, contaminated ground and similar hazards are site-specific rather than universal. They should nevertheless be investigated early enough that any required barriers, ventilation or substructure measures can be integrated into the permanent platform rather than retrofitted around it.

The raised-floor and undercroft strategy must not be assumed automatically to resolve these issues. Ground-gas control, undercroft ventilation and the location of the conditioned envelope should be designed together for the actual site.

## Permanent fabric and architectural elements

This section describes the principal long-lived building elements and the interfaces by which shorter-lived components attach to them.

### Structural robustness and legibility

Because the primary structure is the longest-lived part of the platform, it should favour clear load paths, durable materials and details that remain understandable to future engineers.

The structural design should seek, where proportionate:

- simple and legible load paths;
- robust connections that do not depend on inaccessible short-lived components;
- protection of metalwork and reinforcement from credible corrosion environments;
- avoidance of trapped water at structural bearings and connections;
- inspection access to structurally important interfaces where deterioration is credible;
- deliberate allowances for foreseeable future loads where these can be provided economically;
- local robustness so that damage to one secondary component does not create disproportionate failure elsewhere;
- structural openings and service crossings designed before construction rather than cut later.

The structure should not be made unnecessarily elaborate in pursuit of theoretical flexibility. Long-term robustness generally favours understandable structural systems that a future engineer can survey, calculate and repair.

Foundation design is necessarily site-specific. Ground investigation, groundwater, nearby trees, shrinkable soils, made ground and other local conditions should be resolved before the permanent substructure is fixed. The doctrine requires that the resulting foundation strategy be durable and legible; it does not prescribe a universal foundation type.

Changes that affect structure should remain distinguishable from ordinary fit-out work. A future owner should be able to tell when an alteration requires structural design rather than assuming that every removable-looking element is non-structural.

### External wall concept

### Primary floors

Engineered timber joists are currently preferred, including open-web or similar engineered systems where appropriate.

Joists should connect to masonry through deliberately engineered interfaces such as masonry hangers rather than routinely embedding vulnerable timber deep inside masonry.

No later service drilling should be assumed. Required crossings should be incorporated into the structural and service design before construction.

### Stairs and circulation

Stairs should receive more design attention than is typical in domestic construction. They are used constantly, form major architectural elements and are a significant source of household injury.

The staircase should therefore combine generous geometry with architectural quality.

The implementation should investigate:

- deeper-than-minimum treads, comfortably accommodating large feet;
- moderate and highly consistent risers;
- generous landings;
- graspable handrails;
- lighting that makes tread edges clear;
- deliberate changes of direction;
- avoidance of winders where a proper landing can reasonably be provided;
- adequate stair width for daily use and for moving furniture and equipment.

On a compact plot, this will consume valuable floor area. That area should be treated as useful circulation and safety infrastructure rather than as wasted space.

### Roof volume, attic accommodation and maintenance access

A conventional domestic loft should not be created by default. Roof volume is valuable space and should either be usefully occupied, deliberately allocated to accessible services, or left substantially empty of components that require maintenance. A dark residual void reached only through a small ceiling hatch should not become an accidental technical storey.

Where the roof geometry permits, the preferred direction is to bring the principal attic volume into ordinary use with a normal stair, appropriate headroom and a deliberate environmental strategy. This may take the form of an attic storey, rooms within the roof, storage rooms or a combination of occupied and technical space. On a compact plot this also recovers useful floor area without enlarging the footprint.

Residual eaves spaces may remain, but they should be treated as designed cavities rather than inaccessible leftovers. Where access is required they should use proper doors or large removable panels, include safe local flooring or working platforms, and avoid containing equipment that cannot be removed through the available opening.

The roof build-up should be coordinated with the moisture doctrine. A warm-roof or otherwise conditioned-roof strategy should be investigated where it simplifies moisture control and service access, but the final build-up must be justified by hygrothermal analysis rather than selected by rule of thumb. Any junction between roof structure and brittle interior finishes should also accommodate predictable roof deflection and seasonal movement through designed interfaces rather than relying on plaster to remain crack-free.

The roof itself should be designed to be maintained safely. The implementation should provide, where appropriate:

- a proper roof-access hatch from an attic landing or technical area, preferably on a rear or secondary roof slope rather than the principal street elevation;
- a stable and adequately sized landing or standing area immediately inside and outside the hatch;
- engineered permanent restraint or anchor points located for real maintenance tasks rather than added after construction;
- predetermined locations for temporary edge protection, scaffold ties or other safe-access equipment where required;
- deliberate maintenance routes to chimneys, rooflights, gutters, rainwater outlets, solar equipment and other roof-mounted components;
- roof-mounted equipment positioned with access and replacement in mind rather than wherever spare roof area remains.

Permanent anchors are part of the access strategy, not a substitute for appropriate edge protection or other work-at-height measures. The objective is that routine roof maintenance has an intended method and route from the day the house is designed.

### Windows

Windows should be treated as replaceable assemblies installed within permanent openings. The project should not depend on a novel or bespoke window technology where a high-quality conventional system can provide the required performance. Design effort should concentrate on the interface between the window and the permanent building.

Thermally broken aluminium is the current preferred material for external window frames. It is compatible with the requirement that timber should not be routinely exposed to weather or wetting, while allowing slender profiles appropriate to a classical facade. Other frame materials may be considered where they meet the same durability, repairability and architectural requirements.

The masonry opening should provide a durable and accurately formed mounting zone. The implementation should investigate a mechanically fixed subframe or equivalent interface so that the window is attached to a stable building interface rather than being assimilated into the reveal by foam, plaster and perimeter sealant. Any subframe must preserve thermal continuity, drainage and the integrity of the cavity and air-control layers.

The connection between frame and building must accommodate differential movement. Plaster or other brittle finishes should not bridge directly from masonry to the window frame. A designed perimeter movement zone should be concealed by an overlapping, removable reveal or trim fixed to one assembly only. Brass or bronze may form part of the visible architectural detail where appropriate, but dissimilar metals should be isolated where necessary to avoid corrosion.

Water management at the opening should rely primarily on geometry and drainage. The building-side detail should include, as appropriate:

- a sill with sufficient projection, fall and drip;
- cavity and reveal details that direct incidental water outward;
- durable sill trays or flashings beneath vulnerable interfaces;
- explicit external drainage and weep paths;
- capillary breaks and overlapping weathering details;
- perimeter sealants as maintainable secondary protection rather than the sole defence against water ingress.

The window manufacturer may provide the weathering of the window unit itself; the architecture must provide a safe path for water that reaches the interface around it. Water should not be able to disappear unnoticed into permanent masonry, insulation or timber.

Window systems should favour:

- independently replaceable glazing units;
- replaceable gaskets and weather seals;
- replaceable hinges, locks, handles and other ironmongery;
- mechanical frame fixings that remain accessible after removal of designated trim;
- commodity or widely supported components where practical;
- opening elements that can be serviced safely from inside where practical;
- complete future removal of the frame without destructive rebuilding of the structural opening or surrounding finish.

The surrounding masonry and internal finishes should not have to be damaged because a glazing unit, gasket, hinge or whole frame reaches the end of its useful life.

### Doors and thresholds

Doors should follow the same separation between permanent opening and replaceable assembly, but their interface should be designed around repeated mechanical use, adjustment and wear. The structural opening should remain permanent; the door set, frame, seals, ironmongery and threshold should be removable or replaceable without rebuilding the opening.

The implementation should investigate a mechanically fixed mounting frame, subframe or equivalent fixing zone within the masonry opening. Removable architraves or adjacent joinery should conceal and protect the fixing interface while allowing access to it. These elements may also carry the deliberate vertical electrical routes described elsewhere in this document. Plaster should not be relied upon to lock a door frame into the wall or to bridge a junction that may move.

The door assembly should allow practical adjustment of hinges, catches and alignment over time. Commodity ironmongery and mechanically replaceable wear components should be preferred over proprietary mechanisms where they provide equivalent performance.

Internal thresholds should normally be avoided where a continuous level floor is practical. Where different floor assemblies meet beneath a doorway, the junction should be designed explicitly for movement and replacement, potentially using a narrow brass or bronze interface rather than grout, filler or a brittle edge-to-edge joint.

External thresholds should be treated as weathering assemblies rather than decorative strips. They should combine outward falls, drainage, durable sill or tray detailing, capillary breaks, movement allowance and replaceable wear surfaces so that failure of a sealant is not the event that allows water into permanent fabric. Any accessible drainage channel or perimeter dry zone outside the door should remain cleanable and should not provide a pest route into the building.

## Human adaptability and spatial permanence

A house intended to last for generations should be able to accommodate changes in its occupants without forcing major reconstruction of the permanent fabric.

The objective is not institutional accessibility everywhere or a permanently generic plan. It is to remove avoidable barriers and to reserve sensible adaptation paths before they become expensive emergencies.

### Lifetime adaptability

The site-specific design should investigate, where reasonably achievable:

- at least one step-free or readily adaptable entrance route;
- generous principal door openings and circulation widths;
- a ground-floor WC that can be adapted for reduced mobility and, where the plan permits, a credible future shower arrangement;
- future fixing provision for handrails, grab rails and similar support without opening permanent walls;
- controls, isolation points and frequently used fittings positioned for practical reach;
- landings and turning spaces that remain useful for children, injuries, luggage and reduced mobility as well as ordinary daily use;
- a route by which a principal living space could temporarily or permanently function as a bedroom if required.

These provisions should be integrated into ordinary architecture rather than presented as visible medical adaptations before they are needed.

### Vertical circulation and future lift provision

A lift need not be installed merely because the house may one day require one. However, the cost of reserving a plausible future route can be much lower than reconstructing floors and rooms later.

The implementation should therefore investigate whether vertically stacked cupboards, storage areas, service-adjacent spaces or another deliberately reserved zone could accommodate a future domestic lift or lifting platform without cutting primary structure or destroying principal rooms.

Any reserved route must remain useful in the initial house and must not compromise the clarity of the plan merely to satisfy a hypothetical future technology. Structural loads, pit or head requirements and service routes should be considered only far enough to ensure that the reserved geometry is credible rather than fictional.

### Spatial permanence

Adaptability should respect the distinction between enduring architecture and changeable layers.

Principal rooms, staircases, major openings, structural walls, facade composition and important axes should normally be designed as long-lived spatial commitments. Secondary partitions, joinery, services and fit-out may be more readily altered where doing so extends the useful life of the house.

A room should not be made shapeless merely because its future use is unknown. Well-proportioned rooms with good light, useful dimensions and sensible connections can support many uses without requiring literal physical reconfiguration.

The preferred form of adaptability is therefore often **capacity and compatibility**, not continuous movement: spare routes, adequate dimensions, planned fixing points and future connection possibilities allow change while the architecture itself remains composed and stable.

## Acoustic design and vibration control

Acoustic performance should be designed into the plan, structure and service architecture rather than added later as a surface treatment. The objective is not to make every room acoustically dead or silent. It is to provide predictable privacy, quiet where it matters, controlled transmission between rooms, and freedom from avoidable mechanical noise and vibration.

The design should distinguish between:

- **airborne sound**, including speech, music and television;
- **impact sound**, including footsteps, moving furniture and dropped objects;
- **structure-borne sound and vibration**, including pumps, fans, pipework, doors and appliances;
- **room acoustics**, including reverberation and speech clarity within a room;
- **external noise**, including traffic, aircraft, railways, neighbours, rain and external plant.

These mechanisms require different responses and should not be treated as one generic problem.

### Acoustic planning and room adjacency

Acoustic control should begin with the arrangement of rooms.

Where the plan allows, bedrooms, studies and other quiet rooms should be separated from plant rooms, utility rooms, kitchens, stairs, bathrooms and other recurrent noise sources. Corridors, wardrobes, service walls, bathrooms and storage can act as useful acoustic buffers.

This should be coordinated with maintenance geography rather than treated as a competing requirement. A compact house may use the service edge or spine to concentrate plant and high-service rooms while allowing quieter rooms to occupy more protected parts of the plan.

External noise should also influence the site-specific plan. Bedroom orientation, window placement, courtyards, landscaping and the location of ventilation openings should respond to actual noise sources rather than assuming that the facade experiences uniform conditions.

### Separation by mass, decoupling and absorption

Airborne and impact separation should normally be achieved through a combination of:

- sufficient mass;
- physical separation or resilient decoupling between layers;
- acoustic absorption within cavities;
- control of gaps and penetrations;
- avoidance of rigid bridges that bypass the intended separation.

Heavy masonry is valuable for airborne sound isolation, but mass alone is not sufficient where sound can flank through floors, ceilings, ducts, doors or service voids.

Where two assemblies are deliberately acoustically decoupled, later fixings and services should not inadvertently reconnect them. Acoustic decoupling should be treated as another designed interface and should remain legible in the construction details.

### Floors and impact sound

Inter-storey floors should be designed specifically for both impact and airborne sound. The preferred direction is a layered assembly in which the walking surface, structural floor and ceiling do not form one rigid vibrating system.

A typical strategy may combine:

```text
SECTION — ROOM ABOVE TO ROOM BELOW

ROOM ABOVE
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ removable finished floor cassette           │
├~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~┤  resilient bearing
│ structural deck / engineered joist zone     │
│    ░░ acoustic absorption within void ░░     │
├ - - - resilient / decoupled supports - - - ┤
│ ceiling lining                              │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────┘
ROOM BELOW

Impact and vibration paths are interrupted at the resilient interfaces.
```

The exact build-up is an implementation matter, particularly because the floor system must remain compatible with removable access panels and service zones.

Removable floor cassettes should seat securely without rattling and should not create hard bridges across resilient layers. Access requirements must not be allowed to destroy the acoustic separation of the floor.

### Plant and structure-borne vibration

Mechanical equipment should not be rigidly coupled to the building where its normal operation would transmit vibration into occupied rooms.

Pumps, fans, ventilation plant, heat-pump equipment and similar machinery should use appropriate vibration-isolating mounts, flexible connections and support arrangements. The plant room itself should use sufficient mass and separation to prevent ordinary equipment noise becoming a feature of adjacent rooms.

Equipment selection should favour low-noise operation at normal duty rather than relying on enclosure alone to suppress inherently noisy plant. Larger, slower-running equipment may be preferable to smaller high-speed equipment where this improves acoustic performance without compromising other priorities.

The supporting structure should be checked for structure-borne transmission paths. An isolated machine mounted to a rigid pipe or duct can still transmit vibration around its isolators.

### Plumbing and drainage noise

Water and drainage systems should be designed so that ordinary operation does not transmit unnecessary noise through the structure.

The implementation should consider:

- resilient pipe clips and supports where appropriate;
- sleeves or interfaces that prevent rigid contact at structural penetrations;
- acoustic treatment of soil and waste pipes near quiet rooms;
- avoidance of soil stacks on bedroom walls where the plan allows;
- water velocities and pressure control that reduce water hammer and flow noise;
- accessible measures for arresting or correcting water hammer;
- isolation of pumps and other active equipment from primary structure.

Acoustic treatment must remain compatible with inspection and replacement. A quiet pipe that becomes permanently entombed is not an acceptable solution.

### Service routes and flanking transmission

No service route or service void should unintentionally bypass the acoustic separation between rooms.

At room or acoustic-zone boundaries, open service voids should terminate at a barrier. Individual services may pass through using designed penetrations, sleeves or attenuated interfaces, but the surrounding air volume should not continue freely from one room to the next.

This principle should be coordinated with fire, smoke, dust and pest compartmentation so that one well-designed boundary performs several functions.

Cable trays, ducts, pipes, removable skirting routes and corridor service decks should be detailed so that they do not create continuous acoustic paths through otherwise separated rooms.

### Ventilation and acoustic cross-talk

Ventilation should provide required airflow without creating an acoustic connection between rooms.

The design should avoid direct grille-to-grille paths where privacy matters. Depending on the system, this may require:

- acoustically attenuated transfer paths;
- branch attenuators;
- sufficient duct length or directional change;
- low air velocities;
- appropriately sized ducts and terminals;
- resilient fan mounting;
- flexible connections where required;
- separation of noisy plant from quiet rooms.

Door undercuts may be acceptable where privacy requirements are modest, but they should not be used automatically as the transfer-air solution for bedrooms, bathrooms or other rooms where a large open acoustic path would defeat the intended separation.

### Doors and acoustic boundaries

A high-performing wall should not be undermined by a lightweight or poorly sealed door.

Where acoustic privacy matters, door assemblies should use sufficient mass and should control perimeter gaps. Solid-core doors, well-adjusted latches, replaceable perimeter seals and, where justified, drop seals or equivalent threshold details should be considered.

These details should remain compatible with the wider door doctrine: the frame, seals, ironmongery and threshold remain adjustable, inspectable and replaceable rather than being permanently assimilated into the wall.

### Room acoustics and architectural absorption

Room acoustics should be resolved through architecture and furnishing rather than through conspicuous studio-style treatment unless a particular room genuinely requires it.

The classical architectural language provides many useful means of controlling reverberation, including:

- curtains and other textile window treatments;
- rugs and carpets where appropriate;
- upholstered furniture;
- bookshelves and irregular surfaces;
- timber panelling;
- coffered or articulated ceilings;
- fabric-backed or acoustically absorptive panels integrated into joinery where required.

The desired acoustic character should vary by room. A drawing room, dining room, study and stair hall need not have the same reverberation characteristics. The objective is controlled and intentional acoustics rather than maximum absorption everywhere.

### Verification

Acoustic performance should be verified at both design and completion stages where it materially affects the quality of the house.

The implementation document should establish appropriate targets for:

- bedroom and study privacy;
- airborne separation between important rooms;
- impact transmission between storeys;
- noise from plant and ventilation;
- plumbing and drainage noise;
- external noise intrusion;
- room reverberation where relevant.

Where acoustic separation is important, completed assemblies should be tested or otherwise commissioned rather than relying solely on nominal product ratings. Particular attention should be paid to flanking paths and service penetrations, because these can dominate the performance of an otherwise strong wall or floor.

## Fire, life safety and security

Life safety requirements override the convenience or visual purity of the platform. They should nevertheless be integrated into the architecture from the outset so that safety does not depend on improvised additions or hidden assumptions.

### Whole-house fire strategy

The house should have an explicit fire strategy addressing, as applicable:

- means of escape from each occupied level;
- protection of principal escape routes;
- smoke movement and compartmentation;
- detection and alarm coverage;
- fire resistance of structural and separating elements;
- service penetrations through fire-resisting construction;
- combustion appliances and chimneys;
- plant rooms, electrical equipment and future battery systems;
- roof and attic-storey escape;
- external fire spread and boundaries;
- access and operational considerations for the fire and rescue service.

The exact provisions are implementation and regulatory matters, but the strategy should be established before the plan, risers and service voids are frozen.

### Escape should remain simple

A person unfamiliar with the technical systems of the house should be able to leave safely without operating software, finding a special tool or understanding the service architecture.

Security measures, smart locks, shutters and access controls must not create hidden dependencies that obstruct emergency escape. Where powered security devices are used, appropriate mechanical release or fail-safe behaviour should be incorporated.

The addition of an attic storey, future lift, security gate or service compartment should be tested against the escape strategy rather than treated as an independent design problem.

### Fire compartmentation and future alterations

Fire-resisting boundaries should be designed as durable, inspectable elements of the platform.

Services may cross them only through deliberate penetrations whose fire, smoke, acoustic and pest functions are coordinated. Future additions to trays, ducts or pipework should not require improvised holes through fire barriers.

Where practical, fire-stopping at maintainable service boundaries should remain visible or inspectable after completion. The building record should identify the location and intended performance of important fire barriers so that later trades do not unknowingly compromise them.

### Detection, suppression and isolation

Fire and smoke detection should use widely supported equipment and should remain testable and replaceable. Alarm functionality should not depend solely on internet access, a cloud account or a proprietary home-automation controller.

Where automatic suppression, local extinguishing systems or specialist protection is justified by the eventual design, it should be treated as a maintainable replaceable system rather than hidden permanent fabric.

Emergency electrical, fuel and other service isolation should be obvious and reachable without entering the immediate hazard zone where practical.

### Physical security

Security should rely first on good physical construction and clear territorial design rather than on electronics alone.

External doors, windows, roof hatches, undercroft entrances, utility access points and gates should use robust but replaceable hardware and should not create easy bypass routes into the occupied house. Locks, cylinders, hinges, bolts and other wear components should favour established formats that can be replaced without rebuilding the opening.

The service architecture must not create a security weakness. An accessible undercroft, plant room, side passage or roof-maintenance route should have deliberate boundaries between maintenance access and occupied space.

Electronic alarms, cameras, access control and smart locks may supplement physical security but should not become the only means of secure operation or ordinary entry.

Security and escape must be designed together; making entry difficult must not make emergency exit obscure or unreliable.

## Service architecture

Building services should form a deliberately planned three-dimensional network with clear hierarchy, spare capacity and working access. The service architecture is part of the original architectural and structural design rather than a later routing exercise.

### Spatial organisation of maintenance

Maintainability should influence the plan and section of the house, not only the detailing of individual components. Spaces and routes that require regular inspection, repair or replacement should be geographically organised so that maintenance can occur through a small number of predictable technical routes.

The implementation should therefore seek to align, where the plan allows:

- the utility and plant entry;
- the plant room;
- the principal service riser;
- service-heavy kitchens, bathrooms and utility spaces;
- horizontal service spines;
- undercroft and service-trench access;
- attic and roof-maintenance access;
- external service routes and maintenance entrances.

This does not require a second hidden circulation system in a compact house. Ordinary corridors, stairs and joinery may perform both domestic and maintenance functions if technical access remains deliberate, clean and unobtrusive. The objective is to prevent every room from becoming a potential work site. Where practical, formal reception rooms and other low-service spaces should sit away from the principal maintenance routes.

A side or rear service entrance and a coherent service edge or spine should be investigated in the site-specific plan. A tradesperson should be able to understand how to move from utility entry to plant room, riser, wet rooms, undercroft and roof without searching the building or carrying out destructive investigation.

### Plant room

The plant room is the central service hub and is distinct from the main vertical riser.

It should contain major equipment and distribution points such as:

- water manifolds;
- electrical distribution;
- heating equipment;
- pumps;
- controls;
- ventilation equipment;
- communications infrastructure;
- gas isolation and distribution where required;
- future major equipment.

It should provide:

- generous working space;
- good lighting;
- drainage where appropriate;
- clear labels;
- accessible isolation;
- space to place tools and temporary equipment;
- sufficient clearance to remove and replace major plant.

Replacement logistics should be checked during design. A component is not genuinely serviceable if it can be reached but cannot be removed from the building.

### Main service riser

The main riser is the vertical service trunk connecting floors and should ideally be directly adjacent to the plant room.

Horizontal services should use purpose-designed service spaces rather than occupying primary structural zones.

Corridors are attractive distribution routes because they can form service spines between the central riser and individual rooms.

### Deep inter-storey zones

The building should allow generous inter-storey depth where this materially improves serviceability.

A deeper floor zone may be simpler and more robust than forcing services through a conventional shallow structure. This can be combined with local variation in ceiling height.

For example, an upstairs bathroom may sit above a corridor, WC, wardrobe, joinery zone or another service-heavy room. The service zone beneath the bathroom can then deepen locally without compromising the principal ceiling height of a reception room.

The preferred approach is likely to combine:

- a generally generous inter-storey zone; and
- local deeper service zones above secondary spaces where required.

Variation in ceiling height should be treated as an architectural composition issue rather than automatically as a defect.

### High-service rooms

Bathrooms, kitchens and utility rooms should be treated differently from low-service rooms such as bedrooms and reception rooms.

Where practical they should cluster around the main service core, and wet rooms on different floors should stack or nearly stack.

Each high-service room should use an accessible service wall where practical.

A failed fitting should not require destruction of tile, stone, plaster or structural fabric.

The house should be investigated as a slightly elevated building sitting on a substantial masonry or stone plinth.

Rather than burying ground-floor services beneath a slab, an accessible undercroft should be considered beneath the occupied floor.

The undercroft should be designed as usable service infrastructure. Routine maintenance should not require crawling. Principal service routes should permit crouched movement with tools, with standing-height working areas where the task, equipment or frequency of access justifies them.

### Cleaning and routine care

Cleaning is a recurring form of maintenance and should be designed rather than assumed.

Any component whose normal function causes it to collect grease, dust, lint, sediment, hair, leaves, scale or other contamination should have a deliberate cleaning method. Typical examples include:

- kitchen extract filters and ducts;
- ventilation filters, grilles and terminals;
- shower channels and traps;
- floor drains;
- rainwater outlets and gutters;
- perimeter drainage channels and sediment traps;
- window drainage paths and weeps;
- undercroft inspection areas;
- external air intakes and insect screens;
- appliance strainers and accessible condensate traps.

The design should avoid narrow inaccessible pockets that predictably accumulate dirt or water but cannot be reached without dismantling permanent finishes.

Removable grilles, covers and strainers should use simple robust attachment methods and should tolerate repeated removal. If an element is expected to be cleaned frequently, its access method should be faster and simpler than that of an element inspected once a decade.

Cleaning should not require unsafe access where a safer architectural arrangement could reasonably have been provided. The roof-maintenance strategy, for example, should account for gutter and outlet cleaning as well as major repair.

### Controlled utility entry

Incoming electricity, water, gas and communications should enter through a deliberately designed utility intake zone rather than through unrelated penetrations made independently by different trades or utility providers.

The intake zone should provide:

- predefined penetrations;
- replaceable sleeves;
- spare penetrations;
- sealing and vermin protection;
- clear internal distribution paths;
- access for future replacement.

### Spare capacity

Major service routes should contain deliberate spare capacity.

Provide where sensible:

- empty conduits;
- spare sleeves;
- blanked openings;
- spare tray width;
- future manifold positions;
- draw wires;
- spare space in plant areas;
- physical room for additional equipment.

The permanent structure should be as agnostic as practical about technologies that do not yet exist.

## Service compartmentation and pest defence

### Service compartments

The house should not contain a single continuous service void connecting large areas of the building.

Service routes should be divided into controlled compartments. Individual pipes, cables and ducts may pass through compartment boundaries using designed penetrations, but the surrounding open void should not continue uninterrupted.

```text
SERVICE CELL A        COMPARTMENT BARRIER        SERVICE CELL B
┌─────────────┐       ███████████████████       ┌─────────────┐
│ power ──────┼──────[ sealed penetration ]─────┤ power       │
│ data  ──────┼──────[ sealed penetration ]─────┤ data        │
│ water ──────┼──────[ sleeve / collar    ]─────┤ water       │
│ OPEN VOID   │       ███████████████████       │ OPEN VOID   │
└─────────────┘                 ↑                 └─────────────┘
                         open void stops here
```

This arrangement should contribute to:

- fire resistance;
- smoke control;
- acoustic separation;
- dust control;
- odour control;
- pest control.

Where a compartment boundary also forms an acoustic boundary, service penetrations should be detailed so that pipes, ducts, trays and sleeves do not create an unintended flanking path or rigid bridge. Acoustic, fire and pest requirements should be resolved together rather than by independent layers that obstruct one another.

Fire stopping and compartment boundaries must themselves remain inspectable where practical, particularly at service penetrations that may later be modified. The whole-house fire strategy defined elsewhere in this doctrine governs the required performance of these boundaries; this section governs how service architecture preserves that performance.

### Rodent defence

The service architecture must not create an internal route network for rodents.

No service void should provide:

- an uninterrupted route through the house;
- a route from outside into internal service spaces;
- a route from foul drainage into service spaces;
- a route between external cavities and occupied areas.

Rodent barriers should rely on durable materials such as masonry, metal, stainless steel mesh and tightly fitted metal collars. Primary defence should not depend on expanding foam, plastic mesh, soft insulation or other easily damaged or chewable materials.

Ventilation openings should use durable vermin-resistant screening. Utility entries, undercroft boundaries, drainage penetrations and external tell-tales should receive particular attention.

Foul drainage must remain a sealed system within the service environment. Rodent resistance at sewer connections, traps and abandoned branches should be addressed explicitly in the drainage design.

Electrical routes within rodent-accessible service spaces should receive specific protection. Loose polymer-insulated cable should not form the primary exposed wiring method in such zones where a credible chewing risk exists; suitable metal containment, armouring or equivalent durable protection should be incorporated into the electrical design.

## Water, drainage and wet rooms

Water-bearing systems should be simple, accessible and tolerant of credible failure. Pressurised water, drainage, wet appliances and moisture-intensive rooms should be designed together so that leaks, blockages and routine humidity do not threaten permanent fabric.

### Water manifolds

Water distribution should favour central manifolds and clearly labelled individual isolation.

The exact pipe material remains open. Commodity repairability takes precedence over adopting a proprietary distribution system merely because it is technically sophisticated.

Accessible water routes should assume that leaks will eventually occur.

Visible metalwork may use brass or bronze where architecturally appropriate. Concealed containment should use the material best suited to long-term wet service, potentially stainless steel, copper or another durable material selected by the engineer.

### Leak tell-tales

Water-containment zones should preferably discharge by gravity to deliberately visible external tell-tales or another equally legible safe location. The preceding section diagram illustrates the relationship between the containment trough and a visible outlet.

Different service zones may have separate outlets so that the approximate source of a leak can be identified quickly.

Leak containment should not simply discharge invisibly into the foul drainage system. The containment system should protect the building while still making the fault evident.

### Drainage strategy

Drainage is less flexible than most services because gravity imposes gradients and soil pipes are physically large.

Wet rooms should therefore cluster around accessible soil stacks and use short, legible horizontal drainage routes.

Cleanouts and rodding points should be provided deliberately at useful locations, particularly around changes of direction and other likely blockage points.

### Upper-floor drainage

Where an upper-floor wet room requires significant drainage depth, the design should use one or both of the following approaches.

#### Generous inter-storey depth

Increase the overall structural and service zone between floors.

#### Local structural drop

Lower the structural floor beneath the wet room and use the resulting depth for drainage and other services. Where possible, locate the deeper zone over a room below that can accept a lower ceiling without compromising an important space.

The finished bathroom floor should ideally remain level with adjoining rooms.

### Failure-state maintenance

Service spaces should be designed around credible failure and maintenance scenarios rather than normal operation alone.

For a major leak or blockage, a plumber should be able to identify the route, isolate the affected service, reach the fault and use normal tools without first damaging the house to discover where the problem is.

For drainage this requires:

- known and documented routes;
- accessible joints where practical;
- obvious cleanouts;
- inspectable changes of direction;
- sufficient working room;
- deliberate transitions to buried external drainage.

The same test should be applied to electrical, ventilation, heating and communications systems.

### Transition to buried drainage

Some external foul drainage will inevitably need to enter the ground.

The objective is not to prohibit buried infrastructure outside the building. The objective is to keep the transition from accessible building drainage to buried external drainage to the smallest practical number of deliberate, inspectable points.

The main soil system should remain accessible through the undercroft for as long as practical before leaving the building through a designed penetration and inspection arrangement.

### Utility room as a wet service room

The utility room should be treated explicitly as a wet service room rather than as an ordinary room containing appliances. Washing machines, sinks, water-treatment equipment and similar components should be assumed capable of leaking significantly during the life of the building. Their failure should be contained locally rather than allowed to test the rest of the house.

The room should therefore provide, as appropriate:

- a continuous water-tolerant floor assembly with upstands or equivalent containment at vulnerable edges;
- a subtle fall toward an inspectable drainage or containment point;
- a drainage arrangement that remains sealed against sewer gases and pests and can itself be maintained;
- individually accessible isolation valves for water-connected appliances;
- visible and accessible appliance hoses and joints where practical;
- service access behind or beside appliances without destructive removal of cabinetry;
- durable lower wall finishes in areas exposed to splashing or accidental flooding;
- no vulnerable timber within the primary containment zone;
- enough ventilation to dry the room after ordinary laundry use or an abnormal wetting event.

A washing machine should be allowed to fail without causing structural damage. The same principle should apply to other water-connected appliances, although the scale of containment may differ.

### Bathrooms as moisture-intensive rooms

Bathrooms should be designed both as wet rooms and as rooms that generate large short-duration humidity loads. Waterproofing should not stop at the immediate shower enclosure if a wider room-level containment strategy can make failure safer. Floors should be capable of receiving accidental water and directing it toward an accessible drainage path rather than toward adjacent dry construction.

The bathroom should also be able to dry rapidly after use. This requires both warm, well-insulated internal surfaces and deliberate source extraction of humid air. Persistent condensation on windows, ceiling edges, external corners or cold fittings should be treated as a design defect rather than an unavoidable characteristic of bathrooms.

## Electrical and communications

Electrical and communications systems should be physically legible, granularly isolatable and replaceable without chasing permanent walls or floors. Distribution should use accessible architectural routes and should remain understandable without proprietary control systems.

### Skirting as horizontal infrastructure

Adding or replacing an outlet should require removal and reinstatement of joinery rather than cutting masonry or plaster.

### Door surrounds as vertical electrical routes

Where light switches, controls or wall-mounted electrical accessories require a vertical route, the design should favour removable architectural joinery rather than chased masonry. Door architraves, adjacent timber panels or other dry, mechanically fixed trim may form deliberate vertical service routes between the skirting or service floor and the accessory.

The joinery should remain visually appropriate to the classical interior and should be removable without damage to plaster or masonry. The electrical route behind it should remain physically segregated from other services as required and should not depend on adhesive-fixed trim for future access.

### Distribution, isolation and circuit identification

Electrical distribution should be organised as a legible hierarchy rather than as an accumulation of routes added room by room. The final topology should be designed with the service riser, service decks, skirting distribution and plant spaces from the outset.

Isolation should be sufficiently granular that a local fault, maintenance operation or appliance failure disables as little of the house as reasonably practical. Dedicated or clearly separated circuits should be considered for major plant, high-load appliances, wet-service rooms, external systems and other functions where independent isolation materially improves safety or maintainability. Granularity should remain purposeful rather than becoming unnecessary complexity.

The main electrical isolation arrangement should be conspicuous, permanently labelled and reachable without moving furniture, appliances or stored possessions. Major electrical zones and plant should have clear local isolation where appropriate. The implementation should consider credible fault and fire scenarios so that the whole building, or an affected zone, can be de-energised quickly without first locating an obscure consumer unit or entering a hazardous space.

Circuits should use durable, consistent identifiers. The same identifier should appear, as appropriate, at:

- the distribution board;
- accessible branch or junction points;
- both ends of principal cable routes;
- local isolators;
- service diagrams and as-built records.

Identification should survive redecoration and should not depend solely on handwritten labels or digital records.

### Protection in service voids

Electrical cables in undercrofts, utility-entry zones, service trenches and other locations with credible rodent exposure should not be left as vulnerable loose wiring. The design should use metal conduit, enclosed metal trunking, armoured cable or another suitably durable chew-resistant arrangement where the risk justifies it.

Cable routes through compartment boundaries should use close-fitting, inspectable penetrations rather than oversized holes filled with foam. The protection strategy should remain compatible with fire stopping, ventilation, inspection and future cable replacement.

### External electrical systems

External electrical systems should be treated as a separate service domain rather than as extensions improvised from internal circuits. Garden power, gates, lighting, outbuildings, pumps and vehicle charging should use deliberately planned external routes, ducts and distribution points.

Where justified by the scale of the installation, a separate external distribution board or clearly segregated external circuit group should allow outside systems to be isolated without unnecessarily affecting the house. External routes should leave the building through designed service penetrations rather than future ad-hoc drilling through the envelope.

### Earthing and bonding

Earthing and bonding should be designed as permanent building infrastructure from the beginning rather than added around completed architecture. Earthing conductors, bonding routes, test points and connections that require inspection should remain identifiable and accessible.

The structural and service design should reserve deliberate routes for earthing and, where required by the final risk assessment and electrical design, lightning-protection conductors. These systems should be coordinated with the envelope, foundations, utility intake, external electrical systems, solar generation, battery storage and vehicle charging so that later additions do not require improvised routes through permanent fabric.

## Ventilation, gas and combustion

Air movement, combustion and fuel systems can interact with one another and should therefore be designed as a coordinated set of systems rather than as independent appliances.

### Ventilation, humidity and condensate

Ventilation should follow the same principles as other services: deliberate routes, adequate access, replaceable plant, measurable performance and visible maintenance points. The design should remove moisture and contaminants close to where they are produced rather than relying on weak whole-room dilution after they have already spread through the house.

#### Kitchen source extraction

Cooking extraction should be treated as a source-capture system. The primary kitchen extract should discharge outside rather than depend on recirculation as the normal means of removing heat, water vapour, grease and cooking contaminants.

The implementation should favour:

- a hood or canopy with enough depth and geometry to capture the rising plume at the hob;
- removable, readily washable grease filters;
- smooth rigid ductwork with short routes and few abrupt bends;
- accessible inspection and cleaning points;
- a remote or otherwise acoustically separated fan where this provides quieter operation and easier replacement;
- an external termination that is inspectable and does not stain or saturate vulnerable facade areas;
- a deliberate replacement-air strategy so high extract rates do not create excessive depressurisation.

The visible hood may be integrated into classical joinery, a chimney-piece form or another architectural enclosure, but the extract path behind it should remain mechanically simple and serviceable. Kitchen extraction must be coordinated with whole-house ventilation, airtightness and any fireplace or combustion appliance.

Bathrooms, showers and laundry spaces should likewise use source extraction. In bathrooms the extract terminal should normally be high level and close to the shower or bath, where the moisture plume is generated. A sloping ceiling, shallow coffer or other architectural geometry may help bring humid air toward a high-level extract point, but ceiling shape alone should not be relied upon to control steam. Airflow must be deliberately created.

The visible bathroom terminal should not need to contain the fan itself. A preferred arrangement is a quiet architectural extract grille or linear slot connected by a short, smooth, accessible duct to a remote fan or central ventilation unit located in a service zone. This allows the visible terminal to be resolved as part of the ceiling or cornice architecture while the mechanical equipment remains accessible for replacement. Brass, bronze or another durable metal may be appropriate for the visible grille where it suits the room.

Good extraction also requires a deliberate replacement-air path. Air should be able to enter the bathroom from the adjoining dry zone through a controlled transfer path, such as an appropriately detailed door undercut or transfer grille, while moist air is extracted at high level. Openable windows are desirable as supplementary purge ventilation where the architecture permits, but should not be the sole means of humidity control.

Ductwork serving moisture-producing rooms should favour:

- short routes where practical;
- smooth rigid ducts rather than unnecessarily long flexible ducting;
- minimal sharp bends and restrictions;
- accessible terminals and cleaning points;
- acoustic treatment that does not compromise airflow;
- insulation where cold duct surfaces could condense moisture;
- a deliberate route for any condensate that may form.

Ventilation performance should be commissioned by measuring actual airflow at the terminal. A fan catalogue rating is not evidence that the installed room is ventilating effectively. Automatic humidity sensing or timed boost controls may be useful, but basic ventilation must remain understandable and operable without cloud services, network connectivity or proprietary automation.

Large air ducts are likely to be better suited to accessible ceiling or dedicated service zones than to floor service decks. Whether the final system uses central mechanical extract, heat-recovery ventilation or another arrangement is an implementation decision; the doctrine requires accessible plant, source capture, maintainable routes and verifiable airflow.

Condensate from ventilation, cooling, heat pumps or other plant should use deliberate, traceable drainage routes. It should not be allowed to disappear into inaccessible construction.

### Gas

Gas should be accommodated if required, despite the preference for simplified service systems.

It should have a dedicated, clearly segregated and accessible route. The system should be conventionally constructed, clearly isolated and independently removable.

The house should be designed so that a future owner can remove the gas subsystem without modifying permanent building fabric or unrelated services.

### Fireplaces, chimneys and flues

A fireplace may form part of the permanent architecture, but the combustion technology should remain replaceable. Where a real fireplace or stove is included, the preferred arrangement is a durable masonry chimney breast, hearth and flue route containing replaceable or inspectable liners and a replaceable fire appliance or grate.

The system should provide:

- access for sweeping and inspection without damage to finishes;
- replaceable flue liners or other renewal strategy appropriate to the selected system;
- access to the chimney head through the planned roof-maintenance route;
- deliberate combustion-air provision where required;
- clear separation from combustible materials;
- a drainage and weathering strategy at the chimney head and roof penetration that follows the wider moisture doctrine.

Fireplace combustion air, whole-house ventilation and powerful kitchen extraction must be designed together. An extract system should not be able to create unsafe pressure conditions at a chimney or other combustion appliance. The exact appliance and any legal restrictions on fuel use are implementation matters; the permanent chimney and fireplace should not assume that one particular appliance will remain in use for the life of the house.

## Replaceable fit-out and occupation

Fit-out should be treated as a shorter-lived layer installed within the permanent building. Everyday occupation should not require repeated damage to structural or finished masonry.

### Removable floor systems

Accessible floors should feel permanent and substantial under normal use.

The floor pattern itself can form the access system. Possible upper-floor construction includes timber-faced structural cassettes seated within precision frames.

- timber;
- timber-faced structural cassettes;
- stone-faced panels;
- actual stone slabs in suitable ground-floor locations.

Brass or bronze framing may be used where it improves edge durability and expresses the removable grid as part of the architecture.

### Designed wall-fixing infrastructure

Permanent masonry should not be treated as a blank surface that successive occupants repeatedly drill whenever a picture, mirror, shelf or cabinet needs support. Selected walls should provide deliberate fixing infrastructure suited to the expected use of the room.

The primary domestic solution should build on established architectural elements rather than introduce conspicuous technical rails. The implementation should investigate:

- structurally useful picture rails for pictures, mirrors and other suspended objects;
- concealed continuous fixing channels or rails behind removable mouldings, dado rails or panelling where greater capacity or lower fixing heights are useful;
- known engineered fixing points or plates for exceptional loads;
- secondary service or joinery walls to carry cabinetry and fittings where this avoids repeated drilling of permanent masonry.

A fixing rail should be mechanically anchored to the permanent structure during original construction and should accept repositionable mechanical fixings without requiring new holes in the masonry. The concept should not depend unnecessarily on a proprietary product; the important requirement is a durable, understandable and replaceable mechanical interface.

Fixing infrastructure should be documented physically and in the as-built record. It must remain coordinated with service routes, airtightness, moisture control and structural requirements. Where a heavy object cannot reasonably use the general fixing system, its intended structural fixing point should be designed and identified rather than improvised later.

### Bathrooms as replaceable fit-out

Bathrooms should be treated as high-service fit-out installed within the permanent building rather than as finishes permanently fused to it.

The service-wall strategy addresses access from behind. The occupied side should also be designed with replacement in mind.

The implementation should investigate:

- mechanically mounted stone or tile panels where suitable;
- removable bath surrounds;
- accessible shower traps and wastes;
- replaceable waterproof liners or trays;
- access to valves and concealed cisterns without damaging finishes;
- joints that permit differential movement rather than relying solely on sealant.

Conventional bonded tile systems may still be used where they provide the best result, but they should not be adopted automatically if they create large areas of short-lived finish that can only be replaced destructively.

### Kitchens as replaceable fit-out

Kitchens should be treated principally as furniture and equipment rather than as permanent construction.

Cabinetry, appliances, worktops and splashbacks should be removable without damage to primary walls or floors. Appliance isolation points should remain accessible without first removing the appliance they serve.

Standard appliance sizes, standard service connections and mechanically fixed cabinetry should be preferred where compatible with the architecture.

Water-connected kitchen appliances should have local failure containment appropriate to their risk. A dishwasher, plumbed refrigerator or similar appliance should not be able to leak unseen into permanent flooring, joinery or wall construction. Shallow removable trays, visible drainage paths or other simple passive arrangements should be preferred to inaccessible electronic leak-detection systems.

## Commissioning, documentation and future change

The building should remain understandable and operable over generations, including if digital records, manufacturers or control platforms disappear.

### Whole-building commissioning

Completion should establish a measured baseline for how the building actually performs rather than merely confirming that components have been installed.

The commissioning plan should be prepared during design and should identify which systems require measurement, functional testing, witnessing or recorded inspection. Depending on the final design this may include:

- ventilation airflows and operating modes;
- airtightness and significant leakage locations;
- water pressure, flow and isolation;
- drainage falls, cleanouts and functional discharge tests;
- passive leak-containment paths and visible tell-tales;
- electrical protection, earthing, bonding and isolation;
- heating and cooling operation;
- condensate drainage;
- fire and smoke detection;
- operation of shutters, roof access and other safety-critical mechanisms;
- acoustic testing where performance matters;
- moisture condition before sensitive assemblies are closed;
- operation and accessibility of important service valves, panels and replacement routes.

Commissioning should include realistic functional tests where failure management is part of the design. A drain, overflow, containment tray or isolation arrangement should not be assumed to work merely because it is shown on a drawing.

Where systems depend materially on seasonal conditions, provision should be made for seasonal commissioning or early post-occupancy review.

### Building passport

The house should be handed over with a durable building record sufficient for a competent future owner, architect or trade to understand what was built and how it is intended to be maintained.

The record should include, as appropriate:

- as-built plans, sections and service routes;
- structural information and designated no-drill zones;
- the location and performance of fire and acoustic boundaries;
- valve, circuit, duct and service identifiers;
- photographs of important assemblies before they were closed;
- interface and fabrication drawings for repeatable custom components;
- material and finish specifications;
- commissioning results and baseline measurements;
- roof-access and maintenance information;
- inspection and maintenance intervals;
- replacement and disassembly sequences for important systems;
- records of spare conduits, sleeves and reserved future capacity.

Critical information should be stored in durable, widely readable formats. No essential building knowledge should exist only inside proprietary software, a cloud account or a vendor portal.

A concise physical copy of critical isolation, fire, roof-access and service information should remain in the building even if the full record is digital.

### Inspection regime

Long life depends on small defects being found before they become large failures.

The implementation should establish a proportionate inspection schedule for elements whose condition materially affects durability or safety. This may include roof coverings and flashings, rainwater goods, perimeter drainage, window seals and drainage paths, visible service containment, undercroft conditions, ventilation filters, fire-stopping at modified service routes, chimney components and other exposed wear items.

The purpose is not to create an onerous maintenance bureaucracy. It is to identify a relatively small number of high-consequence components whose neglect could defeat otherwise durable construction.

### Change control

Future alterations should leave the building at least as understandable as they found it.

When services are added, removed or rerouted, the relevant physical labels, drawings and building record should be updated. Abandoned pipes, cables and ducts should not simply accumulate indefinitely in service spaces without identification or removal.

Any alteration affecting fire, acoustic, moisture, structural or pest boundaries should reinstate the full performance of that boundary rather than merely closing the visible hole.

The building passport should therefore function as a continuing record of the platform rather than as a static handover document.

### Physical legibility and self-documentation

The building should remain understandable even if every external drawing, digital model and maintenance record is lost.

Use permanent identifiers for:

- valves;
- circuits;
- pipes;
- risers;
- service compartments;
- floor access panels;
- leak outlets;
- drainage routes;
- major isolation points.

Identifiers should match at both ends of a route.

Major service diagrams should be made as durable physical objects, for example engraved brass plates in the plant room.

```text
PHYSICAL SERVICE INDEX — EXAMPLE

WATER
  W01  kitchen cold       route: R1 → SD-E
  W02  kitchen hot        route: R1 → SD-E
  W03  ground-floor WC    route: R1 → SD-W
  W04  first-floor bath   route: R1 → SD-W

ROUTES
  R1    main service riser
  SD-E  east service spine
  SD-W  west service spine

ROUTE SUMMARY
  MAIN → R1 ─┬→ SD-E → W01 / W02
             └→ SD-W → W03 / W04

```

Physical documentation should complement the building passport, as-built drawings, digital models, commissioning records and maintenance records rather than replace them.

### Manual fallback

No essential household function should depend solely on software, network access or a remote service.

Smart controls may improve convenience and monitoring, but critical systems should retain understandable local operation and manual isolation where technically practical.

Examples include:

- physical water isolation valves;
- conventional electrical isolation;
- local heating controls;
- manual operation or override for important equipment;
- service information that does not require access to a proprietary application.

Electronics should enhance a fundamentally understandable building rather than make the building dependent on them.

## Design review tests

The following tests are deliberately phrased as failure or change scenarios. They should be used during design reviews, technical coordination, mock-ups and commissioning. A system that performs well in ordinary operation but fails a credible test below should be reconsidered.

### Serviceability, access and replacement

- **Leak.** Can the affected service be identified, isolated, exposed and repaired without destructive investigation? If the leak is not immediately noticed, does passive containment protect permanent fabric and eventually make the fault visible?
- **Blocked drain.** Can the route be identified and mechanically cleared from an appropriate access point without removing finished construction?
- **Failed cable.** Can it be replaced without opening plaster or drilling permanent structure?
- **Failed major plant.** Can the equipment be isolated, disconnected, physically removed from the building and replaced?
- **Replacement sequence.** Can a shorter-lived component be removed without damaging a longer-lived component around it? Are all prerequisite access panels, fasteners and clearances available?
- **Maintenance route.** Can a tradesperson move from the service entrance or utility intake to the plant room, riser, principal wet rooms, undercroft and roof-maintenance access using obvious routes and without turning formal rooms into work sites?
- **Roof maintenance.** Can gutters, chimney heads, rooflights and roof-mounted equipment be reached using the designed hatch, working positions, anchors and edge-protection strategy rather than an improvised access method? Can major roof-mounted equipment be removed when it reaches the end of its life?
- **Wall fixing.** Can ordinary pictures, mirrors, shelving and anticipated heavy objects be attached using designed fixing infrastructure without repeated drilling of permanent masonry or accidental conflict with services?
- **Door replacement.** Can a door frame, ironmongery and threshold be adjusted or replaced without cutting plaster, damaging masonry or destroying adjacent flooring?
- **Cleaning access.** Can every component expected to collect grease, lint, hair, leaves, sediment, scale or dust be reached, cleaned and reinstated with ordinary tools and without damaging permanent finishes?

### Movement, moisture and environmental failure

- **Differential movement.** If adjoining assemblies move relative to one another by a few millimetres, is that movement accommodated harmlessly at a designed interface rather than expressed as a crack?
- **Sealant failure.** If a sealant, gasket or membrane reaches the end of its service life, does the assembly remain protected long enough for the failure to be detected and repaired, or does water immediately enter permanent fabric?
- **Envelope wetting.** If the first weather seal, flashing or rainwater component fails, where does the water go next? Can it drain or dry without reaching vulnerable permanent fabric?
- **Drying.** If an assembly becomes wet during construction or service, what is its credible drying path? Has any combination of membranes, coatings or finishes trapped moisture between low-permeability layers?
- **Bathroom humidity.** After a long hot shower, is humid air captured close to its source, is replacement air available, and has actual extract airflow been measured? Are any surfaces likely to remain chronically cold enough for repeated condensation?
- **Appliance flood.** If a washing machine hose ruptures, a dishwasher leaks or another water-connected appliance fails at maximum credible flow, where does the water go? Can the affected appliance be isolated quickly, and can the resulting water be contained and drained without damage to permanent fabric?
- **Undercroft humidity.** Under humid summer conditions and during cold-weather operation, can the undercroft or service trench accumulate condensation on structure, pipes or ducts? Is its ventilation or environmental strategy explicit rather than accidental?
- **Ground interface.** Could later paving, planting or soil accumulation bridge the moisture-control detail at the base of the building without the problem becoming visually obvious? Can the perimeter drainage zone be inspected and cleaned?
- **Heatwave.** During a prolonged hot spell, what prevents the house from accumulating heat? Are external shading, glazing, thermal mass, purge ventilation and any mechanical cooling strategy coordinated rather than treated independently?
- **Service outage.** If power, heating or mechanical ventilation is unavailable for an extended period, does the house fail gradually and safely? Can occupants obtain useful ventilation and basic habitability without operating a complex control system?
- **Indoor air quality.** Are strong pollutant sources removed or captured at source? Are fresh-air intakes separated from exhausts and credible external pollutants, and can filters and terminals be maintained without destructive access?

### Services, boundaries and life safety

- **New technology.** Is there an obvious route from the utility or plant area to the part of the house that needs it, with enough spare capacity to make installation reasonable?
- **Rodent entry.** If a rodent reaches an external opening, utility penetration, undercroft or drainage interface, is there any continuous route into the house or between service compartments?
- **Kitchen extraction and combustion interaction.** Does the kitchen extract capture cooking emissions effectively at source, remain cleanable and replaceable, and receive sufficient replacement air? If a fireplace or other combustion appliance is present, have pressure interactions been explicitly resolved?
- **Fire and escape.** If a fire begins in the kitchen, plant room, utility room, attic or another credible origin, are occupants given clear warning and an understandable escape route? Do service routes or later alterations provide a path for smoke and fire around intended barriers?
- **Security and emergency egress.** Can external openings, undercroft access, roof access and service entrances resist ordinary forced entry without relying solely on electronics? Can occupants still leave rapidly during an emergency if power, networking or access-control systems have failed?
- **Structural legibility.** Can a future engineer identify the principal load paths, structural elements and critical connections? Are foreseeable service crossings and future loads accommodated without encouraging later drilling or cutting of primary structure?

### Acoustics

- **Bedroom privacy.** With normal speech, television or bathroom use in adjacent spaces, does the bedroom achieve the intended privacy without depending on occupants behaving unusually quietly? Do doors, transfer-air paths and service voids preserve the intended separation?
- **Impact sound.** When someone walks normally, moves a chair or drops an ordinary object on an upper floor, does the floor assembly control impact transmission without rattling or short-circuiting through removable access components?
- **Plant vibration.** When pumps, fans, heat-pump equipment or other plant operate at normal and high duty, is vibration isolated from the primary structure and from adjacent occupied rooms? Do rigid pipes, ducts or supports bypass the intended isolation?
- **Service cross-talk.** Can speech or mechanical noise travel between rooms through ventilation ducts, service decks, risers, skirting routes or penetrations? Do acoustic barriers remain continuous where service compartments cross room boundaries?
- **External noise.** Have actual or credible external noise sources been considered in room orientation, facade openings and ventilation design, particularly for bedrooms and studies?

### Adaptability and design governance

- **Future occupant.** Can the house accommodate temporary injury, reduced mobility or an older occupant without destructive alteration of primary structure? Is there a credible route to a step-free entrance, adaptable ground-floor sanitary provision and, if justified by the plan, future vertical assistance?
- **Spatial permanence.** Is an element genuinely likely to benefit from future movement or replacement, or has modularity been introduced without purpose? Are the principal rooms, stairs, openings and architectural order allowed to remain confidently permanent?
- **Bespoke dependency.** If the original architect, manufacturer and fabricator no longer exist, can an important custom component still be understood, repaired or reproduced using the building records, common materials and normal fabrication methods?
- **Detail proliferation.** Is a proposed new custom detail genuinely necessary, or can an existing component or interface family be reused? Does the new detail reduce lifetime complexity enough to justify creating another unique assembly?
- **Prototype and maintenance trial.** For a repeated custom assembly, has a full-scale prototype or first article been installed, removed, inspected and reinstated using realistic tools and tolerances before committing to widespread use?
- **Interface ownership.** For every important junction between structure, envelope, services and finish, is one party explicitly responsible for coordinating the complete interface rather than each discipline assuming that another will resolve it?

### Commissioning and records

- **Commissioning completeness.** Has the completed building been tested as an integrated system rather than as a collection of installed products? Are actual measured baseline results recorded for systems whose performance materially affects safety, durability, comfort or health?
- **Record continuity.** If ownership changes repeatedly over a century, does the building record remain readable without proprietary software? Can later alterations be recorded so that labels, drawings and physical reality remain consistent?
- **Lost documentation.** Can a competent tradesperson understand the principal system from the physical building, labels and diagrams alone?

The tests are not a substitute for calculations, statutory approvals or specialist commissioning; they are a way of checking whether the design remains coherent under stress, maintenance and change.

## Doctrine register

The following principles are a compact register of the doctrine rather than a second specification. They should be read with the detailed sections above; where a summary entry is ambiguous, the detailed section governs:

1. Longevity and serviceability are inseparable design objectives.
2. Beauty is a primary design requirement, not a finishing exercise applied after technical design.
3. The permanent building acts as a long-lived architectural platform for replaceable systems rather than being materially entangled with them.
4. Services are not embedded in permanent fabric.
5. Required structural crossings are designed before construction and use deliberate sleeves or openings.
6. Structural, service, moisture and interface design proceed together from the beginning.
7. Commodity repairability is preferred over proprietary sophistication.
8. The building is expected to move throughout its life and is detailed accordingly.
9. Brittle finishes do not bridge assemblies expected to move independently.
10. Independently moving or replaceable assemblies meet through designed interfaces.
11. Construction tolerances and movement are absorbed by interfaces rather than by filler and cracking.
12. Shorter-lived components are not trapped behind longer-lived components without a replacement strategy.
13. Replaceable layers favour reversible mechanical assembly where technically appropriate.
14. Sealants, adhesives and membranes are not relied upon as the sole primary defence against predictable water ingress or movement where geometric and mechanical detailing can perform that function.
15. Water should only reach materials and locations intentionally designed to receive it, drain it or tolerate temporary wetting.
16. Assemblies exposed to credible wetting provide both a drainage path and a credible drying path.
17. Airtightness and vapour control are designed as continuous building systems and protected from routine service alterations.
18. Predictable failures should become visible and accessible before they damage permanent fabric.
19. Service routes are accessible but generally concealed within removable architectural elements.
20. Plant rooms, risers, undercrofts and service routes provide real working space.
21. Wet services assume that leaks will eventually occur and include passive containment.
22. Water-connected appliances are arranged so that credible leakage can be isolated and contained locally.
23. Moisture-producing rooms use deliberate source extraction and are designed to dry after use.
24. Critical protection does not depend on software, networking or proprietary electronics.
25. Service voids are compartmentalised for fire, acoustic, cleanliness and pest control.
26. Rodent defence is designed as part of the building fabric rather than improvised after construction.
27. The building physically documents its own systems.
28. Maintenance design considers credible failure states, not only normal operation.
29. Materials are selected with regard to how they age, behave when realistically wet, can be repaired and can eventually be replaced.
30. Essential household functions retain understandable local or manual operation where technically practical.
31. Construction moisture is managed deliberately and moisture-sensitive assemblies are not closed before they are sufficiently dry.
32. Electrical systems are zoned, clearly identifiable and capable of granular isolation without destructive access.
33. Earthing, bonding and principal electrical routes are integrated into the original structural and service design rather than added retrospectively.
34. External electrical systems use planned, segregated routes and can be isolated without unnecessary dependence on the internal house circuits.
35. Maintenance is given an explicit geography: plant, risers, wet rooms, undercroft, roof access and external service routes are organised into understandable paths rather than scattered arbitrarily through the plan.
36. Roof spaces and roof-mounted systems are designed for safe inspection, maintenance and eventual replacement rather than treated as inaccessible residual volume.
37. Permanent walls provide designed fixing strategies for ordinary occupation so that routine hanging and mounting do not require repeated ad-hoc drilling of masonry.
38. Door and window openings are permanent interfaces; frames, ironmongery, thresholds and other shorter-lived components remain mechanically replaceable without destructive rebuilding.
39. The base of the building maintains a deliberate, inspectable and drained separation from soil, planting and paving.
40. Kitchen extraction removes cooking moisture and contaminants at source through a maintainable external route and is coordinated with replacement air and any combustion appliances.
41. Chimneys and fireplaces, where provided, separate long-lived masonry architecture from replaceable liners and combustion appliances and remain accessible for inspection and sweeping.
42. Acoustic privacy and mechanical quiet are planned through room adjacency, structure and service routing rather than treated as decorative afterthoughts.
43. Acoustic separation uses mass, decoupling, absorption and controlled penetrations; service routes and later fixings do not create unintended flanking paths or rigid acoustic bridges.
44. Impact sound and structure-borne vibration are controlled at their source through resilient interfaces, isolated plant and deliberately layered floor and ceiling assemblies.
45. Ventilation and transfer-air routes preserve acoustic privacy and do not create direct room-to-room sound paths where privacy matters.
46. Acoustic measures remain inspectable, maintainable and compatible with the building's wider principles of access, replacement and commodity repairability.
47. Standard and widely supported components are preferred wherever they can satisfy the doctrine; bespoke design is concentrated where it materially improves the long-term building.
48. Custom work is developed as a small number of repeatable component and interface families rather than as unrelated one-off details.
49. A removable custom component is not sufficient by itself: important bespoke assemblies should also be reproducible from durable records using common materials, standard fasteners and ordinary fabrication methods where practical.
50. Repeated or high-consequence custom assemblies are prototyped or treated as first articles before widespread fabrication, with maintenance and disassembly tested as well as appearance.
51. Important interfaces have explicit design ownership, and the procurement process preserves a lead designer responsible for the coherence of the architectural platform.
52. The house is designed for functional as well as physical longevity: likely changes in occupants, climate and technology are accommodated without destructive alteration of the permanent architecture.
53. Modularity is concentrated in layers that benefit from change; principal rooms, structural order and defining architectural relationships may remain deliberately permanent.
54. Fire and life safety are established as a whole-house strategy before service routes, risers, attic accommodation and security systems are fixed.
55. Fire-resisting and smoke-control boundaries remain identifiable and are not silently degraded by later service alterations.
56. Security relies first on robust physical construction and replaceable standard hardware, while emergency escape remains simple and independent of software or network availability.
57. Thermal comfort is addressed first through orientation, envelope, shading, mass and controllable openings before dependence on active cooling or other mechanical correction.
58. The building is designed for proportionate passive survivability during credible heat, cold and utility-outage conditions.
59. Indoor air quality follows a hierarchy of source reduction, source capture, deliberate ventilation and maintainable filtration where required.
60. The primary structure favours clear load paths, durable connections and enough legibility that future engineers can inspect, understand and repair it.
61. Routine cleaning is treated as maintenance: components that predictably collect contaminants have deliberate, safe and repeatable cleaning access.
62. The completed house is commissioned as an integrated building and begins life with recorded baseline performance rather than unverified design assumptions.
63. A durable building passport records the as-built platform, commissioning results, critical interfaces, maintenance information and later alterations in widely readable formats.

## Current preferred implementations

The following remain subject to site-specific architectural and engineering design. They are grouped by system for readability; the grouping does not change their provisional status.

### Design system and fabrication

- a deliberately limited catalogue of repeatable interface families rather than unrelated bespoke details;
- commodity service components installed within custom architectural enclosures or mounting systems where needed;
- common fasteners, ordinary fabrication processes and documented dimensions used in custom assemblies wherever practical;
- full-scale prototypes or first articles for the most repeated or highest-risk custom details before broad fabrication;
- fabrication and interface drawings retained as part of the permanent building record.

### Architecture and permanent fabric

- Georgian or closely related classical architectural language;
- restrained brass or bronze used as a functional visual language for interfaces, access and disassembly;
- mechanically articulated wall-to-ceiling joints, potentially using brass or bronze slip lines integrated into small classical cornices;
- approximately 215 mm dense masonry inner external-wall leaf;
- engineered timber primary floor structure;
- substantial inter-storey service depth;
- raised ground floor on a substantial plinth;
- a continuous drained and inspectable perimeter dry zone separating the plinth from soil, planting and ordinary paving;
- useful attic accommodation or first-class roof-space access rather than a conventional inaccessible loft, with a rear or secondary roof hatch and designed roof-maintenance anchors and access positions;
- thermally broken aluminium windows, or equivalent durable systems, installed as replaceable assemblies within designed permanent openings;
- mechanically mounted door frames and replaceable threshold interfaces concealed by removable architectural trim;
- structural picture rails and selected concealed fixing rails or known fixing points in rooms where this materially reduces future masonry drilling;
- removable timber or stone floor cassettes.

### Service architecture and maintenance

- one principal plant room;
- one walk-in primary service riser;
- a coherent maintenance geography linking utility entry, plant, riser, wet rooms, undercroft and roof access, preferably biased toward a side or rear service edge where the plan allows;
- corridor-based horizontal service distribution;
- local structural drops beneath upper-floor wet rooms where useful;
- accessible undercroft with a deeper service trench beneath principal wet and service areas;
- high-service rooms using accessible service walls;
- engraved brass or bronze physical system diagrams and labels.
- deliberate cleaning access for components that collect grease, dust, lint, sediment, leaves, hair or scale.

### Adaptability, environmental resilience and life safety

- a step-free or readily adaptable secondary entrance route where the site permits;
- ground-floor sanitary provision arranged so that reduced-mobility use and a future shower can be accommodated without rebuilding primary structure where practical;
- investigation of a credible future lift route using stacked cupboards, storage or another initially useful zone rather than installing a lift by default;
- external solar control, useful thermal mass, operable windows and secure purge ventilation integrated with the classical architecture;
- fire and escape strategy coordinated early with the stair, attic accommodation, service riser, plant spaces and any future lift provision;
- robust physical security at service entrances, undercroft and roof-access points without reliance on cloud-connected access control;
- a project commissioning plan and building passport prepared as part of technical design rather than assembled after completion.

### Acoustics

- quiet rooms separated from plant, utility, stairs and other recurrent noise sources by plan arrangement or buffer spaces where practical;
- heavy masonry used strategically where it materially improves privacy, particularly around plant and other noisy service spaces;
- inter-storey floors developed as layered acoustic assemblies with resilient support, cavity absorption and decoupled ceiling construction while preserving service access;
- removable floor cassettes seated on stable resilient interfaces so that access panels do not rattle or bypass impact isolation;
- mechanical plant and pumps mounted through vibration-isolating interfaces with flexible service connections where required;
- plumbing and soil stacks resiliently supported and acoustically treated where they pass near quiet rooms;
- service-compartment boundaries also used as acoustic barriers, with controlled service penetrations rather than continuous open voids;
- low-velocity ventilation distribution with acoustic attenuation and no direct grille-to-grille paths between rooms requiring privacy;
- solid-core or otherwise acoustically substantial doors in rooms where privacy matters, with replaceable perimeter sealing where justified;
- acoustic absorption integrated into ordinary architecture and furnishing rather than added as visually intrusive treatment.

### Water, moisture and ventilation

- manifold-based water distribution;
- gravity-drained secondary water containment;
- visible external leak tell-tales;
- utility room designed as a genuine wet service room with passive floor-level containment and maintainable drainage;
- bathrooms treated as moisture-intensive wet rooms with room-level water containment and high-level source extraction;
- remote or central bathroom extract plant located in an accessible service zone, with quiet architectural ceiling or cornice terminals and measured commissioning airflow;
- local passive containment beneath water-connected kitchen appliances;
- primary airtight layer protected from routine service alterations by the service-zone strategy;
- externally ducted kitchen source extraction with a cleanable rigid duct route and accessible or remote fan;
- gutterless eaves where site and architecture genuinely support them;
- otherwise fully external and readily maintainable rainwater infrastructure.

### Electrical and communications

- skirting-based electrical and data distribution;
- removable door surrounds or adjacent joinery used as vertical electrical routes for switches and controls where appropriate;
- granular electrical circuit zoning with durable circuit identifiers and conspicuous main and local isolation;
- rodent-resistant electrical containment in undercroft, utility-entry and other vulnerable service zones;
- deliberately segregated external electrical distribution and ducts for garden, gates, outbuildings and vehicle charging;
- earthing and bonding routes designed into the building from the outset, with accessible inspection and test points.

### Gas and combustion

- dedicated gas route;
- a permanent masonry fireplace and chimney where desired, with replaceable liner and fire appliance and deliberate combustion-air provision.
