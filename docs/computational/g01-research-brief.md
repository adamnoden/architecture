# G-01 — Georgian-Derived Long-Life House Grammar Research Brief

**Status:** corpus and derivation plan v0.1  
**Purpose:** define the scope, evidence base, annotation method and falsification process for deriving the first actual architectural grammar.  
**Important:** G-01 does not yet exist as a rule set. This document prevents it being invented from taste, memory or a handful of favourite precedents.

## 1. Why G-01 exists

The Reference House already has an architectural temperament:

- English Georgian proportions and hierarchy;
- restrained classical order rather than archaeological reproduction;
- red-brick domestic construction;
- strong room character and settled spatial order;
- a courtyard with a slight Andalusian influence;
- Long-Life House tectonic requirements expressed through real architectural elements.

That makes it the best first vehicle for testing architectural grammar.

The first grammar should therefore be provisionally scoped as:

> **G-01 — a Georgian-derived domestic architectural language for contemporary low-rise English houses, informed by English Palladianism and broader Georgian practice, modified where necessary by contemporary habitation and the Long-Life House doctrine.**

This is a project grammar, not a claim to define all Georgian architecture.

## 2. What G-01 is not

G-01 is not:

- a reconstruction of eighteenth-century life;
- a preservation standard;
- a universal theory of classical beauty;
- a Palladio emulator;
- a catalogue of mouldings;
- a “neo-Georgian style pack”;
- a rule that every house must be symmetrical;
- a historical claim that Georgian architecture was homogeneous.

The Georgian period itself contains Baroque, Palladian, neo-Classical, Gothic and Regency currents.

The grammar must therefore state its lineage honestly.

Its initial centre of gravity is **English Georgian domestic classicism with a strong Palladian inheritance**, not the entire 1714–1830 period.

## 3. Proposed first scope

To keep the language coherent, the first derivation should focus on:

### Primary building type

- domestic;
- low-rise;
- freestanding or substantially freestanding;
- compact country / suburban villa scale rather than palace scale;
- one principal architectural body;
- contemporary family occupation.

### Primary architectural questions

- massing;
- plan topology;
- room hierarchy;
- circulation;
- axes;
- bays;
- room proportions;
- floor-to-floor / ceiling hierarchy;
- façade composition;
- openings;
- plan-section-elevation coordination;
- principal architectural elements.

### Secondary questions

These should be recorded but should not control the first grammar extraction:

- detailed ornament;
- furniture;
- colour;
- decorative plaster;
- landscape grammar;
- outbuildings;
- estate planning.

### Excluded from first derivation

- urban terrace generation as a primary type;
- palaces / very large country houses;
- explicitly Regency picturesque composition;
- Georgian Gothic;
- full archaeology of classical orders;
- literal reconstruction of historic servicing arrangements.

Town houses and grand houses may still enter the corpus as **evidence for specific relationships**.

## 4. Why the corpus must be stratified

A random sample of “Georgian houses” would be almost useless.

The evidence should be divided by role.

### Corpus A — normative/theoretical sources

What architects and pattern-book authors said should be done.

Initial sources:

- Andrea Palladio, *Four Books on Architecture*;
- Colen Campbell, *Vitruvius Britannicus*;
- James Gibbs, *A Book of Architecture*;
- James Gibbs, *Rules for Drawing the Several Parts of Architecture*;
- Batty Langley, builder-facing architectural guides;
- Isaac Ware's Palladio translation and architectural writings;
- William Chambers, *Treatise on Civil Architecture*;
- Robert Morris's architectural writings/pattern books where relevant.

Purpose:

- explicit proportions;
- compositional rules;
- element families;
- construction of orders;
- intended transferability of design knowledge.

These texts are evidence of theory, not automatic evidence of built practice.

### Corpus B — canonical English Palladian / Georgian villas

Built works with unusually good documentary or measured-drawing evidence.

Strong initial candidates:

- **Marble Hill House**, Twickenham — identified by Historic England as an unusually instructive exemplar of the English Palladian school; measured plans exist in the Historic England archive;
- **Danson House**, Bexley — compact Georgian villa by Robert Taylor, with published English Heritage analysis explicitly covering proportion, structure and planning;
- **Chiswick House** — essential for the Palladian lineage and extensively surveyed, although function/scale make it a contextual rather than direct domestic template;
- other compact, well-documented villas to be selected after archive review.

Purpose:

- plan topology;
- hierarchy;
- symmetry/asymmetry;
- proportion;
- plan/elevation coupling;
- exceptions to theoretical rules.

### Corpus C — ordinary high-quality Georgian domestic buildings

The grammar must not be derived only from exceptional aristocratic works.

Include:

- modest detached houses;
- smaller villas;
- provincial Georgian houses;
- well-preserved examples with good survey data.

Purpose:

- identify which ordering principles survive without monumental scale;
- distinguish essential grammar from expensive flourish;
- identify builder-executable regularities.

Historic England listing records and conservation surveys can help identify candidates, but listing prose alone is not enough for metric extraction.

### Corpus D — Georgian town houses and terraces

Use selectively.

Sources such as:

- Survey of London;
- Historic England's Georgian townhouse material;
- measured drawings and documented terraces.

Purpose:

- storey hierarchy;
- sash/opening hierarchy;
- relationship between interior hierarchy and façade;
- circulation and stair positioning;
- compact planning;
- bay discipline.

Do not allow the constraints of a narrow urban plot to become universal rules for the freestanding grammar.

### Corpus E — later and contemporary Georgian reinterpretations

Potential examples:

- selected twentieth-century neo-Georgian houses;
- high-quality contemporary Georgian/classical houses;
- Georgian Group award-winning new work.

Purpose:

- discover which rules survive modern life, structure and building services;
- identify living adaptation rather than historical reconstruction;
- test whether the language can remain coherent under contemporary constraints.

These are **validation/adaptation evidence**, not the primary historical source.

### Corpus F — negative and mutation corpus

This is essential.

Include:

- weak neo-Georgian examples where evidence is available;
- deliberate mutations of strong precedents;
- generated near-misses.

Purpose:

- identify the boundary where coherence breaks;
- turn architectural failure into candidate compiler errors.

The most scientifically useful negative examples may be controlled mutations rather than subjective accusations about existing houses.

## 5. Evidence hierarchy

Not all source material deserves equal weight.

Prefer, in order:

1. original architectural drawings with reliable scale/dimensions;
2. measured surveys;
3. authoritative conservation drawings;
4. original pattern books/treatises;
5. scholarly reconstructed plans;
6. high-quality published plans;
7. listing descriptions and photographs;
8. photographs alone.

Do not infer exact proportional rules from perspective photographs if measured drawings exist.

Every extracted metric should retain source provenance.

## 7. Annotation unit

Every corpus building should be represented at several levels.

Do not reduce a precedent to a vector of dimensions.

### Building-level annotation

- source ID;
- date;
- architect/designer if known;
- location;
- typology;
- original use;
- corpus class;
- confidence in original state;
- major later alterations;
- drawing/source provenance.

### Massing annotation

- footprint type;
- number of storeys;
- basement/attic conditions;
- principal orientation;
- frontage hierarchy;
- roof family;
- portico/projection/recess structure.

### Spatial-topology annotation

For each space:

- semantic role;
- rank/hierarchy;
- direct connections;
- circulation depth from principal entrance;
- access to exterior/courtyard/garden;
- enfilade participation;
- service/public/private character;
- alternative paths.

Create a plan graph independently from geometry.

### Ordering annotation

- primary axes;
- secondary axes;
- centres;
- symmetry domains;
- bay lines;
- principal/secondary ranges;
- aligned walls;
- aligned openings;
- intentional misalignments.

Crucially, record **scope of symmetry**.

A façade may be symmetrical while the plan is not.

### Metric annotation

For rooms:

- clear width;
- clear length;
- height where reliable;
- area;
- aspect ratio;
- ratio family proximity;
- dimensional rank relative to neighbouring spaces.

For overall building:

- width/depth;
- floor-to-floor heights;
- bay widths;
- façade width/height;
- cornice/parapet/roof relationships.

Retain raw measurements before normalisation.

### Elevation annotation

- bay count;
- opening centres;
- opening widths/heights;
- solid widths;
- sill/head levels;
- storey-to-storey opening hierarchy;
- entrance hierarchy;
- string/cornice levels;
- visible roof condition;
- edge treatment.

### Element annotation

- door family;
- window family;
- architrave;
- skirting;
- cornice;
- fireplace;
- stair;
- portico;
- threshold.

Initially classify relationships rather than reproduce profiles.

### Construction annotation

Where evidence exists:

- load-bearing logic;
- wall thickness family;
- span direction;
- chimney/fireplace structural role;
- stair support;
- floor structure;
- roof structure.

The purpose is to avoid extracting a geometry grammar that systematically fights construction.

## 8. Rule extraction

Candidate rules should be recorded in a standard structure.

### Candidate rule record

**ID**  
Stable research identifier.

**Statement**  
The proposed relationship.

**Scope**  
Building / storey / room class / façade / element.

**Authority class**
- candidate invariant;
- strong tendency;
- preference;
- optional motif;
- human judgement.

**Evidence**
- supporting precedents;
- source texts;
- frequency where meaningful.

**Counterexamples**
- valid precedents apparently violating it.

**Confounders**
- site;
- scale;
- period;
- typology;
- later alteration;
- construction constraint.

**Proposed computational effect**
- reject;
- warn;
- rank;
- no automatic effect yet.

**Confidence**
- low;
- medium;
- high.

No candidate should become G-01 invariant without counterexample analysis.

## 9. Separate descriptive and normative grammars

This distinction is critical.

### Descriptive question

> What relationships characterise the selected Georgian corpus?

### Normative question

> Which of those relationships should the Long-Life House deliberately preserve?

They are not identical.

Examples of historical conditions we may reject or modify:

- service circulation shaped by servants;
- poor accessibility;
- historic sanitation;
- room functions no longer socially relevant;
- environmental assumptions unsuitable for future climate;
- structural practices superseded by better evidence.

The grammar is not a museum.

## 10. The first hypotheses worth testing

These are research hypotheses only.

### H1 — hierarchy is more stable than exact ratio

Principal spaces will show consistent relational dominance even where exact dimensions vary.

### H2 — topology carries architectural identity

Access graphs, centrality, depth and enfilade patterns will distinguish coherent examples independently of exact dimensions.

### H3 — façade hierarchy reflects interior hierarchy

Opening size/height and storey treatment will correlate with spatial rank.

Historic England explicitly notes this relationship in later Georgian terraces; it should be tested across the wider corpus.

### H4 — bay/order relationships matter more than isolated opening proportions

The placement of openings relative to bays, axes and room centres will prove more characteristic than a single window width/height ratio.

### H5 — good proportion lives in ranges

Built examples will cluster around families/bands rather than exact canonical values.

### H6 — structure and grammar already co-evolved historically

Wall alignments, stacking, spans and fireplace/chimney positions will explain some apparently aesthetic regularities.

### H7 — small violations have unequal consequences

Breaking a primary axis or room hierarchy will damage coherence more than missing a canonical room ratio by a small amount.

This hypothesis is especially suitable for mutation testing.

## 11. Mutation-testing protocol

Take an authoritative precedent and create controlled variants.

Possible mutations:

- shift one façade opening horizontally;
- change one opening height;
- widen one principal room;
- shrink a secondary room;
- break a wall alignment;
- move a stair;
- remove an enfilade connection;
- add an unnecessary corridor;
- move the principal entrance off its ordering relationship;
- vary one bay width;
- raise/lower one storey;
- change the relative hierarchy of upper/lower windows.

For each mutation record:

- what formal relationship changed;
- whether the design remains historically plausible;
- whether expert judgement considers it coherent;
- whether technical performance improved/worsened;
- whether the candidate rule should be hard, soft or discarded.

Mutation testing is explicitly **not** a public beauty survey.

It is a way to discover which relationships carry architectural load.

## 12. Avoid overfitting

A grammar extracted too literally from a small corpus may reproduce quirks rather than language.

Controls:

- hold back a validation subset;
- include several architects;
- include several scales;
- include provincial as well as metropolitan work;
- include different decades;
- test candidate rules on buildings not used to derive them;
- distinguish generic Georgian rule from architect-specific dialect.

A useful future model may be:

~~~text
G-01  Georgian-derived core grammar
   ├── G-01.BURLINGTON
   ├── G-01.GIBBS
   ├── G-01.TAYLOR
   └── G-01.CONTEMPORARY-LONG-LIFE
~~~

This is illustrative, not a commitment.

## 13. The courtyard problem

The Reference House's courtyard is not a generic feature of English Georgian domestic architecture.

That should be treated honestly.

Do **not** distort the historical grammar until courtyard houses appear Georgian by definition.

Instead:

- derive Georgian ordering rules independently;
- treat the courtyard as a project morphology;
- test how G-01 behaves around it;
- allow a project-specific or later courtyard extension to the grammar if coherent.

This is an important test of whether the grammar can guide a new architectural synthesis rather than merely reproduce precedents.

## 14. The Andalusian influence problem

The slight Andalusian character of the inner courtyard should remain a **local architectural dialect**, not contaminate the core G-01 definition.

Possible future architecture:

~~~text
G-01 Georgian-derived core
        +
DIALECT-CY01 courtyard arcade/interface language
~~~

The success criterion is that the house remains one architecture.

This should be resolved compositionally later.

## 15. Long-Life House tectonics as an overlay

Likewise, G-01 should not bake every Long-Life House requirement into “Georgian style”.

Keep separate:

- historical/compositional grammar;
- Long-Life House doctrine;
- reference-house tectonic dialect.

Then test compatibility.

This makes meaningful results possible:

~~~text
G-01                  PASS
Long-Life doctrine    FAIL
~~~

or:

~~~text
G-01                  PASS
Long-Life doctrine    PASS
Reference dialect     PASS
~~~

The axes remain independently explainable.

## 16. Initial deliverables before any G-01 rules are locked

### D1 — corpus register

At least 20–30 high-quality cases across corpus classes.

### D2 — source-quality register

For each case, what drawings/data are available and how trustworthy they are.

### D3 — annotation schema trial

Annotate 3 very different examples end-to-end.

### D4 — topology comparison

Generate plan graphs and identify candidate spatial genotypes.

### D5 — dimensional analysis

Measure room/elevation families without yet declaring rules.

### D6 — plan/elevation coupling study

Identify which variables genuinely coordinate both.

### D7 — first candidate-rule register

Every rule with supporting cases and counterexamples.

### D8 — mutation set

At least 10 deliberate near-misses derived from strong precedents.

### D9 — held-out validation

Test candidate grammar against buildings not used to derive it.

Only then should G-01 v0.1 be drafted.

## 18. Data ethics and historical context

The corpus should not strip architecture from history entirely.

Georgian houses encode:

- class;
- servants;
- gendered social practices;
- colonial wealth;
- land ownership;
- inherited social hierarchy.

Those histories may explain spatial relationships.

A computational grammar should not accidentally naturalise them as universal truths.

When a rule depends on a historical social arrangement, annotate that dependency.

Then decide deliberately whether the contemporary grammar:

- preserves;
- transforms;
- or rejects it.

This is part of architectural judgement.

## 19. Stop conditions

Do not proceed to G-01 rule specification if:

- measured data is too weak;
- the selected corpus is too heterogeneous to support one language;
- major rules collapse under counterexamples;
- “Georgian” turns out to be too broad a label for the desired system;
- the grammar can only be made coherent by ignoring contemporary living requirements.

Possible legitimate outcome:

> G-01 needs a narrower name and lineage.

That would be progress.

## 20. Immediate next action

The next concrete task is **D1/D2: build the corpus and source-quality register**.

Do not begin by choosing room ratios.

Begin by choosing evidence.
