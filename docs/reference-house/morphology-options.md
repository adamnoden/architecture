# Reference House — RH-01 Gross Morphology Options

**Status:** RH-01 design gate record  
**Date:** 2026-10-12  
**Question:** what overall house/courtyard geometry best satisfies the Reference House brief before room planning becomes detailed?  
**Brief authority:** [Brief and Constraint Ledger](brief-and-constraint-ledger.md)  
**Comparison drawing:** [morphology-options.svg](morphology-options.svg)

RH-01 deliberately works at gross-morphology scale. It does not allocate final rooms, prove daylight, engineer structure, select services or design elevations. Its job is to choose a spatial container that gives those later problems a fair chance of being solved without carrying unnecessary area or complexity.

The existing whole-house fixture is included as `F0`. It has no incumbency advantage.

---

## 1. Guardrails

Every conforming option must preserve:

- a genuine open-to-sky courtyard;
- credible G-01 ordering potential without inventing unproven G-01 dimensional rules;
- a relatively compact detached London / Zone-4-type family-house scale;
- a legible entrance → courtyard → garden relationship;
- enough gross depth for convincing principal rooms;
- at least one plausible high-service geography without allowing services to generate the architecture prematurely;
- ordinary external maintenance / replacement access potential;
- no room or wing created merely to complete symmetry.

The G-01 research is especially important here because it **does not** justify one universal room ratio or one whole-building symmetry rule. It does justify keeping hierarchy, route, sequence, scoped axes and plan/section/elevation coupling visible as later design variables.

See:

- [G-01 Candidate Constraint Register](../grammar/g01-candidate-constraints-v01.md);
- [G-01 Dimensional and Proportional Analysis](../grammar/g01-dimensional-analysis.md);
- [G-01 Plan / Section / Elevation Coupling](../grammar/g01-plan-section-elevation-coupling.md).

## 2. Measurement discipline

The following figures are **morphology proxies**, not final GIA or energy calculations.

### Gross enclosed plate proxy

For each storey:

`outer bounding rectangle area − open courtyard area`

This ignores wall thickness, voids, stairs and local projections. It exists only to compare options consistently.

### Plan-envelope perimeter proxy

`outer perimeter + courtyard perimeter`

This approximates the amount of vertical wall line created by the plan. It is useful for comparing façade quantity, weathering/maintenance burden and first-order envelope complexity.

It is **not** a thermal form-factor calculation. Real form factor requires three-dimensional heat-loss area, roof/floor geometry, openings and actual internal floor area.

### Normalised perimeter proxy

`plan-envelope perimeter / gross enclosed plate proxy`

This prevents a larger house from appearing automatically efficient merely because it has more floor area. It exposes the penalty paid when the courtyard becomes a larger fraction of a smaller building.

### Surrogate plot

To make external access legible without inventing a real site, one comparison uses a **15.5 m-wide surrogate plot**, with the house centred. The resulting side clearances are descriptive only; they are not planning or scaffold standards.

## 3. Comparison set

### F0 — Existing coordination fixture

**Outer:** 12.6 × 11.4 m  
**Court:** 3.6 × 3.6 m, centred  
**Gross wing depths:** front 3.9 m · rear 3.9 m · west 4.5 m · east 4.5 m

This fixture was useful because it made service topology explicit. Its weakness is that it acquired architectural authority accidentally. The ring is generous enough that nearly every coordination problem can be solved by spending area.

It remains a useful upper-bound comparator for room depth and planning freedom.

### A — Compact central court

**Outer:** 11.4 × 10.8 m  
**Court:** 3.6 × 4.0 m, centred  
**Gross wing depths:** front 3.4 m · rear 3.4 m · west 3.9 m · east 3.9 m

This option compresses the inherited ring while preserving a clear architectural centre. The courtyard's short dimension remains 3.6 m rather than being squeezed as aggressively as the house around it.

Architecturally, it keeps the strongest parts of the original proposition while stripping away its area surplus:

- a legible centre remains available to entrance/courtyard/garden sequence;
- side wings remain deep enough to test principal-room families;
- front/rear wings become deliberately shallower, forcing RH-02 to distinguish rooms from circulation rather than treating every wing as equally occupiable;
- service asymmetry can still occur *within* a balanced gross form rather than being encoded into morphology prematurely.

### B — Offset thick/thin ring

**Outer:** 11.2 × 10.6 m  
**Court:** 3.4 × 4.0 m, offset east  
**Gross wing depths:** front 3.3 m · rear 3.3 m · west 4.5 m · east 3.3 m

This option makes the asymmetry explicit. One deep wing is reserved for principal spatial use; the opposite thinner wing becomes a natural candidate for circulation, service geography or smaller rooms.

Its strengths are real:

- lowest gross enclosed plate of the comparison;
- shortest absolute plan-envelope perimeter;
- strong opportunity to cluster technical demand on the thin side;
- deep principal-room territory on the opposite side;
- potentially strong hierarchy rather than equal-ring planning.

Its risk is equally important: it may be solving RH-06 before RH-02 exists. The offset courtyard also turns the preferred entrance → court → garden relation into an off-centre composition at whole-house scale. G-01 can accommodate scoped/asymmetric order, but RH-01 has no evidence that this particular asymmetry improves the house enough to justify becoming permanent morphology.

B therefore remains a serious challenger, not a rejected curiosity.

### C — Long axial court / paired bars

**Outer:** 10.8 × 11.4 m  
**Court:** 3.4 × 5.0 m, centred  
**Gross wing depths:** front 3.2 m · rear 3.2 m · west 3.7 m · east 3.7 m

This option maximises the courtyard as a spatial sequence. It creates two principal side bars facing a long central outdoor room, with shallower front/rear connectors.

It gives the strongest raw entrance → courtyard → garden reading and the largest courtyard area. It also gives more rooms a long courtyard frontage.

The cost is significant:

- the inner façade becomes long;
- front and rear bars become shallow enough that they risk becoming circulation/connectors rather than useful rooms;
- the plan-envelope perimeter is almost as high as the much larger original fixture;
- more internal façade means more openings, weathering, drainage junctions and courtyard maintenance burden per square metre of enclosed house;
- a long two-storey court may create privacy and daylight trade-offs that cannot be claimed away at morphology stage.

C tests the upper bound of "courtyard as protagonist". It is architecturally compelling but disproportionate for the compactness brief.

## 4. Quantitative comparison

| Option | Outer rectangle | Court | Court area | Gross plate / storey | Two-storey plate proxy | Plan-envelope perimeter | Perimeter / plate | Side clearance on 15.5 m surrogate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **F0 existing** | 12.6 × 11.4 m | 3.6 × 3.6 m | 13.0 m² | 130.7 m² | 261.4 m² | 62.4 m | 0.478 m/m² | 1.45 m / side |
| **A compact central** | 11.4 × 10.8 m | 3.6 × 4.0 m | 14.4 m² | 108.7 m² | 217.4 m² | 59.6 m | 0.548 m/m² | 2.05 m / side |
| **B offset thick/thin** | 11.2 × 10.6 m | 3.4 × 4.0 m | 13.6 m² | 105.1 m² | 210.2 m² | 58.4 m | 0.556 m/m² | 2.15 m / side |
| **C long axial** | 10.8 × 11.4 m | 3.4 × 5.0 m | 17.0 m² | 106.1 m² | 212.2 m² | 61.2 m | 0.577 m/m² | 2.35 m / side |

Relative to F0:

- **A** reduces the two-storey plate proxy by **16.8%** and absolute plan-envelope perimeter by **4.5%**, while increasing courtyard area from 13.0 to 14.4 m².
- **B** reduces the plate proxy by **19.6%** and absolute perimeter by **6.4%**.
- **C** reduces the plate proxy by **18.8%**, but reduces absolute perimeter by only **1.9%** because the elongated court consumes much of the saving.

The normalised perimeter column matters. All three compact options have more wall line per square metre than F0 because the courtyard occupies a larger share of a smaller house. That is the intrinsic cost of retaining a meaningful internal court while compressing the building. RH-07 must test whether the architectural value justifies the three-dimensional environmental penalty.

## 6. Decision

### Selected working baseline: **A — Compact central court**

A wins because it removes most of the original fixture's unearned area **without making a downstream technical strategy the generator of the house**.

The decision is not "symmetry is Georgian". G-01 evidence explicitly warns against that simplification.

The decision is:

> **At gross-morphology stage, a balanced central court gives RH-02 the largest architectural design space while still imposing real compactness discipline.**

It preserves:

- a strong architectural centre;
- the preferred entrance → court → garden relationship;
- useful side-wing depth;
- a court at least as wide in its short dimension as the existing fixture;
- simple repetitive structural lines;
- freedom to make service geography asymmetrical later;
- improved side-access reserve on a medium-width surrogate plot.

At the same time, it removes approximately **43.9 m²** from the two-storey gross plate proxy relative to F0.

The front and rear wing depths of 3.4 m are intentionally demanding. RH-02 must prove that they can support a convincing entrance/front composition and garden-side condition. If room planning requires them to become materially deeper, the baseline may expand in a controlled way rather than silently reverting to F0.

### Held challenger: **B — Offset thick/thin ring**

B is retained as the explicit fallback if RH-02 or RH-06 shows that A pays too much area/circulation to keep the service-heavy side coherent.

B should **not** be carried in parallel through every stage. That would multiply design work and postpone decisions. It is a recorded rewind option with a known reason for revival:

> revive B only if the central-ring baseline cannot produce convincing domestic room order and service geography without significant extra floor area or technical corridors.

### Rejected at RH-01: **C — Long axial court**

C is rejected for the current brief. The courtyard becomes too expensive in envelope, maintenance and shallow connector wings relative to the additional spatial value it provides.

The rejection is project-specific. It is not a House Kernel or G-01 judgement against elongated courtyards.

### Superseded as working geometry: **F0**

F0 remains provenance and a useful generous-depth comparator. It is no longer the Reference House working morphology.

## 7. Controlled RH-01 baseline

Until a later gate records a rewind, the Reference House gross morphology is:

- **two principal storeys** as the working configuration;
- **outer reservation:** **11.4 m east–west × 10.8 m front–rear**;
- **open courtyard reservation:** **3.6 m east–west × 4.0 m front–rear**;
- **courtyard:** centred at gross-morphology stage;
- **gross side-wing depths:** **3.9 m** each;
- **gross front/rear wing depths:** **3.4 m** each;
- **gross enclosed plate proxy:** **108.7 m²/storey**;
- **two-storey proxy:** **217.4 m²** before wall thickness, stairs and local voids/projections;
- **court area:** **14.4 m²**;
- **plan-envelope perimeter proxy:** **59.6 m**.

Coordinate system for downstream schematic work:

- origin = south-west outer corner;
- +X = east;
- +Y = north;
- outer: X `0–11.4`, Y `0–10.8` m;
- court: X `3.9–7.5`, Y `3.4–7.4` m;
- gross centreline / court axis: X `5.7` m;
- surrogate street/front remains south for drawing consistency only;
- surrogate rear garden remains north for drawing consistency only.

The south/north convention is **not** a real orientation claim. RH-07 owns solar/orientation stress testing.

## 8. What RH-01 does not freeze

The selected rectangle and court reservation do **not** yet fix:

- final wall thickness;
- room boundaries;
- stair location or form;
- entrance position within the front order;
- whether every court edge is two storeys at every point;
- gallery extent;
- service side;
- plant position;
- bathroom/kitchen location;
- structural material or member direction;
- roof form;
- elevation bay count;
- local projections, bays, porches or shallow recesses;
- final GIA;
- final courtyard architectural treatment.

A later local projection may exceed the 11.4 × 10.8 m reservation if plan/section/elevation coupling justifies it. The baseline controls the **principal mass**, not every piece of architectural articulation.

---

## 9. Rewind triggers

RH-01 should be reopened only if a later stage demonstrates a material failure such as:

1. **RH-02:** convincing contemporary room order cannot be achieved within the 3.4/3.9 m gross wing-depth family without excessive corridor area or weak principal rooms;
2. **RH-03:** stair + upper-floor circulation cannot coexist with the court without consuming disproportionate area;
3. **RH-04:** G-01 plan/section/elevation coupling requires a materially different gross bay/depth relation;
4. **RH-05:** the ring creates avoidable structural transfers or implausible spans that a modest morphology change would remove;
5. **RH-06:** service topology requires duplicated cores, long gravity routes or a technical corridor that B would materially improve;
6. **RH-07:** courtyard/environmental modelling shows the selected court proportions or wing depths cause an unacceptable daylight, overheating or heat-loss penalty;
7. **RH-08:** external maintenance or roof/drainage geometry reveals disproportionate difficulty caused by the principal mass.

A desire for more generous rooms by itself is not a rewind trigger. Show the conflict.
