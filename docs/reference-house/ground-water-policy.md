# Ground water and rising-damp policy

**Owner requirement, established 30 October 2026.** This policy records the owner's direction for Ovid's House under the [owner-house charter](../../project/owner-house-charter.md). It governs the future site-specific house and constrains further development of the pre-site study. It establishes requirements and evidence obligations, not an accepted foundation, basement, damp-proof-course assembly or service-life result.

## Dryness by position and geometry

**Keep accommodation and essential services above assessed groundwater and flood levels, with an appropriate site-specific margin. Give surface water gravity routes away from the building, including safe overflow when drainage is obstructed. Do not create spaces whose continued usability depends on active dewatering or inaccessible waterproofing holding back groundwater.**

For this house, a pump-dependent basement or a buried waterproof enclosure needed to make otherwise wet space usable is a failure of the brief. Adding standby pumps, alarms or batteries does not remove that dependency. This is an owner-specific design constraint, not a claim that every engineered basement is defective: drained basement systems can also discharge by gravity where conditions permit [6].

Foundations may meet damp or wet ground where their materials and structural design tolerate the assessed exposure. The house must not depend on pumping to suppress groundwater around it. Separate that durable ground-contact construction from accommodation and moisture-vulnerable fabric through a coherent moisture-control design. Bulk water under pressure, upward capillary moisture, sideways rain penetration and water vapour are different duties; a DPC is not proof against all of them [1, 3].

The site assessment must establish groundwater, including relevant perched or seasonal water, surface-water and flood conditions, their uncertainty and appropriate future-hazard allowances. A dry excavation or one groundwater observation is not a lifetime water-level bound. There is no adopted site, design water level, margin or design storm in this policy.

Being at the foot of a hill is not prohibited. The section must show how water bypasses the house, where it can safely accumulate and where it ultimately discharges. Gravity outlets must be assessed with their receiving water levels, backwater, blockage and lawful destination; a downhill pipe or a gravel margin alone proves none of these. Roof-water collection must not concentrate water against foundations or discharge into an unverified soakage zone beside the house.

Any proposed undercroft, deeper service trench, sunken perimeter margin, air moat or courtyard must pass the same test as a basement. Prefer obtaining service clearance through a raised building section where appropriate, while retaining a coherent accessible principal approach. Do not excavate a collection trough and then exempt it from the rule because it is called an access zone. Existing study levels do not establish compliance, and this instruction does not select or redraw undercroft geometry.

The required bulk-water question is: **under the agreed storm and groundwater conditions, with electricity unavailable and the primary outlet blocked, where does the water go?** Also test a flooded receiving outlet where relevant. If usable space depends on pumping or inaccessible groundwater tanking, redesign the section or reject that site/design combination. This requirement concerns keeping the building dry; it does not silently determine the separate foul-drainage or water-supply design.

## Non-plastic damp-proof courses

**Plastic DPCs are rejected as the design direction. Develop suitable slate and engineering-brick alternatives first.** This is an explicit application of the [whole-house material policy](material-policy.md), including concealed joints, coatings, additives and accessories. No polymer exception is approved here, and omitting a necessary moisture-control function is not an alternative.

England's Approved Document C recognises engineering bricks and slates in cement mortar for walls not subject to groundwater pressure. Its example also requires continuity with floor damp protection and generally at least 150 mm clearance above adjoining external ground [1]. That clearance is not a flood allowance or a universal site datum.

Specify an evidenced DPC assembly, not merely a material name. The Brick Development Association recommends DPC1 bricks for buildings, while both DPC1 and DPC2 are acceptable for external works, and describes a bonded, mortared assembly of at least two courses [2]. Those courses together form **one DPC**, not two redundant defences. Slate also needs an appropriate DPC specification, bedding, joints and movement assessment; roof-covering suitability alone does not establish its DPC performance [3]. No grade, thickness, mortar mix or course arrangement is selected by this policy.

Any metallic alternative remains a separately evidenced candidate. A metal cavity tray is not automatically a suitable load-bearing horizontal DPC, and its accessories may contain polymers. ACS's stainless A-Tray, for example, requires butyl-tape joints in its stated installation [5]. Do not remove required accessories and retain the original performance claim.

## Three independent protections against rising damp

The owner requires **triple redundancy against upward capillary moisture**, rather than merely meeting an ordinary single-barrier baseline. Develop three separately identifiable passive, non-plastic barriers across each consequential ground-to-dry-fabric capillary path. This is a design requirement still to be demonstrated; no three-barrier construction is accepted here.

For a candidate to earn that description:

1. Map each relevant moisture path through external and internal walls, both applicable masonry leaves, piers, floor interfaces, corners, thresholds and penetrations. Each of the three barriers must be continuous across the path it claims to interrupt, including its joints and transitions. A convincing straight-wall section is insufficient.
2. Establish each barrier's ability to prevent the defined harmful upward moisture transfer under the specified exposure when the other two are ineffective. Record what evidence supports that performance and what remains unknown. Redundancy cannot be inferred from layer count, different material names or multiplying assumed probabilities.
3. Identify shared failures: mortar, render or plaster bridging; debris; raised paving or soil; roots; penetrating services; joint discontinuity; settlement; crushing; incompatible movement; and later alteration. Show how a single credible defect or common bypass is prevented from defeating all three. RICS documents moisture bypassing an intact slate DPC through bridging mortar [4].
4. Resolve bearing, compression, shear, sliding, restraint and movement with the actual structure, including any consequential arch thrust. Added DPC layers must not create an unassessed slip plane or moisture trap. BDA guidance treats mechanical performance and junction continuity as necessary DPC duties [3].
5. Show construction verification, inspection of vulnerable boundaries, detection of abnormal moisture, drying and a credible intervention route. Repairs must respect the project's protection of permanent fabric; repeated destructive wall cutting is not an assumed maintenance strategy.

Low water exposure, gravity drainage, splash control and inspection are essential supporting protections. **They are not three redundant capillary barriers.** An air gap counts only where its complete detail actually interrupts the claimed capillary path; a void beside a continuous wet masonry pier does not do that. Two brick courses, several slate pieces or a cavity tray serving another water path must not be counted repeatedly.

Appropriate brick and slate assemblies are preferred candidate families. The third independent barrier, the arrangement of all three and their structural compatibility remain unresolved. If a safe, proportionate arrangement cannot be demonstrated, retain that conflict for an explicit owner decision or redesign; do not silently reduce the requirement, install plastic or label complementary measures as achieved triple redundancy.

## The thousand-year ambition

**Use 1,000 years as the owner's ambition for enduring protection against rising damp.** It is not a validated service life, a quantified reliability result, a maintenance-free specification or a promise of zero risk. None of the sources below establishes a thousand-year three-barrier assembly.

The practical consequences are durable ground-contact materials, avoidance of dependence on inaccessible short-lived components, independently effective barriers, retained ground clearances, observable bypass risks and stewardship that future owners can understand. State inspection and renewal obligations honestly. A long-lived material does not establish the life of its joints, workmanship or surrounding geometry, and a century of apparently sound precedent does not prove another nine centuries.
