# Reference House — External Access & Maintenance Plan

**Status:** project brief / design-control document  
**Purpose:** translate Principle 5 and the external maintenance geography research into project-specific tasks for the Reference House without prematurely fixing dimensions or access equipment.

## 1. Objective

The Reference House should demonstrate that its exterior can be inspected, cleaned, maintained, repaired and renewed using credible ordinary methods without avoidable damage to permanent fabric or avoidable dependence on specialist access.

The plan is not a construction method statement and does not pre-design a future contractor's scaffold. It defines the architectural conditions that future safe maintenance methods must be able to use.

The central test is:

> **Can a competent future trade perform the foreseeable task using ordinary appropriate equipment without the architecture first having to be improvised around?**

## 2. Required project deliverable

By developed design, prepare one coordinated **External Access & Maintenance Plan** covering:

- all external elevations;
- the courtyard elevations;
- roof slopes, ridges, valleys, chimneys, rooflights and rainwater goods;
- site/service entrance and side/rear access;
- perimeter ground/support conditions;
- principal gates, passages and changes in level;
- significant permanent planting and mature tree envelopes;
- lightwells, basements, undercrofts, drains and inspection chambers that affect access;
- neighbouring boundaries and any foreseeable highway-access dependency.

The deliverable should be read together with elevation/roof drawings rather than as a facilities-management appendix produced after the geometry is fixed.

## 3. Access hierarchy for the house

For each task, test options in this order:

1. perform the task safely from inside or at ground level;
2. use an existing safe architectural position;
3. use ordinary collective access equipment such as scaffold, tower or suitable mobile access equipment;
4. use a ladder only where the task is genuinely suitable;
5. use specialist access only where the architectural benefit and site constraints justify it.

Do not force every elevation into one access method.

## 4. Required maintenance scenarios

At minimum, the design team should draw and review the following.

### A. Upper-storey masonry repair

Assume periodic repointing or local brick/stone repair to each principal elevation.

Check:

- likely scaffold/tower support positions;
- bays, porticos, porch roofs, eaves and other projections;
- ground conditions beneath support positions;
- material route from site entrance;
- whether temporary access requires neighbour/highway occupation;
- whether the operation remains conventional rather than becoming specialist access by accident.

### B. Upper-storey window cleaning and routine service

Test whether routine glass cleaning and ironmongery service can occur from inside where appropriate.

Do not claim the opening is fully maintainable merely because the glass can be cleaned.

### C. Full upper-storey window replacement

For at least one representative window family, draw the complete replacement route:

**room / opening → release of trims/fixings → frame withdrawal → internal or external handling route → site entrance / delivery route.**

Where external lifting/access is required, coordinate it with the façade-access strategy.

### D. Gutter and eaves renewal

Test ordinary inspection, clearing, local repair and full gutter replacement separately.

Check whether the selected access position remains usable where bays, canopies or planted areas occur below.

### E. Chimney / high-roof repair

Test access to chimney flashings, pots/caps and adjacent roof coverings.

The task should determine whether a roof hatch materially improves the route.

### F. Blocked roof outlet / rainwater failure

Assume an urgent inspection after abnormal discharge or blockage.

The route should be obvious enough that the person responding does not have to invent access under pressure.

### G. Courtyard upper façade

Assume repointing, gutter/eaves work and full-frame window replacement to the courtyard.

Check:

- whether scaffold/tower components can physically pass through the garden-to-courtyard route;
- passage width and headroom;
- turning/handling points;
- courtyard support conditions;
- protection/reinstatement of courtyard paving, fountain and planting;
- whether equipment can be erected without dismantling permanent architecture.

### H. Mature-landscape replay

Repeat the access review using the intended mature size of major trees and permanent planting.

Identify which planting can reasonably be cut back or temporarily removed and which positions must remain substantially open.

## 5. Ground plane and perimeter dry zone

Investigate whether the perimeter dry zone can also form part of the external maintenance support strategy.

This may be advantageous where one detail can provide:

- splashback/moisture separation from the wall base;
- inspectable drainage geometry;
- ordinary pedestrian access;
- latent territory for scaffold/tower support or temporary protection.

Do not set a universal width merely to satisfy this aspiration. The required geometry should emerge from actual access scenarios.

Record areas where:

- support would bear above basement/lightwell/undercroft construction;
- drains, gullies or chambers interrupt the support zone;
- landscaping must remain removable;
- temporary load capacity requires structural coordination.

## 6. Bays, porticos and projections

The Reference House may retain Georgian-derived bays, porticos and other projections.

Each principal projection should be reviewed against the intended maintenance method before its geometry is frozen.

For each, record:

- what façade/roof work it complicates;
- whether conventional temporary works can bridge/pass it straightforwardly;
- whether a modest dimensional change materially improves access;
- whether the access complexity is justified by the architectural value.

The review is not a veto on projections. It is a requirement to understand their whole-life consequence.

## 7. Roof strategy

Do not begin by assuming a hatch.

First map the roof tasks and work backwards to the access strategy.

The roof review should identify:

- components requiring periodic access;
- tasks that can be designed out or moved to safer positions;
- likely access method for each remaining task;
- safe arrival points;
- onward travel to work positions;
- roof edges/openings/fragile surfaces;
- interaction with rooflights;
- temporary edge-protection or scaffold interfaces where relevant;
- component/material handling and removal.

A rear/secondary roof hatch remains a preferred option **if** it improves the complete route. If it merely deposits a worker onto a roof that still requires an independent external access system, its value should be questioned.

## 8. Courtyard logistics route

The garden-to-courtyard connection should be treated as both architectural circulation and latent maintenance logistics.

Its developed geometry should be tested with representative:

- scaffold/tower components;
- full window/door replacement units;
- roofing/rainwater components;
- masonry materials;
- temporary protection;
- waste removal.

The route should remain beautiful and domestic. Its maintenance role should normally be invisible in ordinary use.

## 9. Landscape controls

Landscape design should classify the immediate perimeter into:

**A. Critical access positions** — preserve substantial future capability.  
**B. Removable landscape positions** — planting/furniture may occupy them provided removal is straightforward.  
**C. Unconstrained landscape** — maintenance strategy does not materially depend on them.

Avoid placing uniquely difficult-to-remove features in Category A unless another access strategy exists.

Tree selection and siting should consider mature crown/root geometry where it affects sole access positions, gutters, roof edges and façade inspection.

## 10. Neighbour and highway dependency

Prepare a simple dependency map identifying external work likely to require:

- neighbour access;
- oversailing or temporary works beyond the legal boundary;
- pavement/highway occupation;
- specialist lifting from the street.

The goal is not zero dependency at all costs. It is to avoid discovering an unnecessary dependency only after the building is complete.

## 11. Handover / stewardship information

The building record should preserve:

- the external access plan as built;
- critical support/access positions;
- known below-ground/underfoot constraints;
- any designed scaffold tie/anchor/guard sockets;
- landscape zones whose future obstruction would materially impair access;
- roof access strategy and any special restrictions;
- representative replacement routes for major window/door families.

This information should survive landscape redesign and ownership change.

## 12. Stage gates

### Stage 2 — Concept design

- identify likely access hierarchy;
- flag courtyard logistics and boundary constraints;
- test gross effect of bays/porticos/roof form;
- reserve plausible maintenance territory before landscape hardens.

### Stage 3 — Spatial coordination

- draw task-specific access envelopes for representative scenarios;
- coordinate support positions with structure, drainage, basement/lightwell and landscape;
- determine whether a roof hatch is genuinely useful;
- resolve courtyard passage/logistics geometry;
- identify neighbour/highway dependencies.

### Stage 4 — Technical design

- resolve any permanent anchors, tie points, guard sockets or special interfaces;
- record temporary-load assumptions where they affect permanent structure;
- confirm window replacement sequences;
- coordinate rainwater/roof details with access method;
- issue information necessary for the health-and-safety file / building record.

### Completion

- verify that gates, levels, drains, planting, external plant and final hard landscape have not consumed the designed access strategy;
- photograph/record critical support and route conditions;
- update the building record to the as-built condition.

## 13. Current unresolved decisions

The following remain deliberately open until the Reference House geometry develops:

- exact clear width/headroom of the courtyard logistics route;
- which elevations are best served by scaffold, tower, MEWP or another method;
- exact support-zone dimensions and load assumptions;
- final bay/portico geometries;
- roof-hatch inclusion and location;
- permanent roof anchor/edge-protection provisions, if any;
- extent to which upper windows can be fully replaced from inside;
- exact relationship between perimeter dry zone and temporary access support;
- landscape species/positions around critical access zones.

These are implementation questions. The doctrine requirement is already fixed: the answers must be deliberate and demonstrated rather than left to future improvisation.
