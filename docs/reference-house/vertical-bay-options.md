# Vertical Bay Options — A / B / C Comparison

**Status:** reference-house coordination study  
**Purpose:** compare conservative, selective-tectonic and full-reversible wall/floor bays before any becomes the project default.

All three options use the same underlying architectural wall and working I-joist structural family. They differ in how far reversibility enters occupied-room layers.

---

# Option A — Conservative control

## Build-up

- dense masonry inner leaf;
- high-quality plaster/mineral internal finish;
- certified restraint-type I-joist hanger;
- conventional structural floor / acoustic build-up;
- conventional timber / tile / stone finish as appropriate;
- deliberate wall/ceiling movement joint;
- functional skirting / cornice where useful.

## What it tests

The best version of ordinary construction. This is the control, not a straw man.

It establishes the standard the tectonic systems must beat on solidity, acoustics, build-up depth, cost, fire simplicity, tactile quality and visual calm.

## Weakness

Future access and fit-out changes rely more heavily on local openings, surface repair and wet trades.

## Why retain it

If the experimental systems cannot beat this option on whole-life value without damaging architectural quality, Option A is the correct answer.

---

# Option B — Selective tectonic

## Build-up

- dense masonry inner leaf;
- permanent boundary treatment where required;
- sparse architectural backplane on selected wall;
- robust removable mineral wall lining;
- low-service wall zone;
- certified restraint-type I-joist hanger;
- structural deck;
- local / perimeter / corridor floor-access zone rather than full-room platform;
- functional removable skirting;
- functional cornice / slip joint.

## What it tests

Whether **most doctrine value can be captured selectively** at the places most likely to change: wall fixings, data/power, panel access, floor-edge services and movement junctions. The centre of the floor remains conventional.

## Strength

Lower technical burden than Option C while retaining the backplane and deliberate-interface ideas.

## Weakness

Services outside the designed access zones may still require more disruption.

## Current development preference

**This is the preferred first prototype.** It is the strongest reference-house candidate because it applies proportional serviceability rather than maximum reversibility.

---

# Option C — Full reversible assembly

## Build-up

- dense masonry inner leaf;
- permanent boundary treatment;
- full selected-room backplane;
- full removable wall-lining system;
- low-profile finish-agnostic floor platform across the room;
- deeper coordinated service zone where necessary;
- functional skirting and cornice;
- manufactured finish carriers for timber / tile / stone.

## What it tests

The upper bound of the doctrine: maximum access, maximum depth and material penalty, acoustic and visual costs, and the extent to which future change can avoid wet trades.

## Strength

Highest theoretical serviceability and finish replaceability.

## Weakness

Highest complexity. Likely failure modes include access-floor feel or sound, material added merely to preserve removability, greater boundary debt, dominant joint grammar, proprietary hardware and little real-world benefit beyond Option B.

## Role

Option C is a **stress test**, not the default ambition.

---

# Comparison matrix

| Criterion | A — Conservative | B — Selective tectonic | C — Full reversible |
|---|---|---|---|
| Permanent-fabric protection | Medium | High | Very high |
| Future service access | Low–medium | High where designed | Very high |
| Wall thickness burden | Low | Medium | Medium–high |
| Floor depth burden | Low | Low–medium | High |
| Acoustic risk | Low | Medium | High |
| Fire/cavity complexity | Low | Medium | High |
| Wet-trade dependence | High | Medium-low | Low |
| Remanufacturability | Low | High in selected systems | Very high |
| Visual joint burden | Low | Low–medium | Medium–high |
| Bespoke design burden | Low | Medium | High |
| Chance of feeling conventional/solid | Very high | High if prototype succeeds | Uncertain |
| Whole-life upside | Moderate | Potentially very high | Potentially high but easy to overbuild |

These ratings are design-development judgements, not measured performance.

---

# Decision rule

Move from A toward C only when each added layer solves a real future problem. For every escalation ask:

1. What event becomes less destructive?
2. How often is it plausible?
3. What permanent material, depth or complexity is added now?
4. Does Option B already solve most of it?
5. What new boundary debt appears?
6. Does the room become less beautiful or less solid?

The burden of proof sits with the more complex option.

---

# Prototype sequence

## Prototype 1 — B versus A

Build the selective tectonic bay beside the conventional control.

### Wall comparison

- plaster / masonry control;
- backplane / removable mineral-panel wall.

### Floor comparison

- conventional principal floor;
- local access band / perimeter condition.

### Joint comparison

- ordinary deliberate movement joint;
- functional cornice / skirting interface.

If B cannot match A for room quality, stop.

## Prototype 2 — C only if B succeeds

Add the full low-profile floor platform only after the wall, edge and access-band systems are convincing. Do not debug every experimental subsystem at once.

---

# Present recommendation

Develop **Option B first**. Keep Option A physically present as the quality control and Option C on paper until the basic interface architecture can feel indistinguishable from excellent ordinary construction.

That ordering follows the governing constraint:

> **Serviceability is pursued proportionately.**

The reference house should become unusually maintainable through a small number of exceptionally good interfaces, not by making every square centimetre removable.
