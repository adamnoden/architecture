# Vertical Bay Options — A / B / C Comparison

**Status:** reference-house coordination study  
**Purpose:** compare the conservative, selective-tectonic and full-reversible wall/floor bays before any one option becomes the project default.

The three options use the same underlying architectural wall and the same working I-joist structural family. They differ in how far reversibility is pushed into the occupied-room layers.

---

# Option A — Conservative control

## Build-up

- dense masonry inner leaf;
- high-quality plaster/mineral internal finish;
- certified restraint-type I-joist hanger;
- conventional structural floor / acoustic build-up;
- conventional timber / tile / stone finish as appropriate;
- deliberate wall/ceiling movement joint;
- functional skirting / cornice only where useful.

## What it tests

The best version of ordinary construction.

This is not a straw man.

It establishes the standard that the tectonic systems must beat on:

- solidity;
- acoustics;
- floor depth;
- wall depth;
- cost;
- fire simplicity;
- tactile quality;
- visual calm.

## Weakness

Future access and fit-out changes still rely more heavily on local openings, surface repair and wet trades.

## Why retain it

If the experimental systems cannot materially outperform this option on whole-life value without damaging architectural quality, Option A is the correct answer.

---

# Option B — Selective tectonic

## Build-up

- dense masonry inner leaf;
- permanent boundary treatment where required;
- sparse architectural backplane on selected wall;
- robust removable mineral wall lining;
- low-service wall zone;
- certified restraint-type I-joist hanger;
- structural deck;
- local / perimeter / corridor floor-access zone rather than full-room removable platform;
- functional removable skirting;
- functional cornice / slip joint.

## What it tests

Whether **most of the doctrine value can be captured selectively**.

This option targets the places where future work is most likely:

- wall fixings;
- data / power;
- panel access;
- floor-edge services;
- room perimeter;
- movement junctions.

The centre of the floor remains conventional.

## Strength

Lower technical burden than Option C while preserving the key architectural backplane and tectonic-interface ideas.

## Weakness

Future services outside the access zones may still require more disruption.

## Current development preference

**This is the preferred first prototype.**

It is the strongest candidate for the eventual reference house because it applies proportional serviceability rather than maximum reversibility.

---

# Option C — Full reversible assembly

## Build-up

- dense masonry inner leaf;
- permanent boundary treatment;
- full selected-room backplane;
- full removable wall-lining system;
- low-profile finish-agnostic floor platform across the room;
- deeper coordinated service zone where necessary;
- functional skirting;
- functional cornice;
- manufactured finish carriers for timber / tile / stone.

## What it tests

The upper bound of the doctrine.

This is useful even if it loses, because it reveals:

- the maximum achievable access;
- the maximum floor/wall depth penalty;
- the acoustic cost of removability;
- the material burden;
- the visual burden of joints;
- the extent to which future change can avoid wet trades.

## Strength

Highest theoretical serviceability and finish replaceability.

## Weakness

Highest complexity.

The most likely failure modes are:

- floor sounds or feels like access flooring;
- too much material is added merely to remain removable;
- wall/floor cavities create boundary debt;
- joint grammar becomes visually dominant;
- proprietary hardware creeps in;
- full-room access provides little additional real-world value over Option B.

## Role

Do not treat Option C as the ambition by default.

Treat it as the **stress test** of the doctrine.

# Comparison matrix

| Criterion | A — Conservative | B — Selective tectonic | C — Full reversible |
|---|---|---|---|
| Permanent-fabric protection | Medium | High | Very high |
| Future service access | Low–medium | High where designed | Very high |
| Wall thickness burden | Low | Medium | Medium–high |
| Floor depth burden | Low | Low–medium | High |
| Acoustic risk | Low | Medium | High |
| Fire/cavity complexity | Low | Medium | High |
| Wet-trade dependence | High | Medium-low | Low |
| Remanufacturability | Low | High in selected systems | Very high |
| Visual joint burden | Low | Low–medium | Medium–high |
| Bespoke design burden | Low | Medium | High |
| Chance of feeling conventional/solid | Very high | High if prototype succeeds | Uncertain |
| Whole-life upside | Moderate | Potentially very high | Potentially high but easy to overbuild |

The qualitative ratings are design-development judgements, not measured performance.

---

# Decision rule

The project should move from A toward C only when each added layer solves a real future problem.

For every escalation ask:

1. What event becomes less destructive?
2. How often is that event plausible?
3. What permanent material / depth / complexity is added now?
4. Does Option B already solve most of the problem?
5. What new boundary debt appears?
6. Does the room become less beautiful or less solid?

The burden of proof sits with the more complex option.

---

# Prototype sequence

## Prototype 1 — B versus A

Build the selective tectonic bay beside a conventional control.

This produces the most useful information fastest.

### Wall comparison

- plaster / masonry control;
- backplane / removable mineral-panel wall.

### Floor comparison

- conventional principal floor;
- local access band / perimeter access condition.

### Joint comparison

- ordinary deliberate movement joint;
- functional cornice / skirting interface.

If B cannot match A for room quality, stop.

## Prototype 2 — C only if B succeeds

Add the full low-profile floor platform only after the wall / edge / access-band systems are already convincing.

This avoids trying to debug every experimental subsystem simultaneously.

---

# Present recommendation

Develop **Option B first**.

Keep Option A physically present as the quality control.

Keep Option C on paper until B demonstrates that the project's basic interface architecture can feel indistinguishable from excellent ordinary construction.

That ordering is consistent with the project's governing constraint:

> **Serviceability is pursued proportionately.**

The reference house should become unusually maintainable by making a small number of interfaces exceptionally good, not by making every square centimetre removable.
