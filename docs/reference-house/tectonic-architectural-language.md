# Reference House — Tectonic Architectural Language

**Status:** reference-house design language; not universal doctrine.

## Purpose

The Long-Life House doctrine does not require Georgian architecture, brass details or visible joints.

The reference house does.

Its architectural problem is therefore not merely to hide a technical platform inside a period-looking shell. The stronger ambition is to make the **visible architectural grammar and the real constructional grammar coincide**.

Traditional Georgian interiors already contain a hierarchy of edges, fields, mouldings and thresholds. Those elements can be given genuine technical work so that serviceability, movement and replacement enrich the room rather than appearing as later compromises.

## Governing rule

> **No technical feature is excused from architecture merely because it is necessary.**

Where a technical interface is visible to occupants, it should be resolved through proportion, material, hierarchy and craft.

The result should not read as an engineering demonstrator.

## The interface grammar

### Skirting

The skirting is the natural architectural datum at the floor/wall junction.

It may, where useful:

- conceal or form part of a horizontal service route;
- provide access to low-level electrical/data distribution;
- cover the perimeter edge of a removable floor platform;
- protect wall-lining edges;
- absorb construction tolerance at floor level;
- provide removable access without cutting the wall finish.

The skirting should remain recognisably substantial domestic joinery. Serviceability must not make it look like commercial trunking.

### Dado / chair rail

A dado or chair rail may provide a genuine horizontal division between wall-lining fields.

Possible technical roles include:

- concealing the meeting of upper and lower lining panels;
- providing an attachment datum;
- protecting panel edges;
- carrying low-load fixing infrastructure where appropriate;
- offering a controlled tolerance/movement break.

It should not be introduced merely because a technical joint needs somewhere to go. The room proportions must justify it.

### Picture rail

The picture rail should preferably **work as a picture rail**.

Where structurally appropriate it may:

- carry pictures and mirrors without repeated drilling of masonry;
- coincide with a backplane/fixing datum;
- separate upper and lower lining fields;
- provide a route for small reversible attachments.

Its load capacity and fixing method should be documented.

### Cornice

The cornice can become a true wall/ceiling interface.

Its possible roles include:

- concealing a controlled ceiling/wall movement gap;
- protecting lining edges;
- providing a removable perimeter element;
- concealing selected low-burden technical routes where this does not compromise fire/acoustic performance;
- carrying a restrained brass/bronze slip piece.

The cornice must not become a catch-all service duct. Its first job remains architectural.

### Architrave

Architraves can conceal the fixings and tolerance zones around replaceable door and window assemblies.

The target condition is:

**permanent opening → replaceable frame → removable architectural trim**

Removal of the architrave should expose the serviceable interface without requiring destructive plaster work where practical.

### Panel mouldings

Panel mouldings may define real removable lining fields.

This is particularly useful because it converts what would otherwise be an apologetic access seam into a proportioned architectural subdivision.

A panel boundary should not be faked into the wall surface if no panel exists. Conversely, where a genuine removable panel is used, the moulding may honestly express its edge.

### Thresholds

Thresholds are legitimate places to change floor construction.

They may:

- resolve changes in finish carrier;
- protect platform edges;
- conceal or express movement allowance;
- provide a durable wear surface;
- form a deliberate termination for removable floor modules.

Stone, timber, brass or bronze should be selected for the actual wear/movement condition rather than decorative novelty.

## Brass and bronze

Brass and bronze are a **functional visual language**, not a theme.

The reference house should use them selectively where metal is doing something real.

Preferred roles:

- slip/movement interfaces;
- sacrificial edges;
- thresholds;
- panel borders where the border protects or locates;
- access and inspection covers;
- removable trims;
- latches, handles and touch surfaces;
- ventilation grilles where metal is materially appropriate;
- engraved system diagrams, labels and durable indexes.

### Meaning

A useful house-wide rule is:

> **Visible brass or bronze should usually indicate that something moves, opens, wears, protects, indexes or comes apart.**

This creates quiet legibility over time.

A future occupant does not need to understand the whole doctrine to learn that a restrained metal line often marks an active interface.

### Restraint

Avoid:

- arbitrary brass strips with no constructional role;
- brass merely to make ordinary technical work look expensive;
- visually continuous metal grids that turn rooms into a technical aesthetic;
- custom brass mechanisms where ordinary concealed hardware would be more repairable.

The preferred construction is often:

**ordinary concealed mechanism + durable visible brass architectural piece**

rather than a bespoke all-brass mechanism.

## Panelisation without a panelised aesthetic

Replaceable wall lining and floor platforms should not force a repetitive modular appearance unless that appearance is architecturally desired.

The Georgian room provides multiple ways to absorb module boundaries:

- room bays;
- symmetrical wall fields;
- door/window axes;
- panel mouldings;
- skirting/dado/picture rail/cornice datums;
- fireplace composition;
- thresholds and borders.

Module geometry should therefore be developed **from the room composition and replacement logic together**.

Do not begin with a manufacturer’s standard grid and force the architecture to obey it.

Equally, do not create every panel as a bespoke one-off. The aim is a small family of standard interface geometries with variable visible faces.

## Material truth

The reference house strongly disfavors architectural simulation.

This does not prohibit legitimate cladding, veneer, paint, render or other layered finishes.

It does mean:

- do not use fake brick to imply masonry construction where no brick construction exists without a compelling reason;
- do not use moulded plastic to simulate carved stone or metal merely for appearance;
- do not draw false joints that imply blocks, panels or structural divisions that do not exist unless there is a clear decorative tradition and no misleading technical consequence;
- prefer real timber, real stone, real metal and real mineral surfaces where those materials are selected for their qualities.

The rule is not purism. It is coherence between appearance, material and construction.

## Patina

Materials should be chosen partly for how they age.

Desired ageing modes include:

- timber acquiring wear and repair marks;
- brass and bronze developing patina;
- stone wearing at thresholds;
- painted joinery being locally repaired and repainted;
- mineral surfaces being patched without wholesale replacement.

Undesired ageing modes include hidden delamination, plastic imitation layers peeling away, inaccessible corrosion, and brittle cosmetic skins whose failure exposes that the apparent material was never real.

## Prototype review

Every important visible interface should be judged at full scale under ordinary domestic viewing and use.

Review:

- proportion;
- shadow;
- joint width;
- alignment with room axes;
- touch;
- apparent solidity;
- sound when tapped/stepped on;
- removal method;
- whether the technical mechanism is too visible;
- whether concealment has become deception;
- whether brass/bronze is doing real work;
- how the detail will age.

The benchmark is not “does this access panel look acceptable?”

It is:

> **Would this detail still be desirable if nobody knew it was maintainable?**

If the answer is no, the architectural resolution is not finished.
