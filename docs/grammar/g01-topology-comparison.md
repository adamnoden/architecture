# G-01 Topology Comparison

Spatial organisation carries architectural information before exact dimensions are known. G-01 therefore compares topology, route, sequence and hierarchy separately from proportional analysis.

The comparison below uses Marble Hill House, Danson House and 76 Dean Street because they stress materially different domestic morphologies. Bedford Square extends the urban evidence at a larger terrace/block scale.

## Why topology precedes proportion

Rooms with identical aspect ratios can belong to completely different architectural orders depending on whether they are:

- reached through another principal room;
- distributed directly from a hall;
- separated by a secondary/service route;
- privileged by a double-height volume;
- part of a principal circuit;
- embedded in a constrained urban double-pile plan.

The useful question is therefore not “what ratio did Georgian architects prefer?” but:

> **What spatial relationships recur, how are they ranked, and which survive across different Georgian domestic morphologies?**

## Comparison vocabulary

The evidence can be described without assuming one plan type.

### Space role

- principal;
- secondary;
- circulation;
- service;
- private;
- threshold;
- exterior/court.

### Route role

- principal route;
- secondary route;
- service route;
- optional/alternative route.

### Relationship type

- directly connected;
- adjacent but not connected;
- visually aligned;
- axial;
- sequenced through;
- served by;
- vertically related.

### Hierarchy signals

- centrality;
- room rank;
- route privilege;
- sectional height;
- opening/elevation rank;
- degree of connection;
- sequence position.

These are analytical concepts. They are not all G-01 invariants.

## Marble Hill House

### Morphology

Compact freestanding Palladian villa with later service-wing development.

### Ground-floor order

The phase-coded English Heritage plan shows a central Hall flanked by Breakfast Parlour and Dining Parlour, with deeper stair and service relationships including the Great Stairs, Stone Staircase and circulation toward the later service wing.

A deliberately simplified reading is:

```text
      BREAKFAST       HALL        DINING
       PARLOUR       [P/C]        PARLOUR
           \          |          /
            \         |         /
          DEEPER CIRCULATION / STAIRS
             |                 |
        STONE STAIR        GREAT STAIRS
             |
       SERVICE DIRECTION
```

The diagram does not reconstruct every historical doorway. Its value is the overlap of two systems: a principal Hall-and-parlour order and a deeper stair/service order.

### First-floor order

The Great Room occupies the centre of the first floor and is flanked by chambers/dressing spaces. Its double-height volume continues through the floor above.

Architectural privilege is therefore expressed through centrality, room role and section together rather than plan area alone.

### Topological reading

Marble Hill can be described approximately as:

> **central distributor / principal flanks + deeper circulation/service layer**

That is one morphology, not “the Georgian plan”.

## Danson House

### Morphology

Compact freestanding neo-Palladian villa organised around a central stair core.

### Principal-floor circuit

Fabric research describes the Entrance Hall to the north, Dining Room east, Saloon south and Library west around an elliptical top-lit stair, with a secondary/service stair beside it.

The original east-of-stair space was a closet rather than a Hall→Saloon passage. The Saloon was reached through Dining Room or Library.

```text
                    ENTRANCE HALL
                    /           \
             DINING ROOM       LIBRARY
                    \           /
                       SALOON

                  [PRIMARY STAIR]
                  [SERVICE STAIR]
```

This is not simply a distributor-hall tree. It is closer to a **principal circuit**.

### Sequence is architectural information

```text
HALL → DINING → SALOON
```

is not architecturally identical to:

```text
HALL → SALOON
```

although both graphs contain the same rooms. Danson demonstrates that direct connectivity and privileged sequence are separate facts.

### Primary and secondary circulation

The primary stair and adjacent service stair also show that circulation role is not reducible to a generic “stair object”. Two physically close movement systems can carry different architectural rank.

### Topological reading

Danson can be described approximately as:

> **central circulation core + principal room circuit + paired subordinate circulation**

It differs materially from Marble Hill while remaining within the Georgian/Palladian domestic field.

## 76 Dean Street

### Morphology

Urban terraced townhouse.

Authoritative evidence supports:

- double-pile morphology;
- four storeys plus basement;
- four-window-wide front;
- entrance in the second bay from the right;
- principal and secondary/service stairs;
- stronger first-floor hierarchy.

Historic survey-plan evidence supports the broad front/rear double-pile geometry, principal stair position and distinct secondary/service stair condition. Exact historical doorway states and some room functions remain less secure.

### What the urban case establishes

Even without reconstructing every original doorway, 76 Dean Street defeats several villa-derived simplifications. A coherent Georgian house can have:

- party-wall constraint;
- an even-numbered façade;
- no central entrance bay;
- principal hierarchy expressed vertically rather than through a villa-like central room;
- separate principal/secondary circulation in a narrow plan.

Neither Marble Hill’s distributor condition nor Danson’s circuit can therefore be universalised.

A useful broad reading is:

> **urban double-pile + vertically expressed principal hierarchy + dual circulation**

## Comparative matrix

| Property | Marble Hill | Danson | 76 Dean Street |
|---|---|---|---|
| Broad morphology | compact freestanding villa | compact freestanding villa | urban terrace |
| Primary organiser | central Hall / principal block | central stair core | constrained double-pile stack |
| Principal-room topology | hall + flanking rooms | principal-room circuit | urban front/rear organisation; exact historical graph partly uncertain |
| Primary circulation | Hall + Great Stairs | elliptical central stair | principal stair |
| Secondary/service circulation | Stone Stair + service direction/wing | adjacent service stair | distinct secondary/service stair |
| Vertical hierarchy | double-height Great Room | common principal-floor datum + upper level | first-floor/piano-nobile emphasis |
| Symmetry/order | strong scoped domains | strong order with local centring conflicts | façade order without central doorway |
| Plan/elevation relation | broad shared centre; exact coordinate coupling partly open | direct evidence of controlled internal/external disagreement | façade hierarchy clear; detailed historical coupling less complete |
| Service morphology | later attached wing materially changes reading | vertically layered + discrete secondary circulation | compressed by urban morphology |

## No single canonical plan graph

The cases do not support one topology at the centre of G-01.

A single graph would either overfit the freestanding villa, reject strong urban Georgian cases or become too vague to constrain anything.

The grammar instead needs to distinguish morphology, role hierarchy and topology. The Reference House courtyard is consequently a project morphology rather than something claimed as inherited from Marble Hill or Danson.

## Topology is more than adjacency

At least four graph views are useful:

### Connectivity

Can a person move directly between two spaces?

### Route

Which path is principal, secondary or service?

### Sequence

What spaces are intentionally traversed, and in what order?

### Visual / axial relation

What can be seen or aligned even when there is no direct route?

These can disagree. A doorway can add connectivity without becoming the principal sequence. Two rooms can share an axis without a door. A secondary/service route can occupy the same core while remaining architecturally subordinate.

A model containing only adjacency is therefore inadequate.

## Vertical topology is first-class

The cases also reject a plan-first, section-later model.

- Marble Hill’s double-height Great Room changes the topology above it.
- Danson coordinates different room plans through a strong vertical stair volume and common principal-floor datum.
- 76 Dean Street expresses hierarchy through the first-floor level and its façade treatment.

Useful vertical relationships may include above/below, double-height-over, overlooks, stair-connects and principal-storey-of. Their representational usefulness does not make every one a G-01 rule.

## Secondary/service topology is parallel, not residual

Service and secondary circulation repeatedly affect the architectural reading. They should not be treated as leftover space after principal rooms have been generated.

The relevant questions are:

- what is the principal movement network?
- what other movement/service networks exist?
- where may they coincide?
- where should they remain distinct?
- how does their relationship change between floors?

The contemporary grammar must still separate this architectural capability from obsolete historical social hierarchy.

## Symmetry is scoped

“Is the house symmetrical?” is too crude.

More useful questions are:

- is this façade symmetric about a named axis?
- are these room fields paired?
- is the principal block symmetric while secondary morphology is not?
- does the opening rhythm remain ordered around an off-centre door?
- is symmetry exact, approximate or deliberately broken?

Classical order therefore does not reduce to whole-building bilateral symmetry.

## Historical phase belongs to topology

A building’s graph can change through time.

Marble Hill’s later service wing changes service topology; Danson’s later Hall/Saloon shortcut changes the principal route graph; restoration may reinstate or reinterpret earlier states.

A connection is therefore meaningful only with its relevant phase where history matters. A geometrically accurate present-day graph can still be historically false.

## Transferable higher-order questions

Across unlike morphologies, the following questions remain useful:

1. Which spaces are principal?
2. How is hierarchy expressed?
3. What organises arrival?
4. What are the principal routes?
5. What other movement/service routes exist?
6. Which sequences are intentionally privileged?
7. What is the principal vertical relationship?
8. What symmetry/order domains exist?
9. What plan/elevation relationships are intentional?
10. Where does secondary/service morphology yield to principal architecture?
11. Which relationships survive dimensional change?
12. Which graph changes would materially alter the selected architectural language?

This is closer to a reusable grammar foundation than a fixed plan template.

## Current G-01 consequence

The durable structure is:

```text
MORPHOLOGY / PROJECT CONDITION
        ↓
ROLE HIERARCHY
        ↓
SPATIAL TOPOLOGY
        ↓
ROUTE / SEQUENCE / VISUAL ORDER
        ↓
SECTIONAL HIERARCHY
        ↓
METRIC + PROPORTIONAL RESOLUTION
```

The current normative grammar adopts only the relationships the wider evidence can defend. In particular, it retains relational spatial/sectional hierarchy while rejecting one fixed room sequence or whole-building symmetry condition.
