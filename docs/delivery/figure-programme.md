# The Long-Life House — Figure Programme v0.2

**Status:** publication drawing brief  
**Purpose:** connect the argument directly to architectural drawings. These are explanatory publication figures, not construction information.

## Graphic grammar

Every figure should use one consistent legend:

- **P** — permanent / slow layer
- **R** — replaceable / faster layer
- **I** — designed interface
- **A** — access / maintenance route
- **F** — failure path
- **B** — boundary / compartment line
- **S** — spare or future capacity

The final graphic design may express these through line weight, hatch, tone or colour, but the semantic distinction must remain stable.

Each figure must answer one explicit question. If it merely “looks architectural”, cut it.

# Part I — The Proposition

## Fig. 1.1 — The house after completion
**Question:** What begins at practical completion?

A single domestic section repeated as a time-sequence:
1. completion;
2. cable addition;
3. glazing failure;
4. bathroom renewal;
5. fan/plant replacement;
6. future heating-system change.

Show what is disturbed in a conventional entangled version versus the platform version.

**Purpose:** establish that the book is about the life after handover.

## Fig. 2.1 — Different clocks
**Question:** What changes at different rates?

Exploded section with broad bands:
- structure/site;
- envelope/openings;
- service distribution;
- plant;
- controls;
- finishes/fit-out;
- furniture/appliances.

Avoid precise service-life numbers unless sourced. Emphasise ordering rather than false certainty.

## Fig. 2.2 — Coupled lives
**Question:** How does a short-lived item consume a long-lived one?

Three paired details:
- chased cable / accessible route;
- inaccessible valve / back-access service wall;
- plaster-bound window / replaceable frame interface.

## Fig. 3.1 — Selective permanence
**Question:** Why is maximum flexibility not the objective?

Two plan concepts:
- architecture made generically flexible everywhere;
- strong permanent spatial order with concentrated adaptable/service bands.

## Fig. 4.1 — Architectural platform
**Question:** What is the complete conceptual model?

Exploded axon:
- enduring structure/envelope/spatial order;
- designed interfaces;
- service topology;
- replaceable systems;
- occupation/fit-out;
- documentation/stewardship loop.

# Chapter 6 — The designed interface

## Fig. 6.1 — Anatomy of an interface
**Question:** What can an interface be responsible for?

Generic section with call-outs for:
movement / tolerance / fixing / seal / drainage / boundary / access / renewal.

Do not imply every interface needs every function.

## Fig. 6.2 — Junction versus cosmetic bridge
**Question:** What is the difference between detailing movement and filling it?

Paired wall/ceiling or frame/reveal detail:
- brittle finish bridging independently moving assemblies;
- deliberate gap + overlap/slip/replaceable finishing piece.

## Fig. 6.3 — Window replacement sequence
**Question:** Can the shorter-lived frame leave while the opening survives?

Four frames:
1. remove internal architectural trim;
2. expose mechanical fixing zone;
3. remove frame;
4. install replacement and reinstate trim.

Overlay water/air/thermal continuity that must remain or be restored.

## Fig. 6.4 — Boundary ledger
**Question:** What debts does an access detail incur?

One controlled penetration with ring of responsibilities:
structure / fire / smoke / acoustic / air / vapour / bulk water / thermal / pest / security.

This figure becomes a recurring icon/checklist throughout the book.

# Chapter 7 — Failure architecture

## Fig. 7.1 — First defence / second consequence
**Question:** Where does water go after the first protection fails?

Generic cascade:
shed → drain → contain → reveal/detect → isolate → access → dry.

## Fig. 7.2 — Water-damage-safe distribution spectrum
**Question:** What forms can safe concealed water distribution take?

Side-by-side:
- fully visible pipe;
- pipe-in-pipe;
- drained manifold cabinet;
- accessible service route;
- local tray/containment;
- sensor + auto-isolation overlay.

No single variant presented as universal winner.

## Fig. 7.3 — Appliance leak scenario
**Question:** What happens if a washing-machine hose fails?

Show maximum-credible local event:
source → isolation → floor containment → safe accumulation/discharge → drying/access.

## Fig. 7.4 — Roof blockage scenario
**Question:** What happens when the intended rainwater route is blocked?

Show primary outlet blocked, overflow path visible and directed away from vulnerable fabric.

# Chapter 8 — Maintenance geography

## Fig. 8.1 — Maintenance map
**Question:** Where does maintenance happen in the house?

Plan overlay:
utility entry → plant hub → riser → horizontal spine → high-service rooms → attic/roof → external systems.

Show working nodes and trade approach without creating a separate “servant corridor”.

## Fig. 8.2 — Vertical maintenance topology
**Question:** How does the service system occupy the section?

Section through:
plant / riser / local deep zone / wet-room service wall / roof access.

## Fig. 8.3 — Four geometries of serviceability
**Question:** What does “accessible” actually mean?

One plant item with:
approach / working space / disconnection envelope / withdrawal path.

## Fig. 8.4 — Proportional serviceability matrix
**Question:** Which future events deserve permanent provision?

2-axis or 3-factor diagram:
frequency × consequence × retrofit difficulty.

Examples positioned on it:
filter cleaning, shower valve, cable addition, full heat-pump replacement, speculative future service.

## Fig. 8.5 — Ground-service options
**Question:** How much sectional service space is justified?

Same-scale comparison:
A conventional floor;
B local access panels;
C service border;
D corridor spine;
E local deep trench;
F full undercroft.

For each show: usable area / material / access / moisture / fire / acoustic / cost penalty.

# Chapter 9 — Environmental resilience

## Fig. 9.1 — Passive first, active where required
**Question:** What is the order of environmental design?

Sun/wind/envelope diagram leading through:
orientation → shading → glazing → mass/insulation → controllable ventilation → fans/heat recovery/cooling.

The figure must avoid implying that the last steps are inferior.

## Fig. 9.2 — Summer modes
**Question:** How does the house operate through a hot day?

Morning / peak afternoon / evening / night:
shading, window state, ceiling fan, purge, optional mechanical cooling.

## Fig. 9.3 — Source capture
**Question:** Why capture contaminants before dilution?

Kitchen and bathroom plume diagrams with local extract versus dispersed pollutant/moisture.

## Fig. 9.4 — Graceful degradation
**Question:** What remains when active systems fail?

Normal mode versus outage mode:
operable windows / shading / compartmentation / envelope / manual isolation.

# Chapter 10 — Boundaries

## Fig. 10.1 — Compartmented service spine
**Question:** How can services continue while the void stops?

Two service cells separated by a boundary. Individual services cross through controlled penetrations; open air volume terminates.

## Fig. 10.2 — Acoustic bypass
**Question:** How does an accessible route destroy acoustic separation?

Paired detail:
open/rigid service crossing versus sealed/resilient/attenuated crossing.

## Fig. 10.3 — Access panel boundary stack
**Question:** Can a removable panel restore all required properties?

Exploded access panel showing:
seal / mass / resilient seat / fire layer where relevant / pest closure / fixing.

## Fig. 10.4 — Undercroft state comparison
**Question:** Is the undercroft inside or outside?

Two valid concepts:
- conditioned / inside envelope;
- ventilated external-type service space.

A third accidental hybrid is explicitly marked as unacceptable.

# Chapter 11 — Occupation without consumption

## Fig. 11.1 — Fixing hierarchy
**Question:** How do occupants hang and mount things without repeatedly consuming masonry?

Picture rail → concealed rail/ground → known heavy point → exceptional engineered fixing.

## Fig. 11.2 — Kitchen as fit-out
**Question:** What survives a kitchen replacement?

Exploded elevation:
permanent room / service interfaces / removable cabinetry / appliances / worktop.

## Fig. 11.3 — Bathroom replacement hierarchy
**Question:** Where is demountability actually valuable?

Show permanent enclosure, waterproof/wet-room system, service wall and finish. Highlight high-risk components that need access; do not imply every tile must be removable.

## Fig. 11.4 — Permanent opening / replaceable assembly
**Question:** What is architectural and what is wearing?

Window and door paired axons showing enduring opening versus replaceable assembly.

# Chapter 12 — Legibility and stewardship

## Fig. 12.1 — Information hierarchy
**Question:** How much information belongs where?

Three levels:
1. occupant/user guide;
2. quick-start/emergency/service index;
3. detailed building passport / O&M / as-built record.

## Fig. 12.2 — Building ↔ record loop
**Question:** How does the record stay true?

Design → build → commission → physical IDs → digital record → future alteration → change control → updated record.

## Fig. 12.3 — Physical service index
**Question:** What survives if software disappears?

Actual restrained plate/board design with stable route/valve/circuit IDs and QR/file-reference optional secondary layer.

## Fig. 12.4 — Commission maintainability
**Question:** How is maintainability demonstrated rather than asserted?

Sequence:
isolate → open → inspect → disconnect → withdraw → reinstate → verify boundary → update record.
