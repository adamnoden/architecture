# IAS — remove the secondary walking deck before complicating the shower pan

**6 November 2026 · bounded project-design rejection; IAS-03 remains planned.** Starting authority is main `e1284c500ca4f3cfea38c9fae56b84b3a21b1272`. This resolves the next-decision enquiry preserved at WIP `842a4271f41f276fe372a57a1e199a2898b71970`; it does not select a replacement shower or admit a complete wet assembly.

**Reject this particular closed-RHS secondary walking deck; retain the simpler pan-only comparison below.** It preserves the 1000 shower and offers smaller incoming pieces, but obtains that benefit through heavy bespoke walking panels, numerous closed-section welds, site welding of the primary water barrier and a deeper, dirt-collecting assembly. The component calculations do not identify member bending as the immediate failure. The architectural judgement is that this is disproportionate machinery for the ordinary shower/renewal duty. Reject the secondary deck before adding more bespoke feet, keepers or support hardware to rescue it. No owner budget limit is inferred from the component price.

This is a project-designed trial, not a Silverplat product or OEM-approved modification. The [actual source-choice comparison](ias-03-stair-renewal-reset.md) remains the source for B594, Dreno/Matilde and the conventional receptor limitations. Smaller replaceable parts are useful when they improve the house; they are not an independent objective that warrants a second floor over the water pan.

## Support, water, retirement and cost consequence

An operable version would still need positive module support and maintenance hold before anyone reaches beneath it; upper walking loads must reach retained support without puncturing or overloading the pan. All caps and wet crevices need cleanable finishing and inspection. Four heavy modules, hidden under-deck surfaces and many adjustable contacts add cleaning, lifting and reinstatement work compared with a plain exposed receptor. No polymer anti-slip layer, rubber foot or lubricant is waived or smuggled in to cure those duties.

The proposed retirement sequence would isolate/drain, support/remove the wear modules, release screens and sacrificial junction finishes, expose/protect the lower connection and structural floor, then cut the retired pan into restrained pieces. Incoming parts would enter separately, be supported/jigged and welded with purge/backing access, finished and leak-tested before restoring the connected outlet, barriers and finishes. The permanent floor must remain outside the cutting/heat path. **This sequence is a design intention, not a demonstrated event:** full upstands, grips, support/capture, hot-work protection, source-matched waste, lower access, water boundary, forces and whole incoming/replacement geometry remain unresolved. Field seams in the primary barrier are additional construction/repair obligations, not a maintenance-free substitute for an OEM flange.

Actual fabrication/site TIG capability exists ([Inox Works](https://www.inoxworks.co.uk/services)); that does not supply an approved shower welding procedure, duration, installed price or warranty. Stock material cost already excludes substantial skilled work: forming, fit-up, welding, distortion control, finishing/passivation, inspection/leak testing, protection, fast-layer reinstatement and downtime. Do not invent an hourly duration or free household labour to rescue the comparison.

The key difference is architectural: this RHS/sectional-pan trial creates a second manufactured floor and field water-barrier joints to solve an unproved renewal restriction. Before a complete wet-kit/handler event has been earned, its defined core already imposes55kg of walking panels, extensive shop seams and added floor depth. That is enough reason **not to spend further design effort completing this exact RHS secondary deck**; the verdict does not require pretending that all omitted hardware and duties were solved. It is not a universal rejection of stainless pans, removable finishes or appropriate welded repair.
