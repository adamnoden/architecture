## The overlooked first movement

The [diagnostic sheet](studies/copper-terminal-release.svg), [source](studies/copper-terminal-release.mjs), [tests](studies/copper-terminal-release.test.mjs) and [values](studies/copper-terminal-release.json) test a deliberately simpler question than a complete fitting model:

**Can a rigid carrier, initially engaged in two stationary opposed sockets, disengage its first end by axial translation?** This represents one adverse arrangement, not every compression fitting or union.

Take rightward movement as positive. The permitted translation is bounded by the spare travel into each opposite socket. To free the left end, available rightward travel must reach the left engagement plus the chosen separation allowance. To free the right end, the corresponding leftward inequality must hold. It is enough to free one end first; the test does not incorrectly require simultaneous disengagement at both ends.

For **synthetic** engagements of 40 and 50 mm, with a 5 mm separation allowance, the required first movements are 45 mm rightwards or 55 mm leftwards. If both ends sit against fixed stops, available inward travel is zero and neither movement exists. Unbolting a plate does not remove a body stop. Nor does freeing a clamp necessarily give the adjacent fixed pipework somewhere to move.

A **different state**, after the right terminal body has actually been removed, leaves only the left socket to disengage. An authored 80 mm free bay permits the 45 mm movement with 35 mm remaining; a 44 mm bay fails. The model explicitly assumes, rather than proves, removal of that first terminal body. A real separating joint, removable spool, elbow manoeuvre or larger renewable module must explain that operation without circular reasoning. Do not propose a removable spool whose own two stopped ends create the same trap.

These numbers are **not Conex dimensions, adequate working clearances or selected house reservations**. Lateral movement, flexible neighbouring pipework and other mechanisms could change the answer; each needs its actual three-dimensional path. Removing stops, shortening insertion or relying on elastic bending is not an authorised product modification.

## What travels through the sleeve?

The removal drawing must classify every part as retained, separately removed or carried out with the tube. Include olives, loose plates, permanent end features, supports and temporary handling attachments. Do not check only the nominal copper outside diameter.

A second synthetic counterexample uses a 150 mm minimum bore and 10 mm required diametral clearance. A bare 108 mm tube leaves 32 mm beyond that allowance, while an attached 180 mm plate interferes by 40 mm. An unmeasured ring gives **unknown**, not a pass based on the bare tube. These are invented diagnostic envelopes, not product data or replacements for the earlier sleeve study. A positive concentric clearance also says nothing about ovality, clocking, sag, friction, corrosion deposits or loaded pull force.

Only hardware that actually remains on the carrier belongs in its withdrawal envelope. Conversely, an oversized component cannot be omitted merely because it is called removable: demonstrate when and where it comes off. The whole carrier and handling equipment still need an unobstructed full-length withdrawal route; a short terminal release bay is not that route. No earlier 5250/5450 mm exterior clearance is adopted or resized by this fixture.

## Separate the two water boundaries and the load path

| Duty | Interface to resolve | Evidence that cannot substitute for it |
|---|---|---|
| **W1: conveyed water** | Copper bore, terminal connection and receiving/inlet stub under the actual hydraulic and surcharge cases | A thin rainwater swage, nominal pressure capacity of a different fitting, or an empty-bore flow sketch |
| **W2: surrounding court water** | Continuous pan/collector boundary, its fixed penetrations, wall/floor joins and the route around the outside of the carrier/sleeve | The W1 coupling's seal or pressure rating |
| **S: support** | Independent carrier and fixed-terminal reactions, movement and temporary handling | Friction at a compression seal or an annular closure treated as an uncalculated bearing |

**Develop a fixed water-boundary penetration and a separate accessible service joint, rather than asking one removable seal to do everything.** For example, investigate a continuous fabricated metal collector with a fixed outlet stub, then locate the demounting joint in genuinely accessible territory outside that collector's wet boundary. This separates the renewal tasks in principle; it does not prove the collector-to-pan joint, the dry bay, a copper weld/braze specification or its replacement.

The bay's cover, walls, floor and outlet must be traced under court flooding and a blocked receiver. An open access well below the court flood level is not dry service territory. A sealed chamber with no observable drainage is not a safe leak collector. Where a sleeve is retained, carrier leakage must still reach an inspectable outward tell-tale without soaking the floor; the permanent sleeve and pan remain larger-life-cycle components with their own eventual repair.

The later [ground-water policy](../../docs/reference-house/ground-water-policy.md) also governs this bay: do not deepen a wet service well and make it usable through pumping or inaccessible groundwater tanking. Test loss of power, the primary outlet blocked and relevant receiving-water flooding. A raised or rerouted service section may be preferable, but is not selected here.

Do not promote a new chamber merely to preserve the sleeve concept. If a credible retained W2 boundary plus independent terminal release needs excessive depth, buried joints or routine cutting, return to the accessible-route comparator and the house section.
