## Purpose

Test the attractive proposition that reversible, demountable or adaptable construction is inherently better for occupied change.

The research question is narrower:

> **Which principles of maintainability, adaptability and reversible construction materially reduce foreseeable intervention consequence, and what permanent burdens or failure modes prevent those principles becoming a universal house-wide strategy?**

OCG-02A established that architecture should avoid unnecessarily creating a construction site around work that ought to remain local. OCG-02B now tests what kind of permanent construction actually helps achieve that without converting the house into a permanently demountable technical system.

---

## Evidence posture

The literature on design for disassembly and adaptability is broader than OCG. Much of it is motivated by circular economy, end-of-life material recovery, change of use or large-scale commercial flexibility rather than occupied domestic repair.

OCG therefore distinguishes:

- **serviceability / maintainability evidence** relevant to recurrent building-life interventions;
- **adaptability evidence** relevant to future changes in use or configuration;
- **design-for-disassembly evidence** often aimed at end-of-life recovery/reuse;
- **built domestic precedents** showing what actually survives use;
- **experimental precedents** proving technical possibility but not proportionality.

A strategy does not earn OCG authority merely because it is described as circular, flexible or reversible.

# 1. Source register

## S1 — ISO 20887:2020, Design for disassembly and adaptability

Source: <https://www.iso.org/standard/69370.html>

Type: international standard; confirmed current in 2025.

Material findings:

- DfD/A principles can apply to residential buildings, refurbishment and renovation;
- the standard presents **potential options and considerations**, not one mandatory construction model;
- it explicitly does not set specific performance levels for disassembly/adaptability;
- adoption of a DfD/A principle creates specific duties only where that principle is actually selected.

Transferability: **high for OCG’s proportionality posture**. The standard itself does not justify universal maximum adaptability.

## S2 — ISO 15686 service-life planning family

Source: <https://www.iso.org/standard/45798.html> and current ISO catalogue.

Type: international service-life planning standard family.

Material finding:

- building design should recognise differing service lives and plan maintenance, repair, replacement and renewal accordingly;
- service-life thinking does not require all layers to be equally replaceable or equally short-lived.

Transferability: **high in principle**, especially for lifespan-layer separation.

## S3 — BAMB, Reversible Building Design

Source: <https://www.bamb2020.eu/topics/reversible-building-design/>

Type: EU research/innovation programme.

Material findings:

- reversible design emphasises independence between parts with different functions/lifespans;
- exchangeability and connections that permit removal without destructive damage are key concepts;
- the programme treats reversible construction as experimental and not yet mainstream commercial practice.

Transferability: **moderate**. Useful vocabulary and design logic; evidence base is broader circularity research rather than ordinary domestic maintenance.

## S4 — UK Green Building Council, circular economy / deconstructability guidance

Sources:

- <https://ukgbc.org/our-work/topics/circular-economy/>
- UKGBC guidance on deconstructability and circular design.

Type: UK industry guidance.

Material findings:

- adaptability, longevity, standardisation and reversible/mechanical connections can support circularity;
- deconstructability depends on documentation and knowledge as well as physical connections;
- demountability does not by itself guarantee successful reuse.

Transferability: **moderate-to-high for stewardship and connection principles**, but circular-economy aims are not identical to occupied-change aims.

## S5 — 2024 systematic scoping review of Design for Disassembly

Source: *Sustainable Production and Consumption* (2024), systematic review of DfD literature.

Type: peer-reviewed review.

Material findings:

- recurring barriers include initial cost, skills, undeveloped technologies, information/data continuity, recertification, deconstruction logistics and market conditions;
- environmental/economic benefit is not automatic and depends on reuse cycles, material choices, logistics and assumptions;
- reversible connection does not guarantee actual reuse or successful future disassembly;
- built evidence remains heterogeneous and includes many experimental/small-scale examples.

Transferability: **high as counter-evidence against universalisation**.

## S6 — Tarpio & Huuhka, 2026, *Why is adaptable housing not mainstream?*

Source: <https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10901-026-10293-7>

Type: peer-reviewed housing research, Finland.

Material findings:

- adaptable housing remains rare despite decades of architectural interest;
- barriers include building-services integration, fire, acoustic performance, conventional delivery and weak practical demand for some concepts;
- a developer’s relocatable partition system suffered repeated connector/material damage, visually unsatisfactory results, poor acoustic performance and little actual resident relocation;
- the developer abandoned the reusable partition system in later projects and returned to standard drywall;
- interviewees reported that ordinary, limited future provisions can be more workable than maximum transformability;
- adaptations that do not require moving wet services are materially easier.

Transferability: **high as residential counter-evidence**, though Finnish regulatory/construction context differs from the UK.

## S7 — NEXT21, Osaka

Sources:

- <https://www.councilonopenbuilding.org/projects/next21>
- Osaka Gas project information.

Type: long-running experimental residential precedent.

Material findings:

- separates durable structural/support framework from adaptable infill;
- uses centralised utility/service infrastructure, double floors and replaceable/reconfigurable fit-out;
- dwellings have repeatedly been changed over decades, proving that systematic support/infill separation can work in real residential occupation.

Transferability: **strong as proof of possibility, limited as direct house template**. NEXT21 is a purpose-built experimental multi-unit framework with substantial shared service infrastructure and floor depth.

## S8 — Historic England, repair of older windows

Source: <https://historicengland.org.uk/advice/your-home/maintain-repair/windows/>

Type: UK official heritage guidance.

Material findings:

- repair rather than wholesale replacement often preserves long-life fabric and character;
- work should be limited to the minimum necessary;
- built-in frames may be retained while individual sashes are usually removable for repair without surrounding damage;
- high-quality conventional joinery can support repeated component-scale maintenance without making the whole wall demountable.

Transferability: **very high as a domestic bounded-replaceability precedent**.

## S9 — SPAB sash-window maintenance guidance

Source: SPAB technical guidance on timber sash windows.

Type: UK conservation/maintenance practice.

Material finding:

- beads, cords, pulleys and sashes form an intelligible maintainable assembly in which serviceable components can be accessed and renewed locally.

Transferability: **high as a principle precedent**: selective access and replaceable subcomponents embedded in durable architecture.

## S10 — KieranTimberlake, Loblolly House

Source: <https://kierantimberlake.com/work/loblolly-house/>

Type: experimental single-family residential precedent.

Material findings:

- integrated off-site assemblies were organised as scaffold, cartridges, blocks and equipment;
- connections were intentionally designed for simple hand tools;
- the project demonstrates a highly prefabricated, highly reversible house-scale architecture.

Transferability: **moderate as proof of technical possibility, low as default doctrine**. It is a bespoke experimental construction system with a deliberately technological architectural expression.

# 2. Finding — service-life separation is stronger than universal demountability

The evidence strongly supports recognising that building elements have different expected service lives and that faster-changing elements should not gratuitously consume slower fabric at each renewal.

But that proposition is much narrower than:

> every layer should be demountable.

A permanent masonry wall may rationally remain monolithic if no recurrent short-life system needs to be extracted through it. Conversely, a replaceable fan, valve, sash, pump or cable route needs a credible interface with the host fabric.

### OCG consequence

The operative question is:

> **Does a credible shorter-life intervention require destruction of a materially slower host?**

If yes, investigate the interface.

If no, OCG has not earned a demountability requirement.

**Evidence status:** STRONGLY SUPPORTED.

---

# 3. Finding — DfD/A is explicitly contextual, not a maximum-flexibility doctrine

ISO 20887 applies broadly, including residential work, but deliberately does not establish a universal performance level for adaptability/disassembly.

This is an important control against a common project failure mode: treating the existence of a recognised design principle as evidence that it should be maximised.

### OCG consequence

Reject these implicit claims:

- more adaptability is always better;
- more demountability is always better;
- a house is deficient unless arbitrary future reconfiguration is cheap;
- “future-proof” is a credible target.

OCG may instead use DfD/A strategies selectively where a **specific foreseeable change family** earns them through OCG-01D.

**Evidence status:** STRONGLY SUPPORTED.

---

# 4. Finding — reversible connection is only one part of practical replaceability

A screw instead of adhesive does not make an assembly meaningfully replaceable by itself.

Practical replacement also depends on:

- access to the connection;
- safe working posture and tool clearance;
- isolation/disconnection of services;
- ability to release all hidden restraints;
- empty-space clearance for withdrawal;
- route from work face to outside;
- weight and handling;
- tolerance after movement, paint and repeated opening;
- reclosure and technical-boundary restoration;
- continued availability of compatible replacement parts.

### OCG consequence

A future **intervention envelope** must test the complete sequence:

> expose → isolate → disconnect → release → withdraw → transport → replace → reconnect → test → close.

A detail that scores well at `release` but fails at `withdraw` has not solved the intervention.

This validates OCG’s choice to analyse the complete episode rather than catalogue detachable fixings.

**Evidence status:** SUPPORTED by DfD literature, maintenance practice and precedent.

---

# 5. Finding — adaptable systems can fail precisely because they compromise permanent performance

The 2026 Finnish housing study provides unusually useful real-world counter-evidence.

A relocatable partition concept was actually implemented and then abandoned. Reported problems included:

- repeated connector use damaging components/materials;
- visually unsatisfactory results;
- tolerance problems caused by real structural variation;
- poor sound insulation;
- low actual frequency of resident reconfiguration.

The developer returned to standard drywall.

This is close to OCG’s central proportionality problem: a permanent flexibility system can impose everyday acoustic, visual and tolerance penalties while the promised intervention occurs rarely.

### OCG consequence

**Permanent performance outranks hypothetical future flexibility unless the intervention evidence is strong enough to reverse that presumption.**

In particular, OCG must treat these as real burdens:

- acoustic weakness at demountable joints;
- visible joint/fixing degradation;
- tolerance accumulation;
- loose fit / lost usable area;
- service incompatibility;
- low real-use frequency.

This strongly supports the OCG-00B incumbent posture of conventional quiet surfaces plus targeted access/service geography rather than universal removable partitions.

**Evidence status:** STRONGLY SUPPORTED as counter-evidence.

---

# 6. Finding — circularity benefits are not automatic OCG benefits

DfD research often values future material recovery and reuse. OCG values bounded consequences during foreseeable occupied interventions.

These objectives overlap but are not equivalent.

A fully demountable assembly may improve end-of-life material recovery while doing little for a leaking valve. Conversely, a local dry-side valve access panel may dramatically improve occupied maintenance while contributing little to whole-building end-of-life disassembly.

The review literature also shows that circular benefit depends on:

- actual future reuse;
- product recertification;
- storage/logistics;
- future demand;
- material cycles;
- transport;
- data retention;
- disassembly labour;
- number of realised reuse cycles.

### OCG consequence

Do not use `circular` or `reusable` as a proxy for `low intervention consequence`.

A response must state exactly which OCG-01C consequence row it changes.

**Evidence status:** STRONGLY SUPPORTED.

---

# 7. Finding — support/infill separation is proven possible, but its scale matters

NEXT21 demonstrates that a deliberately designed support/infill system can sustain repeated residential transformation while retaining a durable structural host.

Its success is relevant because the project has genuinely been reconfigured over time.

But the mechanism includes substantial permanent infrastructure:

- centralised service zones;
- double floors;
- coordinated grids;
- separable infill systems;
- multi-unit/shared-building economics;
- experimental long-term management.

### OCG consequence

NEXT21 proves:

> **deep support/infill separation is technically and operationally possible.**

It does **not** prove:

> **a detached house should buy the same permanent infrastructure.**

OCG must compare the number/frequency of interventions using the provision against the area, depth, complexity and performance burdens it creates.

**Evidence status:** STRONG precedent, limited direct transferability.

---

# 8. Finding — the sash window is a stronger Ovidian precedent than the fully demountable house

Historic timber sash windows provide a mature domestic example of bounded replaceability.

The wall opening and much of the host fabric are long-lived. Yet maintenance can often occur through local subcomponents:

- removable beads;
- removable sashes;
- replaceable cords/pulleys/hardware;
- local joinery repair;
- retained surrounding wall.

Crucially, the architecture does not announce itself as a technical access system. The intervention logic is embedded within an ordinary, culturally durable domestic assembly.

### OCG consequence

This precedent supports a stronger candidate abstraction than `demountable house`:

> **Bounded replaceability: let the part that actually changes come out without unbuilding the host.**

The candidate remains research synthesis, not doctrine yet.

**Evidence status:** STRONGLY SUPPORTED as precedent.

---

# 9. Finding — highly integrated reversible construction is possible, but can create a new dependency system

Loblolly House demonstrates that a house can be reorganised around integrated off-site assemblies and simple-tool connections.

That is valuable proof of possibility.

But the same move also creates a new architectural system whose future success depends on:

- bespoke assembly logic;
- dimensional coordination;
- cartridge interfaces;
- documentation;
- compatible replacement fabrication;
- knowledge of the original system.

### OCG consequence

Treat high-system reversibility as an **upper-bound precedent**, not a default solution.

A simpler ordinary component that can be mechanically removed from a durable conventional host may offer better century-scale maintainability than an elegant but proprietary whole-house kit.

**Evidence status:** precedent supports possibility; whole-life superiority NOT ESTABLISHED.

---

# 10. Finding — documentation helps, but physical legibility must survive document loss

DfD guidance commonly relies on records identifying components, connections and material properties.

OCG accepts the value of stewardship information, but a house designed for multi-generational life cannot assume a perfect digital chain of custody.

### OCG consequence

Prefer the combination:

- physical legibility;
- ordinary connection logic;
- durable labels/indexing where useful;
- records as reinforcement rather than the sole means of understanding.

A future worker should not need a dead SaaS account to discover how to isolate a branch or remove a trim piece.

**Evidence status:** SUPPORTED synthesis.

---

# 11. Finding — ordinary structural solutions can support targeted adaptability

The Finnish study includes examples where limited future change was enabled through specific provisions rather than universal flexible systems: anticipated openings, loose-fit rooms, or local floor/service allowance.

Those examples are analytically important because they show another strategy:

> identify the credible future change, then provision only the difficult irreversible part.

### OCG consequence

This supports **specific latent capacity** over generic maximum flexibility where the future condition is sufficiently foreseeable.

Examples in OCG terms might eventually include:

- a designed structural opening zone;
- a dry-side service access position;
- a withdrawal clearance;
- one future doorway provision;
- a bounded service route.

None is authorised by this research packet; the principle is simply evidence-supported enough for later testing.

**Evidence status:** SUPPORTED.

---

# 12. Candidate abstraction — intervention envelope

OCG earlier proposed the **intervention envelope** as the territory needed not merely to see a component but to inspect, isolate, disconnect, work on, withdraw, transport and replace it.

The research strengthens this abstraction.

DfD literature’s focus on connections is necessary but insufficient; maintenance precedent adds working/access reality; occupied-change analysis adds routes, services, reinstatement and recovery.

### OCG consequence

Carry the intervention envelope forward to OCG-03/05 as a **test abstraction**, not yet doctrine.

For a given technical object it should ask:

- what has to open?
- what clearance must remain empty during work?
- what has to disconnect?
- in what direction does the object withdraw?
- where does it travel?
- what must be restored after it leaves?

If the envelope spills across an apparently deliberate architectural boundary, later review should ask why.

**Evidence status:** SUPPORTED synthesis; threshold for acceptability not yet defined.

# 13. Hypotheses tested

## H1 — “Reversible construction is inherently better for OCG.”

**REJECTED.**

Reversibility can reduce destructive intervention, but it can also create joints, acoustic weakness, tolerance problems, visual damage, proprietary dependency and maintenance burden.

## H2 — “Mechanical fixings make an assembly replaceable.”

**REJECTED as insufficient.**

Connection release is one step in a complete replacement sequence.

## H3 — “Long-life / short-life separation is relevant to OCG.”

**SUPPORTED.**

It is strongest where repeated renewal of a shorter-life layer would otherwise consume slower host fabric.

## H4 — “Support/infill separation can work in occupied housing.”

**SUPPORTED as proof of possibility.**

NEXT21 demonstrates this at experimental multi-unit scale, but proportionality for Ovid’s House remains unproved.

## H5 — “Universal removable partitions are a safe route to adaptable housing.”

**REJECTED.**

Real housing evidence shows low use, acoustic failure, visual damage and tolerance difficulties can make them inferior to ordinary fixed construction.

## H6 — “Bounded replaceability can coexist with conventional domestic architecture.”

**SUPPORTED.**

Traditional sash-window maintenance is a particularly strong precedent.

---

# 14. Research limits

This packet does not establish:

- a required percentage of demountable construction;
- a universal maximum lifespan mismatch;
- a house-wide rule against adhesives or wet trades;
- that all services must be visible;
- that all walls/floors should be panelised;
- that a raised floor is warranted in the Reference House;
- that a manifold is warranted;
- that off-site manufacture is always preferable;
- that design for end-of-life disassembly automatically improves occupied maintenance;
- a numerical threshold at which future change becomes `foreseeable enough` to buy permanent provision.

# 15. Consequences for later packets

## OCG-02C

Research sequencing/containment/off-site preparation with the following discipline:

- prefabrication earns value where it materially removes on-site operations or compresses the intervention sequence;
- temporary containment remains legitimate;
- permanent routes/zones must be justified by repeated use.

## OCG-02D

Red-team:

- access-panel fire/acoustic/airtightness duties;
- cavity/void risks;
- tolerance and repeat-removal durability;
- proprietary systems;
- visual/institutional character;
- environmental benefit assumptions.

Use the Finnish relocatable-partition failure as explicit evidence against flexibility theatre.

## OCG-03 / OCG-05

Canonical scenarios should test **complete withdrawal**, not only reach/access.

At least one scenario should test a mature bounded-replaceability condition analogous to the sash-window logic, and at least one should expose whether a large generic flexibility system would be disproportionate.

# Result

**OCG-02B PASS — selective lifespan separation and bounded replaceability are evidence-supported; universal demountability is not.**

The strongest surviving research proposition is:

> **Replace the thing that changes without unbuilding the thing that should remain.**

That is narrower than `design a reversible house`, and substantially more aligned with Ovid’s House.

No architectural correction is authorised by this packet.
