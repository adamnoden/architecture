## Purpose

Research the work-method side of occupied change: sequencing, temporary containment, worker/material/waste movement, local isolation and off-site preparation.

OCG-02A established that many dirty/noisy consequences are contractor/method owned by default. OCG-02B established that permanent reversibility must be selective. OCG-02C asks:

> **What competent temporary and off-site methods can already do, and what permanent architecture is actually necessary to let those methods remain bounded and ordinary?**

This packet deliberately resists turning high-control occupied environments into a domestic design model.

---

## Evidence posture

The strongest containment evidence comes from healthcare and other high-risk occupied settings. Those environments prove what temporary separation, negative pressure, controlled routes and cleaning can achieve where the consequence of contamination is severe.

They do **not** establish that an ordinary dwelling should permanently resemble a hospital construction-control system.

Domestic retrofit evidence, including Energiesprong work in Nottingham, is more directly transferable but also more variable: off-site manufacture can compress onsite work dramatically, yet programme evidence shows disruption targets are not automatically achieved.

The synthesis therefore uses:

- healthcare controls as an **upper-bound proof of temporary containment capability**;
- HSE / residential-work guidance as the ordinary control hierarchy;
- Energiesprong as evidence that off-site preparation can reduce onsite exposure while remaining imperfect in delivery;
- OCG-01D to decide whether any permanent architectural response is proportionate.

# 1. Source register

## S1 — CDC environmental infection control guidance for construction in occupied healthcare facilities

Source: <https://www.cdc.gov/infection-control/hcp/environmental-control/recommendations.html>

Type: official high-risk healthcare guidance.

Material findings:

- occupied construction can be bounded through temporary rigid barriers, controlled air pressure, local filtration/extraction, sealed debris handling, route planning and cleaning;
- construction zones and occupied zones can be managed as distinct temporary territories;
- controls are risk-assessed and proportionate to the vulnerability of occupants and nature of work.

Transferability: **high as proof that temporary containment can be technically powerful; low as direct domestic specification**.

## S2 — HSE construction dust / public-protection / housekeeping guidance

Sources already synthesised in OCG-02A.

Type: UK official construction guidance.

Material findings:

- eliminate dusty/noisy work where possible;
- control at source where it remains;
- use temporary barriers/protection/separation where necessary;
- plan deliveries, material storage, waste removal and housekeeping rather than treating them as incidental.

Transferability: **high**.

## S3 — UK Government Code of Practice for residential remediation

Source: <https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/code-of-practice-for-the-remediation-of-residential-buildings/code-of-practice-for-the-remediation-of-residential-buildings>

Type: official occupied-residential-work guidance.

Material findings:

- intrusive work should be kept as short and spatially limited as reasonably possible;
- resident routes, amenities, access and communication need explicit planning;
- work methods, timing and temporary measures can reduce consequence;
- temporary decant is sometimes more proportionate than trying to maintain full occupation.

Transferability: **high in principle**, though remediation scale differs from ordinary house work.

## S4 — Energiesprong UK, Nottingham pilot

Source: <https://www.energiesprong.uk/projects/nottingham>

Type: UK occupied deep-retrofit precedent.

Material findings:

- full-storey external wall panels were prefabricated off site with windows and finishes integrated;
- the initial pilot reports occupants remaining resident and the retrofit completing in about one week;
- integrated manufacture reduced the number of operations performed at the dwelling.

Transferability: **high as proof that aggressive off-site preparation can compress onsite exposure**, but the intervention is a specialised whole-house energy retrofit rather than general maintenance.

## S5 — Nottingham City Council / Energiesprong programme evidence

Sources:

- Nottingham City Council programme reports and later Energiesprong material;
- <https://www.energiesprong.uk/newspage/17-more-pilot-homes-in-nottingham-finished>

Material findings:

- roof/facade components and some M&E were moved toward off-site manufacture to reduce onsite time and disruption;
- later programme documentation acknowledged that early phases remained disruptive and that a stated active-day target had not always been achieved;
- off-site manufacture therefore improves the available method but does not make occupied retrofit automatically low-disruption.

Transferability: **high as counter-evidence to prefabrication triumphalism**.

## S6 — UK off-site / modern-methods evidence

Type: industry/public-sector evidence on off-site construction.

Material findings:

- factory preparation can reduce onsite operations, waste, weather exposure and programme duration;
- benefit depends on design maturity, survey accuracy, logistics, manufacturing quality and installation coordination;
- rework or dimensional mismatch can erase the intended onsite advantage.

Transferability: **moderate** for OCG; strongest where the measured intervention can genuinely be converted into a prepared swap rather than merely relocating complexity.

# 2. Finding — temporary containment is a legitimate first-line system

Healthcare guidance demonstrates that temporary construction boundaries can be very strong where the risk justifies them. Ordinary HSE/residential guidance uses the same basic logic at lower intensity: source control, temporary separation, protected routes and cleaning.

This is powerful negative evidence against permanent over-provision.

### OCG consequence

The house does not need permanent clean/dirty construction zoning.

Instead, later audits should ask whether an intervention can establish a **credible temporary work boundary** using ordinary measures without surrendering disproportionate domestic territory.

Architecture becomes a material owner where, for example:

- the only possible barrier line cuts off all essential circulation;
- open geometry forces the work zone to capture unrelated rooms;
- service access must occur from the principal occupied side despite a plausible dry/service side;
- unavoidable air paths make ordinary containment materially ineffective;
- replacement material cannot reach the work zone without dismantling unrelated fabric.

**Evidence status:** STRONGLY SUPPORTED.

---

# 3. Finding — healthcare containment is an upper bound, not a domestic aesthetic

Negative pressure, rigid sealed barriers, HEPA equipment and controlled debris routes prove that contamination can be bounded even in extremely sensitive occupied settings.

But the risk justification is radically different from an ordinary house.

### OCG consequence

Reject any inference that Ovid’s House should permanently incorporate:

- clinical clean/dirty circulation;
- pressure-controlled construction zones;
- permanent extraction infrastructure solely for renovation;
- airlocks/anterooms for ordinary maintenance;
- hospital-like washable technical interiors.

Use the healthcare evidence only to establish:

> **temporary control is capable enough that permanent architecture must prove why it is still needed.**

**Evidence status:** STRONGLY SUPPORTED.

---

# 4. Finding — off-site preparation has value when it deletes onsite operations

The strongest occupied-change benefit of prefabrication is not abstract factory quality. It is reduction in the number, duration and dirtiness of operations that must occur inside or immediately around the inhabited dwelling.

Energiesprong provides a direct domestic example: storey-height panels and integrated openings arrived substantially complete, compressing external retrofit work while residents remained in place.

### OCG consequence

Off-site preparation earns OCG value where the counterfactual sequence changes materially, for example:

**Conventional sequence**

measure → deliver raw materials → cut → fit → adjust → seal → finish → cure → clean

**Prepared-swap sequence**

survey/measure → fabricate elsewhere → deliver finished assembly → disconnect/release old → remove → place new → reconnect/seal → test

The second sequence is not automatically better. It becomes better where it actually removes onsite consequence rather than moving complexity into a bespoke supply chain.

**Evidence status:** SUPPORTED.

---

# 5. Finding — prefabrication is not a disruption guarantee

Nottingham programme evidence is particularly useful because the same programme that demonstrates very fast occupied retrofit also records that later work could remain disruptive and that delivery-time targets were not automatically achieved.

Possible causes include:

- real-building variation;
- survey/model uncertainty;
- interface fit;
- supply-chain maturity;
- service integration;
- installation sequencing;
- access/logistics;
- rework.

### OCG consequence

Reject:

> `prefabricated` = `low disruption`.

Require instead:

> **Which onsite operations disappeared, and which consequence rows became smaller?**

A highly prefabricated assembly that requires extensive enabling work, awkward lifting, proprietary replacement or destructive interface adaptation may have a large OCG consequence envelope.

**Evidence status:** STRONGLY SUPPORTED as counter-evidence.

---

# 6. Finding — sequencing should preserve functions, not merely optimise trade flow

Occupied-work guidance consistently treats resident access and amenities as constraints on construction planning.

For OCG, that means the optimal sequence is not necessarily the contractor’s shortest sequence if it unnecessarily disables the dwelling.

### OCG consequence

Later canonical scenarios should state explicit functional sequencing questions:

- Can isolation occur only when direct work starts rather than at setup?
- Can one toilet remain available?
- Can circulation be handed back at the end of each working day?
- Can dirty removal happen before clean replacement enters?
- Can testing/commissioning restore a service before cosmetic reinstatement finishes?
- Can furniture/protection be reset incrementally rather than holding several rooms hostage?

These are scenario/delivery questions first. Permanent architecture enters only where geometry/topology prevents reasonable sequencing.

**Evidence status:** SUPPORTED.

---

# 7. Finding — local isolation changes outage scope; sequencing changes outage duration

OCG-02A already separated outage scope from duration.

The sequencing evidence strengthens that distinction.

- permanent topology/isolators can determine **what must be shut down**;
- work packaging, competence, replacement readiness and commissioning determine much of **how long it stays down**.

### OCG consequence

A permanent local isolator may be a strong response where it repeatedly narrows consequences at negligible burden.

That does not justify universal maximum-granularity manifolding.

Later tests must ask:

- what exact branch/function is isolated now?
- which unrelated functions are lost?
- what additional isolation point would change that topology?
- what fittings/space/failure/maintenance burden would the added isolation introduce?

**Evidence status:** SUPPORTED.

---

# 8. Finding — worker/material/waste routes deserve analysis but not dedicated permanent circulation by default

Construction guidance treats access, delivery, staging and waste movement as planning obligations. In an occupied home those movements can spread dirt and commandeer household circulation even when the technical work is local.

But the evidence does not support building a second circulation system into ordinary houses for rare interventions.

### OCG consequence

Canonical scenarios should trace:

1. worker arrival;
2. tool/protection delivery;
3. replacement-component delivery;
4. route to work face;
5. staging;
6. old-component/debris extraction;
7. waste route;
8. final cleaning/protection removal.

Architecture is implicated where the object physically cannot pass, the route necessarily destroys fabric, or one avoidable choke point captures critical household function.

Ordinary temporary floor protection and housekeeping remain contractor-owned.

**Evidence status:** STRONGLY SUPPORTED for analysis; dedicated contractor route NOT EARNED.

---

# 9. Finding — dirty-out / clean-in sequencing is useful but not universal

A common way to limit contamination is to remove dirty/demolition material before introducing finished replacement components and to keep clean replacement material outside the dirty zone until required.

This logic is robust but must remain method-level.

### OCG consequence

Carry forward a candidate intervention sequence:

> **protect / contain → expose → isolate → disconnect → remove dirty/failed object → clean work interface → bring prepared replacement in → install → reconnect → test → close → recover**

For some interventions steps collapse or occur in another order. The sequence is therefore a **diagnostic prompt**, not doctrine or a compiler algorithm.

**Evidence status:** SUPPORTED synthesis.

---

# 10. Finding — measurement uncertainty is a hidden prefabrication dependency

Off-site preparation relies on the replacement/interface being knowable before demolition begins.

This is easy in new build and harder in existing construction.

### OCG consequence

Prefabrication is strongest where architecture provides:

- stable datum/reference geometry;
- inspectable interface dimensions;
- known fixing zones;
- predictable service connection points;
- tolerances/adjustment space at the permanent-to-replaceable boundary.

This does **not** imply tighter whole-house construction tolerances. It suggests that a replaceable assembly needs an explicit tolerance-translation interface.

That idea already exists elsewhere in Ovid’s House manufacturing/assembly thinking and should remain owned there.

**Evidence status:** SUPPORTED inference from off-site practice; no new OCG design authority.

---

# 11. Finding — a bounded daily work state is often more valuable than theoretical maximum speed

In occupied settings, an intervention may last multiple days. The end-of-day condition can therefore matter materially:

- critical routes reopened;
- exposed hazards secured;
- water/power restored where practical;
- dust barrier intact;
- tools/waste removed or safely contained;
- dwelling clean enough outside the work zone for ordinary use.

### OCG consequence

Later scenarios should record whether a multi-day intervention has a plausible **safe paused state**.

This is not a demand that every system return to normal nightly. It is a test of whether partially completed work unnecessarily converts the entire house into a persistent site.

**Evidence status:** SUPPORTED by occupied-work practice; architecture share scenario-dependent.

---

# 12. Finding — service geography can function as intervention geography when it concentrates several consequences together

OCG-02A/02B suggest a potentially important synergy.

A well-designed service zone may do more than make pipes visible. It can also concentrate:

- isolation;
- working space;
- replacement clearance;
- dirty operations;
- staging;
- testing;
- leak/failure inspection;
- the start of worker/tool routes.

### OCG consequence

Later Reference House tests should not reward a riser or plant room merely for existence. They should ask whether it actually reduces **several consequence rows at once** without creating a permanently institutional space penalty.

This is where a local service geography may outperform many scattered access panels.

**Evidence status:** OCG synthesis; requires scenario proof.

# 13. Hypotheses tested

## H1 — “Occupied work needs permanent containment infrastructure.”

**REJECTED as a general rule.**

Temporary containment can be highly capable.

## H2 — “The house should at least be easy to temporarily contain.”

**SUPPORTED as a test question, not yet a threshold.**

Permanent geometry should not gratuitously prevent ordinary containment.

## H3 — “Off-site manufacture is inherently lower-disruption.”

**REJECTED as universal claim.**

It is useful only where it deletes or compresses real onsite operations; pilot delivery targets can still fail.

## H4 — “Worker/material/waste routes are contractor problems only.”

**REJECTED.**

Method owns protection/housekeeping, but geometry can materially determine whether routes consume unrelated domestic territory or whether replacement objects can move at all.

## H5 — “Local isolation is valuable.”

**SUPPORTED conditionally.**

It has clear potential to narrow outage scope, but granularity must earn its permanent fittings/space/maintenance burden.

## H6 — “An intervention should support a bounded paused state.”

**SUPPORTED as occupied-work analytical principle.**

Whether architecture or method owns that state remains scenario-specific.

---

# 14. Research limits

This packet does not establish:

- a mandatory dust-pressure regime in houses;
- a required second contractor route;
- a universal maximum on active work days;
- a universal same-day service-restoration requirement;
- that every replacement should be prefabricated;
- that every room must be independently isolatable;
- that every intervention can remain occupied;
- a requirement for permanent staging space;
- that the Reference House currently passes any containment/logistics test.

# 15. Consequences for OCG-02D and OCG-03

## OCG-02D

Red-team any proposed permanent response against the demonstrated capability of temporary works.

Particular questions:

- Is a permanent cavity/panel/route being added to solve something a temporary barrier already handles?
- Does off-site manufacture create proprietary dependency or lifting/logistics burdens?
- Does the serviceability system itself introduce fire/acoustic/moisture/tolerance duties?

## OCG-03

Canonical scenario coverage should include:

- an intervention where temporary containment is sufficient and architecture should **not** change;
- an intervention where architecture creates avoidable dirty access despite good method;
- a component replacement where withdrawal/logistics, not access, is the limiting issue;
- a service intervention where local isolation meaningfully changes outage scope;
- an intervention where off-site prepared replacement compresses onsite work;
- an IC6 case where decant/major site conditions are accepted rather than permanently designed out.

These are coverage requirements, not the selected scenario set.

# Result

**OCG-02C PASS — competent temporary containment and sequencing are strong first-line controls; permanent architecture earns a role only where geometry, topology or interfaces materially prevent the work from staying bounded.**

The strongest surviving research proposition is:

> **Prepare elsewhere where useful; contain temporarily; isolate narrowly; move cleanly; and require permanent architecture only where it changes that sequence materially.**

No architectural correction is authorised by this packet.
