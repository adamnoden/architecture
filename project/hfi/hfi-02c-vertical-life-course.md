## 1. Purpose

A two-storey house creates a durable life-course question that entrance visitability alone cannot answer:

> **What happens when an occupant can still live independently but can no longer use the stair reliably?**

The answer is not automatically “install a lift”. Nor is “there is a WC downstairs” an adequate whole-life strategy.

HFI-02C tests five materially different provisions:

1. ordinary good stair geometry;
2. a stair that can later accept a stairlift;
3. an entrance-level living / sleeping / bathing fallback mode;
4. a deliberately reserved future through-floor lift route;
5. a lift installed from completion.

These provisions solve different problems for different people. They must not be collapsed into the generic word **adaptable**.

The controlling HFI-01D question is:

> **Which irreversible provision buys broad, consequential future utility at low permanent burden, and which provision should wait for a defined user need?**

# 2. Terms specific to this packet

## 2.1 Entrance-level fallback living

A dwelling condition in which the entrance storey can support the essential daily cycle if routine stair use becomes impossible or temporarily unsafe.

At minimum this means credible access to:

- entrance and principal living space;
- food preparation / eating;
- WC;
- washing/showering;
- a plausible sleeping place.

It does **not** mean every room on the upper floor remains usable.

## 2.2 Stairlift-compatible

A stair arrangement for which a competent future supplier could install an appropriate stairlift without major structural rebuild or destroying the essential circulation function of the stair/landings.

Nominal width alone does not prove compatibility.

Relevant conditions can include clear width, flight geometry, landings and turns, rail run and parking position, door conflicts, transfer position, handrail/guarding interaction, available power, residual ordinary-stair use and user ability to transfer to/from the chair.

## 2.3 Future lift route

A vertically aligned architectural reservation capable of accepting an appropriate lifting device in the future **without structural alteration inconsistent with the claimed provision**.

A vague “soft spot” or an empty cupboard on one floor is not a future lift route.

## 2.4 Installed vertical lift access

A commissioned lift/homelift/lifting platform providing step-free movement between storeys at completion.

It carries product, maintenance, servicing, power, inspection, fire/door/interface and replacement obligations that a spatial reservation does not.

## 2.5 Assistance

Help from another person with movement or daily tasks.

HFI treats assistance as a legitimate scenario, not as an architectural substitute for independent use. Manual handling and transfer technique require occupational-therapy/clinical/specialist judgement where material.

# 5. Stairlift provision and lift provision solve different problems

## 5.1 Stairlift provision

A stairlift is a relatively low-burden retrofit path for a person who can use the chair/platform arrangement and safely transfer at each end. It can preserve upper-floor use without dedicating a vertical shaft through the house from day one.

But it is not equivalent to wheelchair circulation between storeys. Depending on the user and product, unresolved issues include transfer, mobility-aid availability at each floor, parking position, obstruction of ordinary stair use, rail geometry around turns/landings, upper-body/balance capability, power and emergency recovery.

Therefore:

> **“Stairlift-compatible” is a valuable claim only if the stair/landing sequence is genuinely installable, not merely wider than a nominal minimum.**

## 5.2 Through-floor lift provision

A through-floor lift or vertical lifting platform can maintain step-free inter-storey access for a broader class of mobility conditions, including wheelchair use depending on the selected system.

Its architectural burden is larger because the provision affects both floors at the same position, approach/turning space, floor structure, service routes, power, doors/guarding/enclosure, fire/acoustic interfaces and future maintenance/replacement.

M4(3)'s future-lift provisions are useful precisely because they reveal that a credible lift route is a **whole-building alignment commitment**.

## 5.3 Installed lift from completion

Installing a lift immediately may be necessary for a known user/brief and removes later installation delay. For a generic house with no identified need it also imports product lifecycle, servicing, inspection/repair access, powered equipment, replacement and acoustic/interface obligations from day one.

HFI-02C finds no evidence basis for making an installed lift the generic Reference House default.

# 6. Entrance-level fallback living is a separate high-value strategy

If stair use is temporarily or permanently lost, one low-burden way to preserve occupation is to ensure the entrance level can sustain essential daily life.

This can serve temporary injury, stairlift breakdown, age-related reduction in stair use and some care scenarios without requiring a permanently installed lifting machine.

For a family house this is a **fallback mode**, not automatically an equivalent permanent bedroom suite. A credible fallback sleeping option must later be tested for privacy, habitable-room quality, bed placement, route clearances, relationship to shower/WC, disturbance from circulation and whether conversion destroys an essential household function.

HFI rejects both extremes:

> `There is a sofa downstairs, therefore the house is life-course adaptable.`

and

> `Unless every upper room remains wheelchair accessible, entrance-level fallback has no value.`

The value is real but bounded.

---

# 7. Entrance-level washing / showering is disproportionately useful

For a two-storey family house, loss of stair access becomes much more consequential if sleeping can move downstairs but bathing cannot.

Approved Document M M4(2) is notable here: in a two-/three-storey dwelling with three or more bedrooms it expects the entrance-storey WC/basin room to include an installed or **potential level-access shower**.

This is a strong example of low-burden construction-time foresight because the destructive part is often hidden floor/drainage preparation rather than the later visible fitting.

The Reference House already claims this intent.

HFI-02C finds this **type of provision strongly proportionate in principle** for the selected four-bedroom / two-storey programme because:

- the consequence of no washing facility downstairs can be high;
- drainage/floor preparation is much harder to add later;
- the provision can remain visually ordinary until needed;
- the same room already supports ordinary WC use;
- it serves temporary as well as long-term mobility loss.

This is **not** yet a finding that current `G-WC-01` geometry works. HFI-04A and HFI-07B must prove the fixture/use geometry and water-management detail.

---

# 8. Stairlift readiness: strategy supported, current claim not yet proved

Approved Document M M4(2) explicitly uses a stair capable of later stairlift installation and sets a minimum `850 mm` clear width condition for the relevant stair.

The Reference House currently carries approximately `1000 mm` clear width. That is promising, but HFI-02C does not accept “future stairlift option” as proved because:

1. the house uses three flights and full landings;
2. rail continuity/turn behaviour is product-specific;
3. parked equipment must not destroy ordinary landing use;
4. head/foot transfer conditions are not represented at product-installation resolution;
5. handrail/guarding interaction is unresolved;
6. final clear width after construction/handrails is not frozen;
7. power and product replacement route are not modelled.

Current status:

> **The Reference House has a credible stairlift-readiness hypothesis, not a proven installation path.**

HFI-07C later judges this using the frozen audit vocabulary.

# 9. The strongest counterprecedent: Lifetime Homes required a future lift route

The 2010 Lifetime Homes Standard is the principal challenge to any claim that future lift reservation is obviously excessive.

Its mainstream life-course package combined:

- entrance-level living;
- a potential entrance-level bed-space;
- WC/shower-drainage provision;
- stairlift readiness;
- and a future through-floor lift route.

This matters for two reasons.

First, future lift provision is **not an exotic idea invented by wheelchair-specific M4(3)**. It has a mature history as a mainstream adaptability strategy.

Second, current M4(2) no longer includes the same explicit through-floor-lift requirement. HFI must not infer the policy reason for that change without evidence. The correct conclusion is simply that **current statutory guidance and mature historical precedent draw the proportionality line differently**.

Therefore HFI-02C does **not** close the future-lift question by appeal to M4(2).

# 10. Should the Reference House reserve a future through-floor lift?

This remains a live proportionality challenger rather than a settled yes/no.

## 10.1 Benefit case

A genuine future lift route could:

- preserve access to both storeys for people unable to use/transfer to a stairlift;
- reduce later structural disruption;
- shorten adaptation lead time;
- preserve the original bedroom/bathroom arrangement;
- support some wheelchair and care scenarios more independently than a stairlift.

The benefits are meaningful and Lifetime Homes shows a mature precedent for buying them early.

## 10.2 Burden case

A credible provision is not a token void.

Current M4(3) guidance reveals the stronger end of the commitment: a future liftway at least `1100 × 1650 mm` internally, aligned through floors and circulation, removable construction without structural alteration, and future approach/turning conditions demonstrated. Some products need more.

Depending on how cleverly it is integrated, a Reference House reservation could constrain:

- planning on both floors;
- floor framing/opening strategy;
- service-route geography;
- circulation and door positions;
- acoustic/fire interfaces;
- future irreversible use of the reserved zone.

But **gross reserved dimensions do not equal gross wasted area**. Lifetime Homes and M4(3) both permit the future lift zone to have an interim use, subject to conditions. Stacked storage or another deliberately removable use may reduce the everyday burden substantially.

This is exactly why a plan-specific alternative is needed before proportional rejection.

## 10.3 Scenario coverage

Many temporary injuries and ambulant age-related mobility losses can be served by good stairs, reduced stair frequency, stairlift installation and entrance-level fallback living.

A future lift route becomes more valuable when:

- wheelchair access to both floors is a real objective;
- stairlift transfer is not feasible;
- upper-floor facilities must remain independently usable;
- progressive mobility loss is foreseeable;
- the brief explicitly values whole-house step-free life-course access.

## 10.4 Packet verdict

HFI-02C finds:

> **A full future lift route is not yet earned as a generic Ovid's House requirement, but it is too credible and too historically well-established to reject without testing an actual low-burden Reference House option.**

That option must be generated later only if HFI-05B requires it for proportional comparison; this packet does not redesign the house.

The current working hierarchy is therefore:

1. strong ordinary stair;
2. prove genuine stairlift readiness;
3. preserve entrance-level sleeping fallback;
4. preserve technically real potential level-access shower;
5. keep a bounded future-lift reservation as a **live challenger**, not a default and not a dismissed idea;
6. install lifting machinery when an actual user/brief/regulatory condition warrants it.

# 11. “Soft spot” versus credible lift route

A locally framed floor panel, stacked cupboards or removable joinery can be valuable if they genuinely make future installation easier.

But HFI rejects calling such an arrangement a **future lift route** unless later design proves sufficient device envelope, aligned access, intended approach/turning conditions, no destructive structural/service conflict, viable enclosure/door conditions, power and installation/replacement route.

Otherwise it is only a potentially useful **adaptation opportunity**.

False preparedness is worse than an explicit unknown.

---

# 12. External future lift addition

An external lift or lifting platform can sometimes preserve internal floor area and be added later.

HFI-02C does not treat this as a generic fallback because it is highly site-specific: boundaries/setbacks, planning/conservation, weather, external landings, facade openings, maintenance/rescue access, security and real ground conditions all matter.

The site-agnostic Reference House must not invent an external-lift route a real plot may make impossible.

---

# 13. Assistance and care

Life-course design can quietly assume another person will compensate for inaccessible architecture.

HFI rejects that shortcut. “A partner can help them upstairs” is not a vertical-access strategy, and carrying/manually supporting an adult on stairs is not a default architectural provision.

At the same time, HFI does not require every mainstream home to be a high-dependency care setting.

The proportional position is:

> **Do not make ordinary assistance unnecessarily difficult, but do not import specialist-care geometry without a defined need.**

HFI-04A should test bounded assistance scenarios in bathing/dressing. HFI-08D should include occupational-therapy / inclusive-design review if final propositions depend materially on assisted-use claims.

---

# 14. Temporary impairment strengthens the low-burden case

Life-course provision is not only about old age or permanent disability.

A broken ankle, surgery, pregnancy complication, short-term balance impairment or fatigue can make a stair temporarily unusable while leaving the occupant otherwise independent.

This strengthens the proportional case for provisions that are already useful in ordinary life, low-visibility, low-maintenance and immediately available:

- step-free entrance-storey circulation;
- living/eating at entrance level;
- entrance-level WC;
- potential level-access shower;
- a habitable room capable of temporary sleeping.

It gives much less support to permanently installed lifting machinery without a known user need.

---

# 15. Reversibility and timing

The Ageing Better evidence review provides a useful general warning: adaptations can be effective; delays can reduce benefit; and people may resist adaptations that make the home feel medicalised.

This favours a two-part architecture.

### Build early where later retrofit is destructive

Potential examples:

- drainage/floor provision for a future level-access shower;
- wall/structure capacity where broadly useful and low-burden;
- stair geometry that does not block later stairlift installation;
- a genuinely low-burden future lift route **if** a plan-specific comparison later earns it;
- simple power/service enabling where negligible in burden.

### Install later where equipment is user- and product-specific

Examples:

- stairlift chair/rail;
- through-floor lift machinery;
- specialist transfer aids;
- user-positioned grab rails;
- specialist care equipment.

This aligns with Ovid's House's wider preference for durable enabling infrastructure without premature product lock-in.

# 18. Rejected or bounded hypotheses

## `R-LC-01` — “Step-free ground floor solves life-course accessibility”

**Rejected.** It improves visitability/fallback use but does not preserve upper-floor access.

## `R-LC-02` — “A 1000 mm stair proves stairlift readiness”

**Rejected.** Width is only one installation variable.

## `R-LC-03` — “A stairlift is equivalent to whole-dwelling wheelchair access”

**Rejected.** Transfer capability, mobility-aid availability and wheelchair transport differ materially.

## `R-LC-04` — “Future lift provision is just a removable floor panel”

**Rejected.** A credible provision is a vertically aligned circulation/structure/service commitment.

## `R-LC-05` — “Every two-storey house should reserve a lift route”

**Not established.** Lifetime Homes is strong precedent, but HFI-01D requires an actual burden/benefit comparison before adoption.

## `R-LC-06` — “Future lift reservation is obviously over-specification”

**Rejected.** Lifetime Homes makes this historically indefensible as a casual assumption; a low-burden dual-use route remains a serious challenger.

## `R-LC-07` — “A lift is never proportionate in a mainstream house”

**Rejected.** A known user, progressive condition, M4(3) requirement or explicit whole-house step-free brief can justify it strongly.

## `R-LC-08` — “Human assistance can substitute for vertical access”

**Rejected as a generic design premise.** Assistance is a scenario, not default compensation for inaccessible architecture.

## `R-LC-09` — “Adaptations should all be installed at completion”

**Rejected.** Durable enabling work and demand-led equipment have different optimal timing.

## `R-LC-10` — “Adaptations should all wait until needed”

**Rejected.** Some hidden/structural/drainage enabling work becomes disproportionately disruptive if deferred.

# 21. Packet conclusion

HFI-02C finds a strong distinction between **adaptation-ready architecture** and **premature equipment installation**.

The evidence strongly favours entrance-level fallback living, technically real future shower provision and genuine stairlift readiness as low-burden life-course moves.

The through-floor-lift question is deliberately left harder:

> **Current M4(2) does not require a future lift route, but Lifetime Homes did. A future route is therefore neither an obvious default nor obvious over-specification.
