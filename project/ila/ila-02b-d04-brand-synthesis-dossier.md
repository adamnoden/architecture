# 1. Executive decision

Stewart Brand is a **FOUNDATIONAL earlier antecedent** to ILA-C02 as Ovid currently formulates it.

That importance does **not** rest on Brand being first to notice unequal component lives, first to propose independent replacement, or first to warn against destructive physical dependence. ILA-01 already established earlier architectural antecedents, especially SCAG and Century Housing System, for those mechanisms.

Brand matters for a different and more specific reason.

In *How Buildings Learn* (1994), Brand explicitly combines:

1. **Frank Duffy's architectural building-layer model** — components operating at different temporal rates;
2. **R. V. O'Neill et al.'s ecological hierarchy model** — system organisation arising from differences in process rates, with slower processes constraining faster ones;
3. Brand's own architectural synthesis around **shearing, slippage and destructive embedding**.

The resulting proposition is already extremely close to Ovid's current C02 rhetoric.

Brand does not merely draw six differently lived layers. He states an architectural design imperative:

- an adaptive building must permit **slippage** between differently paced systems;
- otherwise slow systems block faster ones;
- faster systems damage slower ones through repeated change;
- embedding differently paced systems together may appear efficient initially but becomes destructive over time.

He then gives physical building examples, including buried services and construction systems that over-connect structure, skin and services.

This materially changes the Ovid delta.

After D03, one possible residual still appeared to be:

> Duffy owned temporal decomposition and generic independence; Ovid sharpened that into explicit physical destructive coupling.

D04 removes even that as a defensible distinction from the project's **already named lineage**.

Brand's own 1994 argument already owns the broad mechanism:

> **different rates + insufficient slippage / embedded systems → fast change damages slow construction.**

Therefore Ovid must not position `coupling`, `fast damages slow`, or `avoid embedding faster systems in slower systems` as its conceptual contribution.

The strongest remaining Ovid interest is narrower and synthetic:

> **Ovid turns an inherited destructive-dependency principle into a house-wide governance and execution discipline: explicit replacement-destruction questions, maintenance geography, access/withdrawal/reinstatement sequences, boundary ledgers, failure containment, proportional serviceability, workmanship controls and typed pattern/evidence/compiler authority.**

Even that is not yet an originality claim. D05 and the remaining ILA dossiers test how much of the physical operationalisation is also established prior art.

D04 also corrects the current reader-facing shorthand.

Current Part I says, in effect:

> Duffy described different-rate layers; Brand popularised a related shearing-layer model; **but** the important condition is coupling.

That is directionally too weak on Brand.

Brand is not merely the populariser who precedes Ovid's coupling insight. His shearing-layer chapter already makes **interaction between rates, slippage and destructive embedding** the architectural point.

D04 therefore creates strong provisional **T5 treatment pressure**: not because the manuscript makes an explicit false priority claim, but because its rhetorical sequence materially understates the intellectual debt and can imply that coupling is the step Ovid adds after Brand.

No manuscript edit is made here. ILA-04 owns the final attribution finding and ILA-05 the smallest correction.

# 4. Brand explicitly derives his layer schema from Duffy

The opening of `Shearing Layers` identifies Frank Duffy as the leading theorist of rates of change in buildings and quotes Duffy's proposition that a building is several layers of longevity of built components.

Brand presents Duffy's four layers:

- Shell;
- Services;
- Scenery;
- Set.

The Duffy quotation used by Brand is from:

**Francis Duffy**, `Measuring Building Performance`, *Facilities* 8(5), May 1990, p. 17.

D03 has already shown that the underlying temporal architecture is older than that 1990 formulation and is strongly present in *Planning Office Space* (1976). D04 therefore distinguishes:

- the **source Brand actually quotes / uses** in 1994;
- the **earlier history of the proposition** established independently by D03.

Brand then translates / expands Duffy's model into six layers:

- Site;
- Structure;
- Skin;
- Services;
- Space Plan;
- Stuff.

Brand's 2018 retrospective removes any ambiguity about the source-to-source relationship. He says explicitly that Duffy had four layers and that for *How Buildings Learn* he expanded them to six.

### Edge

`Duffy → Brand building-layer schema = DOCUMENTED_DERIVATION`  
Confidence: `DOCUMENTED`.

### What the edge does not mean

It does not mean:

- Duffy invented every underlying principle of lifecycle separation;
- Brand merely recopied Duffy;
- Brand's ecological hierarchy argument came from Duffy;
- Ovid's project contact with Brand automatically implies project contact with Duffy or vice versa.

D03 and D04 keep those edges separate.

# 5. Brand's six-S change is a real synthesis, not merely relabelling

Brand does more than rename Duffy's four categories.

## 5.1 He generalises beyond the commercial-office problem

Duffy's canonical model is strongly rooted in workplace / commercial property:

- landlord shell;
- tenant fit-out;
- changing organisational requirements;
- services and office planning.

Brand's six layers are presented as a broad building model.

Adding:

- **Site**;
- **Skin** as distinct from Structure;
- a renamed **Space Plan**;
- **Stuff**;

allows the temporal model to speak more directly to houses, ordinary buildings, facade change and site persistence.

This matters to Ovid because Brand is already a cross-building-type translator before Ovid applies the same broad logic to a house.

## 5.2 He makes the hierarchy relational

The six-S diagram is not Brand's most important contribution.

The stronger move is to ask what happens **between** layers changing at different rates.

The 1994 chapter develops:

- slow → fast constraint;
- fast → slow accumulated influence;
- different organisational actors at different pace levels;
- physical interference when layers cannot move at their own pace;
- slippage as an adaptive requirement.

That is substantially more than a taxonomy.

# 7. O'Neill → Brand is directly evidenced

Brand cites *A Hierarchical Concept of Ecosystems* in the `Shearing Layers` chapter and uses its slow/fast proposition as the conceptual bridge from different building lifespans to a hierarchy of interaction.

The chapter reproduces O'Neill's proposition that system dynamics are dominated by slower components, with rapid components following.

Brand then compresses the architectural consequence:

> slow constrains quick; slow controls quick.

He immediately applies the idea to building layers:

- Site constrains Structure;
- Structure constrains Skin;
- Skin constrains Services;
- Services constrain Space Plan;
- Space Plan constrains Stuff.

Brand also states the reverse influence:

- repeated fast-layer behaviour can eventually be absorbed by slower layers;
- the fast proposes and the slow disposes.

### Edge

`O'Neill et al. → Brand slow/fast hierarchy = DOCUMENTED_DERIVATION`  
Confidence: `DOCUMENTED`.

### Chronology boundary

O'Neill (1986) cannot be a source of Duffy's early temporal layer model.

Duffy's work is already explicit well before 1986.

```text
O'Neill ecological rate hierarchy ──────┘
```


# 9. Brand's decisive architectural move: slippage

The most important D04 finding is on p. 20 of *How Buildings Learn*.

Brand states an explicit **design imperative** for adaptive buildings.

Paraphrased under the ILA contract:

> differently paced building systems need enough freedom at their interfaces to change at their own rates; without that freedom, slower systems obstruct faster change and repeated fast change damages slower construction; integrating the layers too tightly can therefore become destructive over time.

Brand calls the required freedom **slippage**.

This is not an inference drawn by later adaptability literature.

It is the governing architectural statement of Brand's own chapter.

### Physical examples

Brand immediately grounds the proposition in construction.

He criticises slab-on-grade construction where services are buried and maintenance / expansion access is poor.

He contrasts this with timber framing that more conveniently separates Structure, Skin and Services, while noting that standard stud construction can also over-connect layers.

The point is physical:

> rate mismatch becomes an architectural problem when the assembly denies independent change.

This is conceptually very close to Ovid C02.

# 13. Brand and ILA-C04 — change plus continuity

Brand's hierarchy also anticipates part of selective permanence.

The slow/fast model assigns different systemic roles to different rates:

- fast layers generate novelty, response and experimentation;
- slow layers provide continuity, memory, constraint and stability;
- healthy systems require both;
- fast-layer change can accumulate until slower layers eventually respond.

Architecturally, this permits a building to change significantly in faster layers while slower layers preserve continuity.

That is close to Ovid's proposition that:

> change can be concentrated where it is useful so architecture can afford to remain permanent elsewhere.

### Relationship

`EARLIER_ANTECEDENT` to one mechanism inside C04.  
Confidence: `DOCUMENTED`.  
Importance: provisional `SUPPORTING`.

### Why only SUPPORTING

Brand is not the strongest architectural source for selective permanence.

F02 already contains closer sources around:

- support / infill;
- committed form;
- polyvalence;
- selective versus universal flexibility;
- long-life capacity.

Brand contributes a useful temporal-systems explanation of change + continuity, not the full C04 architectural position.

Brand's later pace-layer work is useful but must remain chronologically disciplined.

1. Duffy supplied four architectural layers in the early 1970s;
2. Brand expanded them to six in *How Buildings Learn* (1994);
3. Brand and Brian Eno produced the broader healthy-civilisation pace-layer diagram in 1996;
4. Brand developed that broader formulation in *The Clock of the Long Now* (1999).

- Duffy-derived layers;
- O'Neill-derived slow/fast hierarchy;
- bidirectional influence;
- architectural slippage;
- destructive embedding.

The later pace-layer work is an authorial clarification and generalisation.

# 22. Transfer and scope limits

## 22.1 Ecology is explanatory, not architectural authority

O'Neill's process-rate hierarchy explains a general systems relationship.

It does not tell an architect:

- where to route a pipe;
- how to detail a replaceable window;
- how much access area is justified;
- what fire / acoustic boundary must be reinstated;
- whether one domestic service zone is proportionate.

Brand performs the architectural transfer.

Ovid must not cite ecology as if ecological hierarchy itself proves a building detail.

## 22.2 Brand's time bands are heuristics

The six-S durations are broad empirical / rhetorical bands.

They are not deterministic schedules.

F01's empirical counterpoints remain in force: actual changes are driven by use, regulation, technology, economics, ownership and failure as well as nominal lifespan.

## 22.3 Slippage is not maximum demountability

Brand's argument establishes a need for enough freedom between rates.

It does not imply that every component must be removable, exposed or mechanically disconnected.

Ovid's proportionality remains necessary.

## 22.4 Building hierarchy is not absolute domination

Brand explicitly allows influence in both directions.

Fast layers can eventually alter slow layers.

Therefore the six-S diagram should not be read as a rigid one-way command hierarchy.
