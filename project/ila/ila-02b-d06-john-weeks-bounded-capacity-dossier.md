# 1. Executive decision

John Weeks is a **major earlier architectural antecedent** to the middle position that Ovid currently occupies between tight-fit permanence and universal flexibility.

The important result is not simply that Weeks advocated `flexibility`.

Primary and near-primary material shows a much more precise position.

By 1960 Weeks was already arguing that hospital planning should:

- recognise that different parts change at different rates;
- distinguish relatively static from rapidly changing functions;
- avoid embedding one changing department inside another where this prevents either from altering;
- accept some looseness where growth is probable;
- refuse to treat flexibility as the only objective, because compactness, convenience and operating efficiency remain competing values;
- study a bounded range of plausible uses rather than demand infinite adjustability.

His later `Indeterminate Architecture` work develops the same logic at site / system scale:

- a stable communication and service geography establishes order;
- subordinate parts may grow, alter, be replaced or even disappear without invalidating the whole;
- the extent of indeterminacy is bounded by the capacity and geometry of the communications / service network and by the site;
- future growth can be directional without being fully predetermined in form.

This is materially close to the Ovid position:

> **stable geography + bounded future capacity + selective change rather than universal transformability.**

D06 therefore removes several possible novelty claims.

Ovid must not imply that it originated:

- the distinction between stable infrastructure geography and changeable subordinate parts;
- the idea that future capacity should be bounded rather than unlimited;
- the idea that some building parts deserve more adaptability than others because their change rates differ;
- the idea that independently replaceable / expandable parts can preserve the larger whole;
- the proposition that buying flexibility imposes present penalties and must be balanced against other architectural / operational objectives.

Ovid's remaining C03/C04 interest is narrower and more operational:

1. its explicit proportionality test — **frequency × consequence × retrofit difficulty**;
2. the project governance built from that test — burden classes, incumbent conventional construction, `hold`, co-renewal and rising burden of proof;
3. the domestic transfer — using bounded adaptability while deliberately preserving rooms, axes, stairs, facade order and other architectural commitments;
4. integration with maintenance geography, destructive-coupling control, failure architecture, workmanship robustness and typed evidence / pattern / semantic / compiler authority.

Those are **residual project-method hypotheses**, not originality verdicts. ILA-03/04 must still test them against the remaining dossiers.

Weeks also functions as a useful **counterpoint**.

His indeterminate hospital is intentionally more open-ended than Ovid's house. It seeks an organisation capable of indefinite institutional change and refuses aesthetic completion as a governing constraint. Ovid does not want a self-designing domestic megastructure. It wants a house whose defining architectural order can remain settled while carefully selected systems and uses change around it.

The relationship is therefore:

- `EARLIER_ANTECEDENT` to C03 bounded capacity;
- `EARLIER_ANTECEDENT` to C04 selective allocation of change;
- `EARLIER_ANTECEDENT` to the stable-geography portion of C09;
- bounded `COUNTERPOINT` to Ovid's deliberate architectural permanence.

No formative Weeks → Ovid edge is evidenced. Weeks was first located during ILA-F02 and remains `NO_DOCUMENTED_PROJECT_CONTACT` for project formation.

# 4. 1960: growth and change are already differentiated, selective and spatial

The 1960 material is stronger than a generic `loose fit` citation suggests.

## 4.1 Buildings should not be treated as completed static wholes

The contemporary BMJ account contrasts the closed finished building with an organic / town-planning model capable of continued growth.

The architectural implication is not that every wall must move.

It is that the site plan and relationships between parts must allow local change without disrupting the larger organisation.

This is already close to Ovid's general proposition that future intervention belongs in the plan and section rather than being treated as a later technical nuisance.

## 4.2 Growth requires a stable ordering geography

Weeks's approach uses town-planning concepts:

- traffic / circulation routes;
- zoned areas for development;
- supply / service relationships;
- deliberate placement of departments according to likely future growth.

The future state remains partly unknown, but the **geography that permits change is designed now**.

That is the important C09 overlap.

Ovid's service-topology sequence is far more explicit and domestic in its execution, but the broad proposition:

> stable routes / infrastructure can create order while local uses change

is inherited territory.

## 4.3 Avoid embedding independently changing parts

The 1960 account explicitly warns that embedding one department inside another makes expansion or alteration of both difficult.

This is structurally similar to the dependency logic already encountered in D04 Brand and D05 SCAG / CHS:

`one changing subsystem physically constrains another → future alteration becomes costly / disruptive`.

Weeks is not the earliest source for destructive lifecycle coupling, and D06 does not promote him into that F01 genealogy.

His importance here is the **planning-scale consequence**:

> spatial adjacency and topology should preserve independent growth where future change is credible.

## 4.4 Flexibility is not absolute

The same 1960 discussion makes an important concession.

Loose, low, expandable planning can conflict with:

- convenience;
- compactness;
- operational efficiency;
- transport distances and staffing burden.

Weeks therefore does not simply maximise flexibility.

He asks for a **wise balance** and distinguishes parts according to expected growth / change.

This is directly relevant to C03 because it destroys a simplistic genealogy in which proportionality appears only as Ovid's later reaction against earlier flexibility theory.

The cost / burden trade-off is already inside Weeks's own argument.

## 4.5 Different functions change at different rates

The 1960 material explicitly distinguishes relatively stable ward accommodation from faster-changing diagnostic / technical departments.

The exact service-life categories differ from Duffy / Brand, but the reasoning is familiar:

- some functions are expected to remain serviceable for decades;
- others are exposed to rapid technological change;
- the architectural allocation of looseness should respond to those different expectations.

This is a direct antecedent to **selective** adaptability rather than universal adaptability.

# 5. The `duffle-coat` proposition is bounded generality, not infinite flexibility

Weeks's early loose-fit analogy is often compressed into `make rooms flexible`.

F02 discovery correctly identified a narrower proposition.

The design task is to study a known family of likely requirements and create a small number of useful capacities that can accept several of them.

The analogy is important because it sits between two rejected extremes:

```text
bespoke exact fit for one present use
                ↓
      bounded general-purpose fit
                ↓
infinite / universal adjustability
```

Weeks occupies the middle.

That is unusually close to Ovid C03.

A room, route or system does not need to accommodate **every conceivable** future state. It needs enough capacity to handle a credible range without forcing the whole building into permanent genericity.

### D06 judgement

`Weeks → bounded loose fit / known-range capacity = EARLIER_ANTECEDENT`  
Confidence: `HIGH`.

### Limit

Weeks does not provide Ovid's present decision machinery:

- no B0–B3 burden classes;
- no explicit frequency × consequence × retrofit-difficulty rule;
- no `hold` disposition;
- no project-wide co-renewal rule.

The intellectual middle ground is prior art; Ovid's formal governance of that middle ground remains a live residual.

# 7. Indeterminacy is organised, not formless

Weeks's mature model is not `anything can happen anywhere`.

Its structure is hierarchical.

## 7.1 Stable macro-order

The entrance, principal routes, `street`, communications and service geography provide a legible order.

This macro structure is comparatively stable because later parts depend on it.

## 7.2 Variable subordinate parts

Departments / pavilions can:

- expand;
- alter internally;
- be rebuilt;
- be replaced;
- change use;

without requiring the whole hospital to be redesigned.

## 7.3 Future form is underdetermined, not route direction

Later summaries of Weeks's formulation make an important distinction:

- expansion can be defined in **direction** without prescribing its final form;
- communication routes can remain more fully defined.

This is therefore a genuine architecture of **different certainty levels**.

The design commits strongly where later change depends on stable order and withholds commitment where prediction is weak.

That is extremely close to Ovid's selective-permanence logic at an abstract level.

# 8. Source record D06-S03 — `Indeterminate Hospital Design on Urban Sites`, 1966

## Bibliographic identity

**John Weeks**, `Indeterminate Hospital Design on Urban Sites`, *Hospital Management Planning & Equipment*, vol. 29, no. 360, June 1966, pp. 338–41.

Bibliographic confirmation:

- Journal of Architecture historical scholarship: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13602365.2019.1698640

### Relevant proposition

The 1966 formulation is important because it makes the **boundary of indeterminacy** explicit.

Later scholarly reproductions / close paraphrases record the proposition that continuing growth and change are possible only within limits established by:

- the capacity and geometry of the communications / service network;
- the total site.

The same record identifies four associated principles:

1. major communications / service networks should be physically separable from the buildings they serve;
2. parts should be able to expand or be replaced independently without damaging the workability of the whole;
3. parts should be capable of change of use during design and later life;
4. future expansion may be directional without its final form being fixed in advance.

### Source quality boundary

D06 did not recover a reliable full-page primary transcription of the 1966 article.

The bibliographic identity is secure; the proposition is strongly and consistently reproduced in later technical / architectural scholarship. It is therefore treated as:

- `TIER_A_PRIMARY_SOURCE_IDENTITY`;
- `TIER_B_STRONG_SECONDARY_PROPOSITION_RECOVERY` for exact wording not independently checked against the original page image.

No quotation marks are assigned to wording that was not directly checked in the primary page.

# 9. Weeks explicitly bounds adaptability by infrastructure capacity

This is D06's most important finding for C03.

The logic is:

```text
future change remains uncertain
        ↓
create stable communication / service geography
        ↓
permit local growth / replacement around it
        ↓
but only within the capacity of that geography and site
```

This is not universal flexibility.

The infrastructure itself creates a **finite option envelope**.

That materially anticipates Ovid's desire to provide future capacity without paying permanently for unlimited futures.

### What Weeks owns

Weeks already owns the broad principle that:

> useful future freedom can be deliberately bounded by the capacity of stable infrastructure.

### What Weeks does not yet supply

He does not supply Ovid's exact project-wide proportionality algorithm.

Weeks's bound is primarily:

- site capacity;
- communication / service-network capacity;
- known functional ranges;
- operational trade-offs.

Ovid's C03 asks a different but related governance question:

> for each proposed permanent provision, how often is the future event likely, what happens if provision is absent, and how hard is retrofit later?

The difference is real, but it is a **method refinement**, not a new discovery that adaptability should be bounded.

# 12. The built evidence is mixed — and that matters

The retrospective evidence does not justify a triumphalist account.

## 12.1 Some site-scale adaptation did occur

Later Northwick Park change includes piecemeal upgrading and replacement of individual parts. Fawcett argues that this can plausibly be read as consistent with the site's indeterminate organisation.

The stable larger geography therefore had some real enabling value.

## 12.2 Some anticipated physical options were never used

Documented `open ends` / planned extension opportunities were not necessarily exercised.

This is a direct warning against confusing **designed option** with **realised future value**.

## 12.3 Loose fit cannot solve indefinite future change

Fawcett's retrospective is explicit that a building cannot reasonably be expected to satisfy every requirement indefinitely.

A structural grid or ward size cannot be judged a historic error merely because a larger dimension would have been useful fifty years later.

This is powerful negative evidence for C03.

It says, in effect:

> designing for change has a rational horizon; future-proofing cannot mean buying every conceivable future state.

## 12.4 Overall verdict remains ambiguous

Later healthcare-design literature explicitly describes the Northwick Park verdict as **ambiguous**, not a clean success or failure.

This prevents ILA from using the precedent rhetorically in either direction.

### D06 consequence

Northwick Park supports the *need* for proportionality but does not prove Ovid's exact proportionality rules.

It is evidence that:

- options can expire unused;
- change can exceed original loose-fit assumptions;
- enabling infrastructure has finite capacity;
- adaptability must compete with first cost, efficiency and spatial burden.

That is consistent with Ovid C03 and also predates it.

# 14. C04 — selective permanence after Weeks

Weeks also materially narrows C04.

He already demonstrates that architecture can allocate different levels of determinacy:

- stable communications / service order;
- comparatively stable functions where change is slow;
- deliberately loose / open zones where change is rapid;
- indeterminate final growth where prediction is weak.

That is selective permanence in all but Ovid's terminology.

### But there is an important difference

Weeks's strongest model is intentionally anti-final.

The hospital is allowed to remain architecturally unfinished / evolving. Later descriptions even call it `self-designing`.

Ovid's domestic proposition is more architecturally conservative:

- rooms may remain rooms;
- axes may remain axes;
- stairs may remain stairs;
- facade / courtyard order may remain composed;
- technical and use changes should occur without forcing those defining relationships to dissolve.

Weeks therefore does **not** collapse C04 completely.

Instead, he clarifies what may actually remain Ovid-specific:

> not the allocation of stable and changeable domains, but the deliberate claim that **architectural commitment itself is a valuable stable domain** rather than merely an infrastructure left open for future functional mutation.

D07 Van Eyck / Hertzberger is the dossier most likely to pressure that residual.

# 18. Transfer limits — hospital to house

Weeks's relevance is strong but bounded.

## 18.1 Scale

A hospital campus can behave like a small town.

A detached house cannot justify a campus-scale reserve of circulation, duplicate capacity or future expansion merely because the abstract topology is similar.

## 18.2 Change regime

Hospitals face:

- technological churn;
- changing medical departments;
- institutional expansion / contraction;
- public procurement cycles;
- large service infrastructures.

The ordinary house has a different probability distribution of future events.

This makes Ovid's proportionality filter more, not less, important when transferring Weeks.

## 18.3 Agency

Institutional clients and estate teams can manage a long-running indeterminate plan.

A house needs future legibility for owners, builders and maintainers who may have no institutional memory.

This is where Ovid's stewardship / documentation / physical-index concerns may add a genuinely different layer.

## 18.4 Architectural completion

Weeks is willing to subordinate aesthetic finality to future functional change.

Ovid explicitly is not.

The house is intended to feel architecturally resolved even while its technical layers remain serviceable and replaceable.

That difference is central and should survive into ILA-03.

# 19. Negative evidence / anti-heroic reading

D06 deliberately resists turning Weeks into a new canonical hero.

The dossier records four constraints.

### 19.1 The concept has limits

Even Weeks's own model is bounded by site and infrastructure capacity.

### 19.2 Flexibility creates present penalties

Loose planning can conflict with compactness, convenience and efficiency.

### 19.3 Predicted futures do not arrive cleanly

Northwick Park's subsequent history did not simply exercise the options imagined in the 1960s.

### 19.4 A fifty-year future can exceed rational design foresight

Later retrofit needs do not prove that a larger original grid or permanently more generous room would have been rational at inception.

These points are not failures of the lineage argument.

They are precisely why Weeks is so relevant to C03: he helps show that adaptability and restraint belong to the same intellectual problem.

# 23. Exact Ovid delta after D06

- buildings / functions change at different rates;
- different-rate systems benefit from independence;
- destructive embedding makes future change costly;
