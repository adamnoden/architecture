# The proposition

A house should seek **permanence without stasis**. It is completed once and then altered for decades. Services are renewed, fittings replaced, rooms repurposed, windows fail, pipes leak and new technologies arrive. The structure and major fabric may remain sound throughout, yet ordinary maintenance can repeatedly damage them because shorter-lived work was never given its own place to change.

Ovid’s House starts from that later life rather than stopping at practical completion.

The permanent architecture should remain stable because change has been given deliberate places to occur.

This is **permanence through transformation**. It is not an argument for making the whole house demountable. It is an argument for deciding, deliberately, what should endure; what should be expected to change; and how the two should meet.

## The problem is coupling

Buildings change at different speeds. Structure, masonry, windows, services, controls, finishes and appliances do not share one useful life. Different lifespans become destructive only when replacement of a faster layer consumes a slower one.

A cable chased into sound masonry is a simple example. So is a valve hidden behind bonded finish, a window whose removal destroys otherwise serviceable internal work, or plant that can be reached for inspection but cannot be withdrawn for replacement.

A component's lifespan matters less than the lifespan of the things that must be destroyed to replace it.

The aim is therefore not maximum durability of every component. It is **independence between unlike lifespans where that independence is worth providing**.

## Selective permanence

Change is not automatically good. A stair, a well-proportioned room, a structural order, a principal axis or a composed facade may deserve to remain stable for generations. A house should not become a neutral technical chassis merely because future change is uncertain.

Ovid’s House calls this **selective permanence**: make defining architectural relationships deliberately enduring, while concentrating adaptability in the systems and layers for which change is probable or consequential.

The same test limits over-design. Spare capacity, additional voids, access zones and reversible assemblies all have immediate spatial, material, cost and carbon burdens. They are strongest where the future event is frequent, damaging, difficult to retrofit or cheap to accommodate in advance. This is **proportional serviceability**.

## The architectural platform

The project treats the house as an architectural platform with four principal parts:

- **Permanent fabric** — foundations, principal structure, major masonry and other long-lived construction intended to remain substantially undisturbed.
- **Replaceable systems** — services, plant, controls, fit-out and other shorter-lived layers expected to wear, fail, become obsolete or be altered.
- **Designed interfaces** — the junctions that manage support, restraint, movement, tolerance, sealing, drainage, access, disassembly and renewal where those functions apply.
- **Maintenance geography** — the routes, working positions, clearances, supporting ground and withdrawal paths that make maintenance and replacement physically possible.

Two disciplines cut across all four.

**Workmanship robustness** asks the design to absorb ordinary construction variation through clear datums, tolerances, adjustment and verifiable assembly rather than exporting unresolved geometry to site improvisation.

**Repose** asks that all this technical reasoning disappear into a house that remains calm to occupy: physically comfortable, spatially comprehensible, capable of privacy and retreat, locally controllable and perceptually settled.

A useful assembly hierarchy is:

```text
permanent fabric
    ↓
interface infrastructure
    ↓
replaceable architectural assembly
    ↓
commodity component
```

Not every condition needs all four layers. The point is to decide where replacement should stop.

## What the proposition does not require

Ovid’s House does **not** require services to be exposed, every floor to lift, every wall to move or every component to be individually replaceable. Accessible does not mean visible. Concealment is legitimate when the concealing architecture remains coherent and the underlying relationship is understood.

Nor is passive design treated as an ideology. Form, envelope, orientation, shading, mass, openings, drainage and gravity should do the first useful work; mechanical and electronic systems remain appropriate when health, performance or resilience require them.

The objective is more ordinary than a technological house and more demanding than a conventional one: a building whose architecture can remain confidently permanent because foreseeable change, failure and maintenance have been designed into it from the beginning.
