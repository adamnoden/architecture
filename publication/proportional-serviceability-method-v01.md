# Proportional Serviceability Method v0.1

**Status:** controlled R8-A correction method  
**Date:** 2026-10-14  
**Owner:** doctrine / evidence / delivery  
**Origin:** PR-01 R7-C01 — Comparative proportionality and evidence  
**Purpose:** decide when present-day provision for future maintenance / replacement is architecturally justified, when conventional integrated construction should win, and when evidence is insufficient.

This method exists because “design for future change” is not a decision rule. Every service route, access zone, removable layer, spare capacity or replacement path has a present cost in some combination of material, carbon, floor area, section depth, boundary complexity, workmanship and governance. A future event may never occur.

The method therefore has one governing presumption:

> **The burden of proof rises with the permanence and scale of the provision being bought today.**

A cheap passive sleeve may be justified by a plausible future event. A deeper whole-storey floor, spare structural capacity or house-wide demountable lining is not.

This is an architectural screening method. It is **not** a substitute for a project life-cycle cost assessment, whole-life-carbon assessment, specialist maintenance plan or product-specific service-life study. Its purpose is to decide which ideas deserve that further work and which should be rejected before expensive analysis gives weak ideas false precision.

---

## 1. Comparator rule

Every non-standard serviceability proposal must be compared with an **excellent conventional baseline**, not with negligent or badly coordinated construction.

The baseline must satisfy the same immediate requirements for:

- life safety;
- legal / regulatory performance;
- structural adequacy;
- environmental performance;
- acoustic / fire / moisture / security boundaries;
- present-day architectural quality;
- ordinary maintainability required by professional practice.

Examples of legitimate conventional baselines include:

- conventional service distribution in ordinary floor/ceiling zones with local wall chases;
- accessible plant with conventional fixed downstream pipe/duct routes;
- mechanically fixed windows with normal seal/reveal renewal at replacement;
- conventional wet-room strip-out at major refurbishment while maintaining access to routine service items;
- ordinary local access panels where a specific component genuinely requires maintenance.

A proposal earns Ovid’s House treatment only through **additional whole-life value beyond that baseline**.

---

## 2. Reference period and service-life evidence

For comparable screening, use a declared analysis period. Where no project-specific period has been set, use the **60-year UK building reference study period used by the RICS Whole Life Carbon Assessment professional standard** as the comparison window.

This is not a claim that an Ovid’s House building has a 60-year life. The RICS standard itself distinguishes the reference study period from the actual life expectancy of the asset. The 60-year period is used only to make options comparable.

Component/service-life assumptions must be sourced and labelled as scenarios, not treated as certainties.

Useful current reference sources include:

- RICS, *Whole life carbon assessment for the built environment*, 2nd edition (2023; current version) — indicative component lifespans and 60-year UK building reference study period;
- CIBSE Guide M11 and the 2026 Economic Life Expectancy Tables — building-services economic life / predicted-service-life guidance;
- product/manufacturer data and project FM evidence where specific selections exist.

Indicative RICS values are sufficient for **screening only**. Current published indicative values include approximately:

- heat source / heat pump: 15–20 years depending type;
- space heating / air treatment: 20 years;
- galvanised ductwork: 40 years;
- electrical installations: 30 years;
- communications / controls: 15 years;
- water / disposal installations: 25 years;
- sanitaryware: 20 years;
- windows / external doors: 30 years.

Project analysis must replace these with more specific scenarios when better information exists.

# 3. Future-event classes

Do not begin with the proposed solution. Begin with the event it claims to serve.

| Class | Meaning | Typical evidence burden |
|---|---|---|
| **E0 — speculative** | No credible project-specific reason to expect the event within the analysis period. | No permanent provision by default. |
| **E1 — plausible** | Event could occur, but occurrence/timing is uncertain and not supported strongly enough to assume one cycle. | Only negligible/local passive provision unless consequence is unusually high. |
| **E2 — expected** | Component life, ordinary maintenance/refurbishment cycles or project evidence makes at least one occurrence within the analysis period a reasonable scenario. | Local provision normally worth testing; spatial provision requires comparison. |
| **E3 — recurrent / high-consequence** | Multiple interventions are expected, or one credible failure/change has sufficiently severe safety/damage/operational consequence. | Strong candidate for deliberate access/locality, subject to boundary and present-burden tests. |

A low-frequency event may still rank E3 where consequence is high enough. Conversely, a frequently changed decorative item does not automatically justify infrastructure if its conventional renewal consumes only other short-lived layers.

---

# 4. Present-provision burden classes

The same future event can justify one scale of provision and fail another.

| Burden | Meaning | Examples | Default posture |
|---|---|---|---|
| **B0 — coincident / negligible** | Uses geometry, information or access already required; trivial material/area penalty. | labelling; documenting isolation; sleeve in an already-required crossing; spare draw cord in an existing conduit; accessible fastener already hidden by removable trim | **favour** where useful |
| **B1 — local** | Small additional component/depth/interface; no material increase in whole-room/building area. | removable architrave; local access panel; small sacrificial backplane; local overflow/tell-tale; slightly larger service cupboard opening | **adopt for E2/E3 when boundaries remain simple** |
| **B2 — spatial / assembly** | Consumes meaningful plan/section volume or creates a new repeated interface family / boundary burden. | dedicated riser; enlarged plant room; service wall depth; distributed accessible room route; additional containment layer | **requires explicit excellent-baseline comparison and aggregate benefit** |
| **B3 — systemic / structural** | Alters whole-building structure, floor system, major area or many interfaces primarily for future optionality. | house-wide access floor; house-wide removable lining; interstitial service storey; spare structure for unknown extra storey; whole-house duplicate containment | **presumption against; quantitative project evidence required** |

The classes describe **burden**, not virtue. A B2 riser can be entirely rational where it carries many E2/E3 systems. A B0 provision can still be rejected if it creates a safety/boundary defect.

# 5. The eight tests

A proposal passes screening only if the relevant tests are answered explicitly.

## T1 — Functional equivalence

Are the Ovid and conventional options being compared at equivalent present-day performance and architectural quality?

Do not allow the Ovid option to win because the conventional option was intentionally made inaccessible, ugly, unsafe or underperforming.

## T2 — Event credibility

What is the event class, and what evidence supports it?

If the event is E0, stop unless the provision is genuinely B0 and carries no material penalty.

## T3 — Collateral-destruction delta

What **slower or still-serviceable layers** does the conventional intervention consume that the proposed provision avoids?

Do not count as “avoided destruction” layers that would ordinarily be renewed at the same intervention anyway.

This is the **co-renewal rule**.

Example: preserving bathroom tiles during a 25-year full bathroom renewal may have little value if the finishes/waterproofing are themselves due for renewal. Preserving sound masonry around a 30-year window replacement may have much greater value.

## T4 — Present burden

Classify the proposal B0–B3 and record:

- additional material;
- area / section depth;
- embodied-carbon implication where material;
- capital / design / coordination burden;
- new maintenance burden created by the provision itself.

## T5 — Boundary debt

What additional fire, acoustic, air, vapour, water, thermal, pest or security interfaces are created or repeatedly opened?

A reversible detail does not receive credit merely for being reversible.

## T6 — Governance / workmanship debt

Does the provision require unusual records, specialist supervision, proprietary knowledge, repeated re-certification or precise reinstatement that the conventional option avoids?

This is a present and future burden.

## T7 — Future-specificity risk

Is the provision robust to multiple plausible future implementations, or is it an expensive bet on one predicted technology / dimension / route?

Generic geometry and standard interfaces score better than technology-specific reserve infrastructure.

## T8 — Architectural value / opportunity cost

What present-day architecture is displaced or weakened?

Record lost room area, daylight, ceiling height, structural simplicity, solidity, repose, material character or other competing goods. Do not convert every architectural judgement into money.

# 6. Decision rules

The method intentionally avoids a single pseudo-scientific weighted score.

Use the following fail-closed rules.

## B0

May be **ADOPTED** for E1–E3 if T1 and safety/boundary tests pass.

B0 should still be rejected if it creates future lock-in or a new critical failure mode.

## B1

May be **ADOPTED** for E2/E3 where the collateral-destruction delta is clear and the new boundary/governance burden is minor.

For E1, require either high consequence or clear multi-use value.

## B2

May be adopted only when at least one is true:

- several E2/E3 systems aggregate into the same spatial provision;
- the avoided conventional intervention is materially destructive to slower/valuable fabric;
- a high-consequence failure needs the space for safe isolation/repair;
- the provision has strong present-day architectural/functional value in addition to serviceability.

B2 requires a written comparator and project-specific area/material/cost/carbon assessment at the precision available.

If that evidence is absent, status is **HOLD**, not “Ovid principle therefore adopt”.

## B3

Default outcome is **REJECT** or **HOLD**.

B3 may advance only with project-specific quantitative evidence and sensitivity testing demonstrating that the whole-life benefit remains defensible under plausible low-use scenarios.

The fact that the infrastructure would make future work easy is not sufficient evidence.

---

# 7. Symmetrical permanence test

Selective permanence must carry the same burden of proof.

For any element declared deliberately enduring—major wall, stair, facade order, room relationship, structural grid—record:

1. what present and long-term value earns permanence;
2. what future changes that permanence constrains;
3. whether those constraints are acceptable under credible life-course / use scenarios;
4. whether an equally good architecture could preserve the value with less lock-in.

“Architectural intention” is evidence of authorship, not evidence that permanence is proportionate.

---

# 8. Screening outcomes

Every reviewed provision receives one of five outcomes:

- **BASELINE** — excellent conventional construction is the preferred answer;
- **ADOPT** — additional serviceability provision is proportionate at current evidence;
- **HOLD** — plausible value, but evidence does not yet justify locking the provision into architecture;
- **PROTOTYPE / CALCULATE** — the decision depends on physical, engineering, cost or carbon evidence that can be obtained before lock-in;
- **REJECT** — present burden / specificity / boundary debt is disproportionate to the credible future event.

A HOLD is not silent approval. The building proceeds with the best current baseline unless the gate is later passed.

# 9. Representative domestic intervention comparisons

These comparisons are deliberately bounded. They test the method against ordinary future work; they are not cost plans.

## Case A — add or renew an electrical/data route to a principal room

**External lifecycle pressure:** RICS indicative electrical installation life ~30 years; communications/controls ~15 years.  
**Event:** E2.  

### Excellent conventional baseline

Use normal accessible ceiling/floor distribution where available, with local wall chase / making-good to final outlet positions. Document routes. Avoid structural drilling.

### Ovid candidate

Use an already-required skirting/joinery/service zone with draw/access provision.

### Comparison

- collateral destruction under good conventional construction is local, often plaster/decoration rather than primary structure;
- an accessible route becomes attractive if it coincides with skirting/joinery already required architecturally;
- creating a deep whole-floor void solely for hypothetical future cables would be grossly disproportionate.

**Outcome:**

- existing skirting/service route: **ADOPT — B0/B1**;
- dedicated local room route where multiple service classes aggregate: **project-specific B1/B2 test**;
- whole-house raised access floor for future cabling: **REJECT — B3** absent extraordinary brief.

This case demonstrates that “future cable change” does **not** justify the largest available access solution.

---

## Case B — service / replace a concealed wet-room valve or connection

**External lifecycle pressure:** RICS indicative water/disposal installation life ~25 years; sanitaryware ~20 years.  
**Event:** E2; some leak/failure conditions E3 by consequence.  

### Excellent conventional baseline

Provide access to components intended for service, use reliable conventional concealed pipework, and accept local opening/making-good for infrequent failures. At full refurbishment, strip finishes that are themselves at renewal.

### Ovid candidate

Where a high-service room already backs onto dry/service space, place routine service items on an accessible side or within a local service wall.

### Comparison

- dry-side access can avoid damaging waterproofing and wet finishes during a component-level repair;
- a full bathroom renewal does not justify preserving every short-lived finish;
- a dedicated secondary corridor or large inaccessible cavity built only to reach one valve is not earned.

**Outcome:**

- clustered dry-side service wall serving multiple components: **ADOPT / B2 only where adjacency already works architecturally and aggregate benefit is shown**;
- local access to known service item: **ADOPT — B1**;
- house-wide replaceable wall lining to protect all wet-room finishes: **REJECT/HOLD — B3**.

This case explicitly applies the **co-renewal rule**.

---

## Case C — replace a complete external window frame

**External lifecycle pressure:** RICS indicative windows/external doors ~30 years.  
**Event:** E2 over the 60-year comparison period.  

### Excellent conventional baseline

High-performance window mechanically fixed within a durable opening, with conventional perimeter sealing and local reveal/decoration renewal when the frame is replaced.

### Ovid candidate

Keep primary opening/fabric independent of the frame; provide reachable replacement fixings and a finishing/sealing arrangement that can be renewed without rebuilding sound masonry.

### Comparison

- masonry/opening life is materially slower than the frame/seal/hardware family;
- local trim/reveal renewal is already normal and should not be counted as failure;
- a complex permanent subframe is only justified if it improves replacement while meeting thermal/water/air/fire/acoustic requirements without adding disproportionate material/bridge risk.

**Outcome:**

- accessible mechanical replacement interface / removable trim: **ADOPT — B1**;
- proprietary or bespoke permanent subframe: **PROTOTYPE / CALCULATE** until product/building-physics evidence exists;
- doctrine does **not** require one universal window system.

---

## Case D — full bathroom renewal

**External lifecycle pressure:** sanitaryware ~20 years; actual finish/waterproofing/service lives vary and must be project-specific.  
**Event:** E2.  

### Excellent conventional baseline

At major renewal, remove sanitaryware and fit-out; renew waterproofing/boards/tiles where those layers are themselves exhausted or design change requires it. Preserve primary structure/masonry where practical.

### Ovid candidate

Make all room linings/floor finishes demountable so a future bathroom can be renewed with minimal wet/demolition work.

### Comparison

A fully replaceable lining system buys permanent joints, fixings, depth, acoustic/solidity questions and future reinstatement obligations. Much of the “avoided” work may be legitimate co-renewal.

**Outcome:** **BASELINE / REJECT full-room reversible lining as default.**

Retain only local access to components whose service interval/failure consequence materially differs from the room finish.

This confirms the project’s earlier decision not to make house-wide removable lining a baseline.

---

## Case E — replace primary heating / ventilation plant

**External lifecycle pressure:** indicative heat source ~15–20 years; air treatment ~20 years.  
**Event:** E3 over a 60-year comparison period.  

### Excellent conventional baseline

Accessible plant location with normal working clearances and a door/removal path sized for selected equipment / realistic replacement family.

### Ovid candidate

Plant/service hub with explicit approach, working and withdrawal geometry; replacement opening independent of unrelated finished rooms where proportionate.

### Comparison

A credible withdrawal path is strongly earned because multiple replacement cycles are likely. A generous dedicated plant room is a separate B2 spatial decision and cannot be justified simply by naming it a service hub.

**Outcome:**

- explicit plant withdrawal route: **ADOPT — B1/B2 depending geometry**;
- accessible isolation / identification: **ADOPT — B0/B1**;
- Reference House plant-room *size*: **HOLD project-specific proportionality until area/equipment/QS review confirms it**.

The method therefore accepts the maintenance task while refusing to pre-approve the room size.

---

## Case F — ventilation distribution / duct renewal

**External lifecycle pressure:** air-treatment equipment ~20 years; galvanised ductwork ~40 years as indicative RICS values.  
**Event:** fan/filter/controls E3; principal duct replacement E1/E2.  

### Excellent conventional baseline

Accessible fan/filter/control equipment; durable ductwork in coordinated conventional voids with local access to dampers/joints requiring service.

### Ovid candidate

Create fully accessible distributed duct routes or a larger interstitial zone so the complete network can be replaced without opening finishes.

### Comparison

The frequently serviced components strongly justify access. The long-lived static duct network does not automatically justify crawlable or house-wide removable construction.

**Outcome:**

- fan/filter/condensate/control access: **ADOPT — B1**;
- coherent coordinated duct route with selected local access: **BASELINE / ADOPT depending existing geometry**;
- large distributed access infrastructure solely for complete future duct replacement: **REJECT/HOLD — B2/B3**.

---

## Case G — house-wide access floor / removable floor platform

**Event:** multiple possible future services, but no single defined E2/E3 task demonstrates need across the whole floor.  
**Provision:** B3.

**Outcome:** **REJECT as default.**

Selective access may still be tested where a specific route/task earns it. The systemic provision fails because its permanent acoustic, solidity, depth, threshold and interface burden is certain while its future use is diffuse.

---

## Case H — structural reserve for an unknown future extra storey

**Event:** E0 absent a real brief/planning strategy.  
**Provision:** B3 structural/material burden.

**Outcome:** **REJECT.**

A long-lived house does not earn speculative structural carbon merely because future change is possible.

---

# 10. What these cases establish — and what they do not

The cases support a narrower proposition than the pre-adversarial project sometimes implied.

They establish that:

- serviceability is strongest where the event is expected/recurrent and the provision is B0/B1;
- aggregation can justify selected B2 service geography;
- co-renewal sharply reduces the value of some demountability;
- access to maintainable equipment is not evidence for access to every static route;
- B3 systemic infrastructure normally loses without project-specific evidence;
- an excellent conventional baseline is often the correct answer.

They do **not** establish exact future cost or carbon savings for the Reference House. Those remain project/specialist work. The method instead prevents missing numbers from becoming tacit approval: B2/B3 provisions remain HOLD/REJECT until the evidence exists.

# 12. Method gate

**R8-A method result: PASS for architectural screening.**

The method is sufficiently explicit to reject serviceability proposals and to prevent high-burden provisions from being justified by doctrine alone.

It deliberately leaves B2/B3 project decisions unresolved where cost/carbon/area evidence is absent. That is a feature, not a failure: the correct output of insufficient evidence is **HOLD or BASELINE**, not assumed future value.
